Before we outline all of history

How we will build the outline

A staged method for finding the whole story without letting chronology, famous doctors, surviving Western records, or search keywords quietly become the whole story.

This is the method, not the content outline.
Passes 1–10 have now produced the first actual outline for review.

The problem

A single brainstormed table of contents would reproduce whatever its author remembers best. For this subject, that usually means Europe, famous men, medical institutions, and the last two centuries.

We will outline in separate passes, then force those passes to confront one another. Gaps must become visible before article drafting begins.

Three structures, one history

01

The backbone

A chronological journey from prehistoric evidence to the present. It lets readers see change, continuity, collision, and rediscovery.

02

The lenses

Recurring subjects such as emotion, distress, the brain, healing, disability, institutions, professions, power, and coping.

03

The entry doors

Focused pages shaped around real questions, people, places, objects, events, controversies, and search language.

The twelve-pass method

1

Set the map's edges

What belongs, what is adjacent, and what would make this project infinite?

Turn the approved mission into a scope test, a terminology policy, and a list of dangerous shortcuts. Define how we will treat modern diagnoses applied to historical people, prehistoric interpretation, spirituality, wellness, addiction, disability, and ordinary coping.

Scope testTerm rulesExclusion logDrift alarms
Gate: Two researchers using the rules should make roughly the same inclusion decision.
2

Build the evidence model

What can each kind of evidence actually tell us?

Define source types, claim-level records, evidence labels, translation notes, archive gaps, disagreement handling, and the difference between a source produced by a patient and a source produced about one.

Source schemaClaim schemaConfidence labelsCitation rules
Gate: Every future claim has a place to record support, uncertainty, disagreement, and reuse.
3

Sketch the chronological backbone

What major periods and transitions help a public reader travel through the story?

Create a deliberately provisional sequence from prehistory to today. Use overlapping ranges where regions change at different times. Avoid treating European labels such as “medieval” as universal world periods.

Period mapTurning pointsContinuitiesDate disputes
Gate: Every period label states where it applies and where it does not.
4

Run the global coverage pass

Which regions, cultures, languages, and knowledge traditions disappear inside the first chronology?

Build a period-by-region matrix. Mark evidence-rich areas, evidence-poor areas, translation dependencies, contested boundaries, and regions needing specialist help. Empty cells are findings, not invitations to improvise.

Region matrixLanguage needsSpecialist needsKnown gaps
Gate: No period can be called “global” while large omissions remain hidden.
5

Run the thematic passes

What stories cross periods and borders but vanish in a chronology alone?

Trace recurring lenses separately: brain and body, mind and self, moods and emotions, distress and difference, healing and treatment, wellness and coping, caregiving, disability, institutions, professions, law, work, war, family, spirituality, and power.

Theme mapsCross-era linksCompeting ideasLong arcs
Gate: Each theme earns inclusion through the scope test and has evidence beyond a single famous figure.
6

Restore lived experience

What did systems feel like to the people inside them, and can we actually know?

Search for direct testimony, letters, oral histories, art, court records, patient publications, activism, family accounts, and material evidence. Where voices are missing, record who controlled the archive and what can only be inferred.

Voice inventoryArchive-power notesTestimony mapSilence markers
Gate: Institutional records are never silently rewritten as patient experience.
7

Map encounters, exchanges, and power

How did ideas travel, mix, get suppressed, or get renamed?

Trace trade, migration, translation, conquest, colonization, slavery, missionary activity, war, professional networks, and scientific exchange. Look for ideas later presented as isolated “discoveries” after moving between cultures.

Transmission routesTranslation chainsPower shiftsAttribution disputes
Gate: “Discovery” pages identify predecessors, exchanges, and people excluded from credit.
8

Turn the map into page families

Which ideas need a broad guide, and which deserve focused explanations?

Convert the research map into authority pages and supporting pages. Define page jobs for periods, regions, people, ideas, treatments, objects, institutions, professions, controversies, and “How we know” evidence explainers.

Page inventoryPage jobsParent-child linksDuplicate test
Gate: Every proposed page has a distinct reader question and a non-duplicative job.
9

Add reader language and search paths

How do real people ask for this history?

Research search language, related questions, terminology changes, common misconceptions, and conversational queries. Apply findings to titles, summaries, FAQs, definitions, and internal links without letting keyword demand rewrite the historical canon.

Query mapQuestion coverageTerminology bridgesLink plan
Gate: No thin page exists only because a keyword exists.
10

Stress-test the balance

What did our structure make too large, too small, too certain, or invisible?

Audit geographic balance, gender and power, professional dominance, patient voice, disability framing, colonial inheritance, present-day bias, evidence strength, and whether modern categories have been projected backward.

Bias auditCoverage auditConfidence auditRevision list
Gate: Every imbalance is repaired, justified, or visibly logged as an evidence gap.
11

Choose and build the pilot cluster

Can this system produce one page family that is accurate, engaging, humane, and useful?

Select a demanding early-history cluster. Research it fully, build the claim ledger, draft the pages, test the voice, plan visuals, and run every review gate. Use the pilot to expose missing fields and broken assumptions.

Pilot sourcesPilot draftsReview recordModel changes
Gate: The pilot passes editorial review before the project scales or the application schema changes.
12

Freeze version one of the master outline

Is the outline ready to govern years of research without pretending to be final forever?

Incorporate pilot lessons, assign stable identifiers, record dependencies, define research waves, and publish an approved outline snapshot. Later changes require dated decisions rather than silent reshuffling.

Master outline v1Research wavesDependency mapDecision log
Gate: Matthew explicitly approves the outline before bulk drafting begins.

The evidence vocabulary

DocumentedDirectly supported by appropriate primary evidence.
Strongly supportedMultiple strong sources or scholarly consensus.
PlausiblePermitted by evidence, but not established as fact.
DisputedCredible experts materially disagree.
UnknownThe evidence cannot responsibly answer.
