## 1. The assignment

Create a cited, global history of how human beings have:

- understood the mind and brain;
- experienced and described thinking, moods, emotions, distress, difference, and wellness;
- tried to heal, cope, care for one another, and live well;
- classified, treated, supported, controlled, and governed mental life; and
- developed psychology, psychiatry, neuroscience, social work, counseling, public health,
  disability movements, and related fields.

The public result is a major Couch Commons educational collection. It must earn durable
traffic and engagement through usefulness, historical care, strong sourcing, internal
connections, and language people actually search for. Search demand shapes discovery and
publication order. It does not decide what is historically true or important.

## 2. Who it is for

The first reader is the public, especially teens and young adults who are curious about
their minds but do not have specialist training. The work should remain useful and credible
to clinicians, educators, students, historians, and researchers.

## 3. Voice

Write with the energy of Jack Hanna meeting Mr. Wizard: curious, vivid, welcoming, and
delighted by discovery. Do not sound like a textbook, journal abstract, generic wellness
site, or AI-generated summary.

- Explain unfamiliar ideas without talking down to the reader.
- Use memorable present-day comparisons, clearly labeled as analogies.
- Let the emotional register change with the subject.
- Be playful around discovery, ideas, brains, emotion, and coping.
- Be calm and direct around suffering, coercion, prejudice, abuse, and death.
- Never joke at a patient's expense.
- Never invent a scene, motive, belief, diagnosis, feeling, quotation, or experience.

## 4. Historical and geographic scope

The project is global. Europe and the United States are regions in the story, not the
default story with the rest of the world added as sidebars.

Begin with the earliest responsibly interpretable evidence, including prehistory. Clearly
separate physical evidence from interpretations about what people may have believed.

The final inclusion boundary will be set while creating the master outline. The working
test is:

> Include a subject when it materially changed how people understood, experienced,
> described, classified, treated, supported, or governed mental life.

Potential first-class areas include medicine, spirituality, philosophy, neuroscience,
psychology, psychiatry, social work, disability, addiction, trauma, public health,
caregiving, law, institutions, war, work, education, family life, wellness, and everyday
coping. Inclusion is not automatic; the outline must show the connection.

## 5. Whose history counts

Professional and institutional history is only one evidence stream. Actively seek:

- patients' and survivors' words;
- disabled people's accounts and activism;
- family and caregiver experience;
- social workers, nurses, counselors, attendants, and community caregivers;
- Indigenous and local knowledge holders;
- religious and spiritual communities;
- court, hospital, asylum, school, military, and welfare records;
- letters, diaries, oral histories, testimony, art, objects, and archaeology; and
- evidence of ordinary coping and mutual aid outside formal systems.

When direct testimony is absent, say so. Explain whose records survive, whose do not, and
how power shaped the archive. Cautious reconstruction is allowed only when the text reveals
whether a conclusion is documented, strongly supported, plausible, disputed, or unknown.

## 6. Evidence standard

Prefer sources in this order, while matching the source to the claim:

1. Primary evidence: original documents, objects, datasets, testimony, contemporary
   accounts, archival records, and authoritative translations.
2. Peer-reviewed scholarship and scholarly books from relevant experts.
3. Museums, archives, libraries, governments, universities, and established professional
   or scientific organizations.
4. High-quality journalism for recent history, investigation, and public reception.
5. Weaker summaries only as leads to better evidence, not normally as final support.

Do not rely on SEO pages, unsourced summaries, copied citation chains, search snippets, or
AI-generated claims as evidence. Trace an important claim to the original source whenever
practical. Never cite a source that has not been opened and checked.

### Evidence labels

Use these labels in research and review records:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Documented** | Directly supported by appropriate primary evidence. |
| **Strongly supported** | Multiple strong sources or a strong scholarly consensus support it. |
| **Plausible** | Evidence permits the interpretation, but does not establish it. |
| **Disputed** | Credible experts offer materially different interpretations. |
| **Unknown** | Available evidence cannot responsibly answer the question. |

When credible sources disagree, explain the serious interpretations and their evidence.
Do not manufacture balance between strong evidence and misinformation, propaganda,
denialism, or a disproven claim.

## 7. Citation and link rules

- Place useful hyperlinks naturally in the body.
- Provide complete citations at the end of each public page.
- Track sources at claim level during research, not only in a bibliography.
- Preserve author, title, date, publisher, source type, DOI or stable URL, access date,
  exact supporting page or passage, archive link when appropriate, limitations, evidence
  label, and every page that uses the claim.
- Use Couch Commons' existing citation ladder: ordinary links by default, dated evidence
  badges when the check date matters, and no more than two pulled-out claim cards per article.
- Use absolute dates.
- Excerpt briefly and link to external work; never republish it.

## 8. Ethical stance

Use contextualized moral clarity. Explain what people believed, what systems rewarded, and
what evidence was available without hiding coercion, racism, sexism, colonialism, eugenics,
conversion practices, exploitation, diagnostic abuse, or preventable harm.

Discuss harmful history with empathy. Historical context should deepen judgment, not erase
it. Legal availability does not automatically make a patient image, human-remains image,
quotation, or case record ethical to display.

Explain medical, religious, philosophical, Indigenous, community, psychoanalytic, and
wellness systems in their own historical contexts. Separately describe how their empirical
claims compare with current evidence. Respect does not require false equivalence.

## 10. Information architecture direction

Use a hybrid system:

1. A global chronological backbone.
2. Interconnected collections for people, places, ideas, treatments, professions,
   institutions, emotions, coping, diagnoses, and lived experiences.
3. Broad authority pages supported by focused pages answering real reader questions.
4. Natural paths to the next era, related idea, competing theory, person, place, or evidence
   question.
5. Maps, timelines, diagrams, historical images, short primary-source excerpts, "How we
   know" panels, and small interactive experiences when they improve understanding.

Historical importance determines the canon. Search research improves page language,
question coverage, metadata, internal links, and publishing sequence.
