## The collection in one sentence

A global, source-led history of how people have understood, experienced, cared for, changed,
classified, and governed mental life—from the evidence left in ancient bodies and texts to
the arguments unfolding around rights, wellness, data, and AI today.

## The editorial promise

This is not a timeline of famous Western doctors. It is a connected public reference where a
reader can ask:

- What did people think was happening?
- What did they call it then?
- What was it like for the person receiving care or control?
- Why did an idea or treatment make sense in its setting?
- Who had the power to define, help, confine, pay, refuse, or reform?
- How did knowledge move—and change—as people translated, migrated, traded, conquered, and
  resisted?
- What do historians dispute?
- What does current evidence say, kept separate from historical explanation?

The collection ends with open questions, not a victory screen. WHO's current account still
describes a large gap between mental-health needs and systems' responses, while rights-based
guidance continues to press for community inclusion and less coercion
([WHO World Mental Health Report](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240049338/);
[WHO and OHCHR](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080737)).

## 8. Page anatomy

Every substantial guide uses this flexible sequence:

1. **Direct answer:** 40–80 words that answer the page's real question without flattening it.
2. **Why this story is trickier than it looks:** the misconception, category problem, or
   evidence limit.
3. **Map in time and place:** dates and regions that apply—and those that do not.
4. **What people called it then:** original terms, language, translation, and changing meaning.
5. **What people thought was happening:** multiple explanations in their settings.
6. **What happened to people:** care, relief, coping, status, rights, labor, coercion, and harm.
7. **A voice or an honest silence:** direct testimony when ethical and available; otherwise a
   specific account of the gap.
8. **How the idea or practice traveled:** named people, routes, institutions, media, and power.
9. **What historians debate:** serious interpretations and their evidence.
10. **Then versus now:** a visually distinct current-evidence section, never medical advice.
11. **How we know:** claim-level notes, objects, records, translations, and limitations.
12. **Where to go next:** one chronological, one thematic, one regional, and one evidence link.
13. **Complete citations:** full end references matching the useful links in the body.

Focused pages can be shorter, but they cannot omit the evidence and human-consequence jobs
that apply.

## 9. SEO/GEO discovery design

Search language changes the door, not the historical finding.

### One authority page, many honest entry questions

Each authority guide records:

- a primary reader question in plain language;
- close variants and vocabulary bridges;
- a 40–80-word citation-ready answer;
- entities with explicit relationships: person, concept, place, date, institution, source;
- a table or timeline only when it resolves a real comparison;
- definitions bounded by time and place;
- a misconception correction where evidence supports it;
- descriptive link text into the chronology, places, experience, and methods;
- a concise “what we still do not know” section; and
- visible author, historian/reviewer roles, last substantive review date, and corrections.

### Thin-page rule

Do not create a page for a spelling variant, city-keyword pair, isolated date, or snippet-sized
question. Merge it into a substantial guide unless it has a distinct historical job, evidence
base, and internal-link role.

### GEO answer integrity

Short answers must preserve the page's essential boundary. For example: “Premodern societies
used many spiritual explanations” cannot be shortened to “people blamed demons.” A reusable
answer block carries its source, scope, evidence label, and last verification date wherever it
appears.

The reader-question foundation is qualitative and English-language weighted. Search-volume,
Search Console, and competitor-gap validation belong to a later prioritization pass; they do
not alter this canon before evidence review.

## 10. Internal-link grammar

Every substantial page links to:

- its parent time band or authority guide;
- the preceding and following change in the same story;
- the place where the claim applies;
- a lived-experience or archive-silence stream;
- a competing explanation, credible dispute, or myth correction;
- the relevant evidence-method page; and
- one present-evidence bridge when the subject has current clinical relevance.

Links should state the relationship: “how Greek texts changed in Arabic translation,” not
“learn more”; “why shell shock is not simply old PTSD,” not “related article.”

## 12. What this outline deliberately does not do

- It does not claim that every listed page is already sourced or publishable.
- It does not produce hundreds of empty country or diagnosis templates.
- It does not assign modern diagnoses to historical people.
- It does not award “first psychiatric hospital,” “founder of psychology,” or similar titles
  without definition and evidence review.
- It does not treat spiritual, Indigenous, community, medical, or scientific knowledge as
  internally uniform.
- It does not confuse historical longevity or popularity with present efficacy.
- It does not use suffering as spectacle.
- It does not move into Couch Commons implementation, database work, or publication.

## 13. Research foundation and limitations

This outline was synthesized from six checked foundation notes covering prehistory and ancient
connected worlds; South, East, Southeast, and Central Asia; Africa, the Americas, the
Caribbean, and Oceania; late-antique through early-modern exchange; modern disciplines,
systems, and rights; and reader questions/search language. Those notes contain direct links,
evidence anchors, disputes, missing voices, and bibliographies.

The foundation is strong enough to map the collection. It is not a substitute for the
page-level source ledger required by `../sources/EVIDENCE-MODEL.md`. Before any page moves from
**Mapped** to **Sourced**, its researcher must open and log the exact evidence for each public
claim, seek lived-experience and local perspectives, verify translations and “firsts,” and
record what the sources cannot establish.
