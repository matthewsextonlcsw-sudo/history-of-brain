## What this document does

This scope map defines what the project will investigate. It is not the master content
outline, a list of article titles, or a claim that every subject below will receive equal
space. It prevents three failures:

1. “All of history” expanding into all of human history.
2. Mental-health history shrinking into a chronology of famous Western doctors.
3. Search demand turning adjacent topics into thin, repetitive pages.

This document makes editorial and organizational decisions, not historical or scientific
claims. External citations begin when the evidence model and research records begin. Every
future factual claim remains subject to the project's citation rules.

## The center of the map

The project studies **human mental life across time**: how people understood it, experienced
it, described it, cared for it, changed it, coped with it, and used power around it.

Every included subject must connect materially to at least one of these eight actions:

1. **Understand** — explanations of mind, brain, self, behavior, emotion, or consciousness.
2. **Experience** — first-person and collective accounts of mental life, distress, difference,
   coping, and wellness.
3. **Describe** — language, categories, metaphors, measurements, diagnoses, and narratives.
4. **Care** — family, community, spiritual, peer, professional, and institutional support.
5. **Treat** — remedies, therapies, medicines, procedures, environments, and prevention.
6. **Cope** — habits, rituals, relationships, creativity, work, rest, substances, and other
   ways people managed inner and social life.
7. **Classify** — distinctions between health and illness, normal and abnormal, capacity and
   incapacity, virtue and vice, danger and difference.
8. **Govern** — laws, confinement, rights, welfare, education, labor, military systems,
   professional authority, surveillance, resistance, and reform.

If a subject does not change or reveal one of these actions, it is outside the project.

## Four scope levels

### Level A — Core history

The subject directly changes or reveals how people understood, experienced, described,
cared for, treated, coped with, classified, or governed mental life. It belongs in the
historical backbone or a first-class thematic path.

### Level B — Necessary context

The subject is not mental-health history by itself, but readers cannot understand a core
development without it. Include only the portion that explains the connection. Examples may
include a war, migration, epidemic, political movement, religious change, technology, or
economic crisis with a demonstrated effect on mental life or systems of care.

### Level C — Cross-reference

The subject is relevant but belongs primarily elsewhere. Give it a concise explanation and
link to the page where its mental-life connection is developed. Do not duplicate an entire
general-history treatment.

### Level D — Outside

The subject has no material connection, lacks responsible evidence, duplicates another page,
or exists only to capture a keyword. Log tempting exclusions when future researchers are
likely to reintroduce them.

## The scope decision path

For every proposed topic, answer in order:

1. **Connection:** Which of the eight actions does it materially affect or reveal?
   - None: exclude.
2. **Historical job:** Does it show a change, continuity, conflict, exchange, lived experience,
   or evidence problem?
   - None: merge it into a stronger topic or exclude it.
3. **Evidence:** Is there a responsible path to primary evidence, strong scholarship, or both?
   - Not yet: place it in a research-gap backlog, not the publishable outline.
4. **Human stakes:** Can we explain who experienced the consequences, who held authority, and
   whose voice survives?
   - Not yet: flag the missing perspective before drafting.
5. **Geographic truth:** Are we using a regional development as if it were universal?
   - Yes: narrow the label or add the missing comparisons.
6. **Distinct page job:** Does the topic answer a question another planned page does not?
   - No: merge it and preserve redirects or alternate terms for discovery planning.
7. **Ethical publishability:** Can we present the material without inventing experience,
   exploiting a person, or stripping harm from its context?
   - No: retain it as a research record, not public content.

A topic is **mapped** only after all seven questions have an answer. “Interesting” is not an
admission standard.

## Core realms

The realms overlap on purpose. Each supplies a different route through the history; none is
the single master taxonomy.

### 1. Mind, brain, body, and self

Include histories of:

- brain structure and function;
- nervous systems, senses, embodiment, pain, sleep, dreams, and arousal;
- consciousness, attention, perception, memory, learning, language, imagination, reasoning,
  decision-making, identity, and concepts of self;
- mind–body relationships and competing explanations of where mental life resides;
- scientific instruments, tests, images, laboratories, and models used to study mental life;
- neurological and bodily explanations when they materially shaped mental-health thought or
  care.

Boundary: general anatomy, neurology, or biology belongs only when its connection to mental
life is explicit.

### 2. Emotion, mood, temperament, and personality

Include histories of:

- named emotions and culturally specific emotion concepts;
- mood, temperament, character, personality, motivation, desire, fear, grief, anger, joy,
  shame, attachment, loneliness, and belonging;
- theories of emotional expression, regulation, measurement, and development;
- changing judgments about acceptable, excessive, deficient, gendered, racialized, classed,
  or age-appropriate feeling.

Boundary: do not treat current English emotion categories as timeless or universal.

### 3. Distress, difference, diagnosis, and disability

Include histories of:

- experiences and behaviors societies interpreted as distressing, unusual, disabling,
  dangerous, meaningful, sinful, gifted, possessed, ill, injured, or socially disruptive;
- naming, classification, diagnosis, measurement, screening, and epidemiology;
- intellectual, developmental, psychosocial, cognitive, and psychiatric disability;
- neurodiversity, mad pride, survivor movements, disability rights, and competing models of
  impairment and difference;
- stigma, status, belonging, exclusion, and identity formation.

Boundary: do not retroactively diagnose historical people unless the evidence and scholarly
purpose justify discussing the debate. Prefer period language plus an explanation of its
limits.

### 4. Healing, treatment, care, and prevention

Include histories of:

- household, community, peer, religious, spiritual, Indigenous, medical, psychological,
  social, and institutional care;
- ritual, environment, conversation, rest, work, art, movement, diet, herbs, medicines,
  surgery, physical procedures, psychotherapy, behavior change, rehabilitation, and mutual aid;
- prevention, early intervention, recovery, harm reduction, palliative approaches, and
  community living;
- who gave care, who received it, who paid, who consented, and who was compelled;
- beneficial, ineffective, disputed, exploitative, and harmful practices.

Boundary: describe historical practices; do not turn historical pages into current treatment
instructions or individualized medical advice.

### 5. Coping, wellness, resilience, and everyday mental life

Include histories of:

- ordinary strategies people used to endure, regulate, recover, find meaning, and flourish;
- friendship, family, play, humor, storytelling, music, art, movement, nature, rest, ritual,
  spirituality, work, leisure, collective action, and mutual aid;
- self-help, hygiene movements, positive psychology, wellness industries, mindfulness,
  resilience, and changing ideals of a good or balanced life;
- adaptive and harmful coping, including avoidance, compulsions, risk-taking, and substance use;
- the commercialization and moralization of wellness.

Boundary: do not present “wellness” as a timeless, neutral, or automatically beneficial idea.

### 6. Relationships, family, community, and development

Include histories of:

- infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, aging, dying, and changing life-stage concepts;
- attachment, parenting, kinship, marriage, friendship, sexuality, caregiving, community,
  loneliness, and social support;
- schools, workplaces, neighborhoods, religious communities, peer groups, and online spaces
  when they shaped mental life;
- migration, displacement, diaspora, social isolation, and belonging;
- family blame, family advocacy, caregiver burden, and family-led reform.

Boundary: general family, sexuality, education, work, or community history belongs only where
the mental-life connection is demonstrated.

### 7. Trauma, violence, disaster, and survival

Include histories of:

- changing explanations of psychological injury, shock, grief, stress, dissociation, and
  survival after overwhelming events;
- war, enslavement, colonization, genocide, displacement, disaster, interpersonal violence,
  persecution, and occupational exposure when mental consequences are part of the history;
- military psychiatry, compensation, testimony, memory, intergenerational effects, and
  survivor organizing;
- debates over universality, culture, diagnosis, evidence, and the political uses of trauma.

Boundary: do not use suffering as spectacle or turn broad historical violence into content
unless the mental-health connection is central and responsibly sourced.

### 8. Substances, addiction, compulsion, and altered states

Include histories of:

- alcohol, psychoactive plants and drugs, medicines, intoxication, dependence, withdrawal,
  craving, recovery, and harm reduction;
- gambling and other behaviors historically framed as vice, habit, impulse, compulsion, or
  addiction;
- ritual, medical, social, criminal, and commercial uses of psychoactive substances;
- prohibition, criminalization, treatment systems, mutual-help movements, and public policy;
- altered states associated with ritual, spirituality, sleep, illness, substances, or
  deliberate practice.

Boundary: neither romanticize nor flatten culturally specific practices into modern clinical
categories.

### 9. Knowledge systems and explanations

Include histories of:

- philosophical, religious, spiritual, Indigenous, local, medical, scientific, legal, and
  popular explanations of mental life;
- theories of soul, spirit, mind, brain, body, morality, heredity, environment, development,
  learning, society, and culture;
- translation, exchange, appropriation, suppression, rediscovery, and competing standards of
  proof;
- folklore, literature, theater, visual art, journalism, broadcasting, film, and digital media
  when they shaped public understanding.

Boundary: explain a knowledge system in context, then separately assess empirical claims when
the page reaches the present. Do not grant or deny respect by pretending all claims have equal
evidentiary support.

### 10. Professions and disciplines

Include the formation, work, authority, conflicts, and public roles of:

- psychiatry and psychiatric nursing;
- psychology and its research, applied, clinical, counseling, school, industrial, community,
  developmental, social, cognitive, and other branches;
- social work, casework, community organization, settlement work, and social welfare;
- counseling, psychotherapy, psychoanalysis, marriage and family therapy, and peer support;
- neuroscience, neurology, medicine, public health, occupational therapy, and related fields
  where their work materially concerns mental life;
- non-professional, traditional, religious, community, family, and mutual-aid caregivers.

Boundary: professional history must include clients, workers, institutions, jurisdiction,
economics, exclusions, and interprofessional conflict—not only founders and theories.

### 11. Institutions, systems, law, and economics

Include histories of:

- households, temples, monasteries, hospitals, asylums, colonies, prisons, schools, armies,
  clinics, community programs, welfare agencies, residential facilities, and digital services;
- commitment, capacity, guardianship, criminal responsibility, disability benefits, insurance,
  parity, privacy, licensing, professional regulation, and civil rights;
- funding, labor, markets, philanthropy, state responsibility, privatization, and access;
- deinstitutionalization, community care, incarceration, homelessness, and service gaps;
- records, surveillance, data, algorithms, telehealth, platforms, and digital mental health.

Boundary: general legal, carceral, welfare, or economic history belongs only where it changes
mental-health concepts, care, experience, rights, or access.

### 12. Power, identity, rights, and resistance

Include histories of:

- colonialism, racism, caste, class, gender, sexuality, religion, age, citizenship, language,
  and disability as forces shaping diagnosis, treatment, credibility, confinement, and access;
- eugenics, forced treatment, sterilization, conversion practices, racialized diagnosis,
  political abuse, and medical experimentation;
- patient, survivor, disability, civil-rights, feminist, queer, anticolonial, labor, and
  community movements;
- consent, autonomy, dignity, personhood, recovery, self-definition, and the right to refuse;
- conflicts between care, protection, liberty, safety, and social control.

Boundary: power is not a detached “controversy” sidebar. It must be examined wherever it
materially shaped the subject.

## Historical contexts admitted conditionally

These are not independent content empires. Include them when they explain a core realm:

- wars, revolutions, disasters, epidemics, and mass displacement;
- political systems, empires, colonial administration, nation-building, and social movements;
- economic change, industrialization, urbanization, labor systems, poverty, and inequality;
- religious change, missionary activity, secularization, and reform;
- transportation, print, mass media, computing, pharmaceuticals, imaging, and communications;
- education systems, prisons, welfare systems, housing, and immigration systems;
- demographic change and shifts in family or community organization;
- climate and environmental conditions when a specific mental-life connection is supported.

The page should include only the contextual history needed to understand that connection.

## Mandatory lenses for every major cluster

Every authority page or cluster must answer these questions when evidence permits:

1. **Time:** What changed, what persisted, and which period labels are regional?
2. **Place:** Where does this account apply, and what happens elsewhere?
3. **Language:** What terms did people use, who coined them, and what is lost in translation?
4. **Explanation:** How did people explain the experience or behavior?
5. **Lived experience:** Whose own words survive? Whose experience is inferred?
6. **Care and consequence:** What help, harm, status, rights, or restrictions followed?
7. **Power:** Who could name, diagnose, confine, treat, pay, refuse, or reform?
8. **Identity and life stage:** How did age, gender, sexuality, race, caste, class, disability,
   religion, and citizenship affect the story?
9. **Evidence:** What is documented, strongly supported, plausible, disputed, or unknown?
10. **Connection:** How did ideas or practices move between cultures and institutions?
11. **Present comparison:** What does current evidence say, without treating the present as the
    inevitable endpoint of history?
12. **Reader bridge:** Which modern question or misconception makes this history useful now?

Not every focused page needs twelve visible sections. The research record must still confront
the applicable questions.

## Evidence streams the outline must seek

“All data” means all relevant kinds of evidence, not every surviving datum. The outline must
make room for:

- archaeological and bioarchaeological evidence;
- artifacts, architecture, instruments, medicines, clothing, and environments;
- medical, institutional, legal, religious, school, military, welfare, and employment records;
- philosophical, scientific, medical, and religious texts;
- diaries, letters, memoirs, oral histories, testimony, interviews, and patient publications;
- newspapers, magazines, pamphlets, advertising, radio, film, television, and digital media;
- art, literature, music, performance, folklore, and material culture;
- demographic, epidemiological, administrative, and economic data;
- professional debates, manuals, diagnostic systems, curricula, and association records;
- family, caregiver, community, activist, disability, and survivor archives;
- Indigenous and local knowledge documented with appropriate provenance and community context;
- modern scholarly analysis, including disagreements over interpretation.

Absence is also evidence about archives and power, but it is not evidence of what an absent
person thought.

## Provisional public page families

These are content jobs, not final navigation labels:

- **Era and transition guides** — orient readers in time without universalizing regional eras.
- **Regional and cultural histories** — develop place-specific accounts on their own terms.
- **Long-arc guides** — trace a recurring theme across eras and regions.
- **People and communities** — place lives and collective work inside their networks.
- **Ideas and explanations** — compare models of mind, behavior, distress, care, or evidence.
- **Experiences and categories** — follow changing descriptions without retroactive diagnosis.
- **Practices and treatments** — explain purpose, experience, evidence, benefit, harm, and legacy.
- **Institutions and systems** — show how rules, labor, funding, and architecture affected lives.
- **Professions and movements** — cover disciplines, workers, clients, conflicts, and reforms.
- **Objects, texts, and experiments** — use a concrete artifact as a doorway into a larger story.
- **Turning points and disputes** — examine reforms, reversals, crises, and credible disagreements.
- **How we know** — explain evidence, archive gaps, translations, methods, and uncertainty.

The master outline may merge, rename, or reject these families after the pilot.

## Explicit exclusions

Do not create:

- general world-history summaries with no material mental-life connection;
- general brain, anatomy, medical, legal, religious, military, or technology history without a
  demonstrated connection;
- current diagnosis, treatment, or self-help instructions presented as historical content;
- individualized medical advice or a substitute for care;
- speculative diagnoses of famous or historical people as entertainment;
- sensational lists of “bizarre,” “crazy,” or “barbaric” treatments stripped of human context;
- biographies that merely collect life events without advancing a historical question;
- catalogs of every theorist, institution, diagnosis, drug, or therapy regardless of influence;
- unverified legends, recycled anecdotes, quotation memes, or claims sourced only to snippets;
- pages built solely for spelling variants, city-keyword combinations, or other doorway tactics;
- copied encyclopedia entries, AI summaries, or lightly rewritten source material;
- a Western chronology with other regions confined to sidebars;
- advocacy disguised as settled history or false balance disguised as fairness;
- exploitative patient imagery, identifiable private records, or graphic material included only
  for attention;
- directory marketing, clinician recruitment, pricing, or product promotion inside the history.

Historically necessary discussions of suicide, self-harm, violence, abuse, or other sensitive
subjects are not categorically excluded. They must serve a real historical question, avoid
crisis-style traffic framing, and pass the project's empathy, safety, and evidence reviews.

## Terminology guardrails

- **Mental health:** use as the modern umbrella for the project, not as a claim that every
  culture or period shared the category.
- **Mental illness / disorder:** use when discussing a defined framework or quoted source;
  do not silently impose it on all distress or difference.
- **Patient, client, service user, survivor, inmate, resident:** use the term appropriate to the
  source and relationship; explain contested terms when they matter.
- **Madness, insanity, lunacy, idiocy, hysteria, degeneration, feeblemindedness:** use in
  quotations, historical names, or careful analysis—not as the narrator's labels.
- **Indigenous / traditional:** name the specific people, place, practitioner, and provenance
  whenever possible. Neither term means timeless, uniform, or unchanging.
- **Primitive:** do not use as the narrator's description of a people or practice.
- **Medieval, Renaissance, Enlightenment, modern:** state the geography and scholarly meaning;
  none is a universal world period.
- **Discovery / first:** verify priority, predecessors, translation routes, and excluded
  contributors before using.
- **Treatment:** distinguish intended healing from custody, punishment, experimentation,
  control, commerce, or care.
- **Wellness / resilience:** identify who defines the ideal, who benefits, and whether structural
  harms are being recast as individual responsibility.
- **Normal / abnormal:** identify the standard, decision-maker, setting, and consequences.
- **Evidence-based:** name the evidence, date, population, outcome, and limits rather than using
  the phrase as a badge.

## Known tensions to preserve

The project should not resolve these by choosing one permanent side:

- brain and body explanations **and** social, cultural, relational, and spiritual explanations;
- relief of suffering **and** respect for difference;
- care and protection **and** liberty and refusal;
- professional expertise **and** lived expertise;
- universal human patterns **and** culturally specific meanings;
- diagnosis as access and recognition **and** diagnosis as labeling and control;
- scientific advance **and** the harms or exclusions embedded in scientific institutions;
- individual coping **and** structural causes;
- historical empathy **and** moral clarity;
- current evidence **and** humility about future revision.

These tensions are navigational features. They should generate comparisons and cross-links,
not be flattened into a single slogan.
