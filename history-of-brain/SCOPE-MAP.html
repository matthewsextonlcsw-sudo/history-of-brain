Set the map's edges

Scope map

A boundary for studying human mental life across all of history without accidentally turning the project into all of human history—or shrinking it into a parade of famous doctors.

Planning artifact, not historical content.
This map makes editorial decisions. Factual claims and external citations begin with the evidence model and research records.

The center

The project studies human mental life across time: how people understood it, experienced it, described it, cared for it, changed it, coped with it, and used power around it.

If a subject does not materially change or reveal one of the eight actions below, it is outside the project.
UnderstandMind, brain, self, behavior, emotion, or consciousness.
ExperienceAccounts of mental life, distress, difference, coping, and wellness.
DescribeLanguage, categories, metaphors, measurements, and narratives.
CareFamily, community, spiritual, peer, professional, and institutional support.
TreatRemedies, therapies, medicines, procedures, environments, and prevention.
CopeWays people managed inner life, relationships, adversity, and daily living.
ClassifyHealth and illness, normal and abnormal, capacity, danger, and difference.
GovernLaws, confinement, rights, welfare, authority, resistance, and reform.

Four scope levels

Core history

Directly changes or reveals one of the eight actions. It belongs in the backbone or a first-class thematic path.

Necessary context

Not mental-health history by itself, but required to understand a core development. Include only the connecting portion.

Cross-reference

Relevant but primarily belongs elsewhere. Explain the mental-life connection briefly and link to its proper home.

Outside

No material connection, no responsible evidence, duplicate page job, or keyword-only justification.

The admission test

  1. Connection: Which of the eight actions does the topic materially affect or reveal? None means exclude.
  2. Historical job: Does it show change, continuity, conflict, exchange, lived experience, or an evidence problem?
  3. Evidence: Is there a responsible path to primary evidence, strong scholarship, or both?
  4. Human stakes: Can we identify who experienced consequences, who held authority, and whose voice survives?
  5. Geographic truth: Are we using a regional development as though it applied everywhere?
  6. Distinct page job: Does it answer a question another planned page does not?
  7. Ethical publishability: Can it be presented without invention, exploitation, or context-stripping?

Twelve core realms

The realms overlap on purpose. Each opens a different route through the history; none is the single master taxonomy.

01

Mind, brain, body, and self

Brain and nervous system; consciousness, perception, memory, learning, language, imagination, reasoning, identity, sleep, dreams, embodiment, and mind–body theories.

Edge: General biology enters only through an explicit mental-life connection.
02

Emotion, mood, temperament, and personality

Named feelings, culturally specific emotion concepts, expression, regulation, character, motivation, attachment, loneliness, and judgments about acceptable feeling.

Edge: Current English emotion categories are not universal or timeless.
03

Distress, difference, diagnosis, and disability

Changing descriptions of unusual or distressing experience; classification, measurement, disability, neurodiversity, stigma, identity, and survivor movements.

Edge: No casual retroactive diagnosis of historical people.
04

Healing, treatment, care, and prevention

Household, community, spiritual, Indigenous, medical, psychological, social, and institutional practices—including help, harm, consent, compulsion, and access.

Edge: Historical description does not become present-day medical advice.
05

Coping, wellness, resilience, and daily life

Rest, ritual, friendship, creativity, movement, nature, humor, mutual aid, self-help, mindfulness, positive psychology, wellness industries, and harmful coping.

Edge: Wellness is not treated as neutral, timeless, or automatically beneficial.
06

Relationships, family, community, and development

Life stages, attachment, parenting, kinship, sexuality, caregiving, belonging, isolation, schools, workplaces, migration, and family-led reform.

Edge: General social history enters only through a demonstrated mental-life connection.
07

Trauma, violence, disaster, and survival

Shock, grief, stress, dissociation, war, enslavement, colonization, genocide, displacement, disaster, interpersonal violence, memory, and survivor organizing.

Edge: Suffering cannot be spectacle or generic historical scenery.
08

Substances, addiction, compulsion, and altered states

Intoxication, dependence, craving, recovery, ritual and medical use, criminalization, treatment systems, harm reduction, gambling, and altered states.

Edge: Do not romanticize or flatten culturally specific practices.
09

Knowledge systems and explanations

Philosophical, religious, spiritual, Indigenous, local, medical, scientific, legal, artistic, literary, and popular explanations—and how they traveled.

Edge: Contextual respect and current evidentiary assessment remain separate.
10

Professions and disciplines

Psychiatry, psychology, neuroscience, social work, counseling, psychotherapy, nursing, public health, peer support, community care, and related work.

Edge: Include workers, clients, economics, exclusions, and conflicts—not only founders.
11

Institutions, systems, law, and economics

Asylums, hospitals, prisons, schools, armies, clinics, welfare, community programs, commitment, rights, insurance, licensing, access, data, and digital systems.

Edge: General institutional history must change mental-health concepts, care, rights, or experience.
12

Power, identity, rights, and resistance

Colonialism, racism, caste, class, gender, sexuality, age, citizenship, disability, eugenics, forced treatment, consent, personhood, activism, refusal, and reform.

Edge: Power belongs inside every relevant story, not in a detached controversy box.

Conditional contexts

These subjects enter only when they explain a core realm. They do not become independent content empires.

Mass eventsWar, revolution, disaster, epidemic, enslavement, and displacement.
Political systemsEmpire, colonial administration, nation-building, and social movements.
Material lifeIndustrialization, urbanization, poverty, inequality, labor, and housing.
Belief changeReligious change, missionary activity, secularization, and reform.
TechnologyPrint, transport, drugs, imaging, computing, communications, and platforms.
EnvironmentClimate and environmental conditions with a supported mental-life connection.

Twelve mandatory lenses

Not every focused page needs twelve visible sections. Its research record must still confront the applicable questions.

TimeWhat changed, persisted, and applies only to a particular region?
PlaceWhere does the account apply, and what happens elsewhere?
LanguageWhat terms were used, by whom, and what is lost in translation?
ExplanationHow did people explain the experience or behavior?
Lived experienceWhose own words survive, and whose experience is inferred?
Care and consequenceWhat help, harm, status, rights, or restrictions followed?
PowerWho could name, diagnose, confine, treat, pay, refuse, or reform?
Identity and life stageHow did age, gender, race, caste, class, disability, religion, sexuality, or citizenship matter?
EvidenceWhat is documented, strongly supported, plausible, disputed, or unknown?
ConnectionHow did ideas and practices move between cultures and institutions?
Present comparisonWhat does current evidence say without making the present history's inevitable endpoint?
Reader bridgeWhich modern question or misconception makes the history useful now?

The hard boundary

Admit carefully

  • Sensitive subjects when they serve a real historical question.
  • General history only to explain a mental-life connection.
  • Competing knowledge systems in context plus current evidence assessment.
  • Historical practices including benefit, uncertainty, harm, consent, and coercion.
  • Missing voices as an archive and power finding—not invented testimony.

Keep out

  • Keyword-only, duplicate, doorway, or city-variant pages.
  • Casual diagnosis of historical people or sensational “crazy treatment” lists.
  • Unopened sources, search snippets, recycled anecdotes, and AI summaries.
  • General history with no demonstrated mental-life connection.
  • Medical advice, product promotion, directory marketing, and clinician recruitment.
  • A Western chronology with everyone else placed in sidebars.

Words that can bend the map

Mental healthUse as the project's modern umbrella, not as proof that every culture or era shared the category.
Patient, client, survivorUse the term appropriate to the relationship and source. Explain contested terms when they matter.
Historical slurs and diagnosesUse in quotations, official names, or careful analysis—not as the narrator's labels.
Indigenous or traditionalName the people, place, practitioner, and provenance whenever possible. Neither term means timeless or uniform.
Medieval or modernState the geography and scholarly meaning. Neither is a universal world period.
Discovery or firstVerify predecessors, translation routes, priority disputes, and people excluded from credit.
TreatmentDistinguish intended healing from care, custody, punishment, experimentation, commerce, and control.
Wellness or resilienceAsk who defines the ideal, who benefits, and whether structural harms became individual responsibility.
Normal or abnormalName the standard, decision-maker, setting, and consequences.
Evidence-basedName the evidence, date, population, outcome, and limits instead of using the phrase as a badge.

Tensions we keep alive

Relief of suffering+Respect for difference
Care and protection+Liberty and refusal
Professional expertise+Lived expertise
Universal patterns+Culturally specific meanings
Diagnosis as recognition+Diagnosis as labeling and control
Scientific advance+Institutional harm and exclusion
Individual coping+Structural causes
Historical empathy+Moral clarity
Current evidence+Humility about future revision

Pass 1 approval gate

  1. The eight actions correctly define the project's center.
  2. The twelve realms cover the subject without making all human history core.
  3. Conditional context remains constrained to its mental-life connection.
  4. The mandatory lenses expose geographic, archival, experiential, and power gaps.
  5. The exclusions block thin, speculative, exploitative, and promotional content.
  6. The terminology rules stop present-day categories from rewriting the past.
  7. The admission test is usable by future researchers without guesswork.
