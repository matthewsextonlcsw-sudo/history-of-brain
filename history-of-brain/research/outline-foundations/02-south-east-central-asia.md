# Outline foundations: South, East, Southeast, and Central Asia

**Status:** Research note for Pass 3–4 outline construction; not a publishable outline or article
**Geographic job:** Identify regionally grounded transitions and transregional routes without treating present-day nations, “Asia,” or any tradition as timeless wholes
**Evidence state:** Candidate anchors opened and checked on Aug. 18, 2026; individual public claims still require claim-ledger extraction and source verification
**Applies:** `PROJECT-BRAIN.md`, `outline/SCOPE-MAP.md`, and `sources/EVIDENCE-MODEL.md`

## Executive finding

The Asian history should not be a row of country summaries or a prelude in which “Eastern wisdom” waits for Western psychiatry. The defensible backbone is a sequence of **changing problems, institutions, and routes**:

1. ancient and classical arguments about self, mind, emotion, conduct, suffering, embodiment, and healing;
2. the movement and translation of Buddhist, medical, philosophical, ritual, and pharmacological knowledge over land and sea;
3. Persianate, Islamic, Sanskritic, Sinographic, Tibetan, and vernacular syntheses that continued to change in courts, monasteries, households, markets, and medical lineages;
4. colonial and imperial systems that made the asylum, census, racial classification, police, prison, school, and professional credential newly powerful;
5. twentieth-century wars, revolutions, developmental states, national professions, mass institutions, and community experiments; and
6. unfinished rights-based, disability-led, community-care, and plural-care reforms in which law, services, family responsibility, coercion, and lived experience often diverge.

That structure lets the master outline hold both **ideas about mental life** and **what happened to people**, while keeping East–West exchange, South–South exchange, colonization, and local agency visible.

## Guardrails before mapping topics

- **Do not translate backward into current diagnoses.** A major Indian historical review explicitly warns that *unmada* cannot simply be conformed to schizophrenia; the same rule should apply to Chinese *dian/kuang*, Persian *malikhūliyā*, possession idioms, Buddhist afflictions, and colonial labels such as “lunatic” ([Jain et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6198594/)).
- **Do not use “Asian thought” as a unit.** Even within one textual sphere, sources disagree: Indian Buddhist theories developed across many schools over roughly fifteen centuries, while classical Chinese philosophers disagreed about the heart-mind, desire, cultivation, and authority ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/); [SEP, “Mind (Heart-Mind) in Chinese Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-mind/)).
- **Do not oppose “rational medicine” to “superstition” as a universal timeline.** Premodern and present-day people have combined household, religious, medical, astrological, herbal, and institutional routes; a study in Rakhine State, Myanmar, demonstrates this plurality rather than a neat replacement sequence ([Coderey, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022463420000259)).
- **Do not make the asylum the whole history of care.** Colonial records overrepresent institutions and officials. Work on colonial psychiatry in Vietnam and Singapore shows that family economies, labor, repatriation, resistance, and care beyond asylum walls are central to the story ([Edington, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8437878/); [Lin, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1215/18752160-3460689)).
- **Separate historical importance from present efficacy.** The endurance or global popularity of Ayurveda, meditation, traditional Chinese medicine, Unani, spirit healing, Morita therapy, or any other system is a historical claim—not proof that every empirical claim or intervention is supported today.

## Proposed chronological transitions and topic fields

### Transition 1 — Before “mental health”: mind, self, suffering, emotion, and order in ancient and classical texts

**Proposed range:** c. first millennium BCE–c. 600 CE, with different local chronologies and explicit dating uncertainty.

**Why it changes the story:** The central units were not “psychiatry” and “mental disorder.” Texts and practices joined mental life to ethics, cosmology, body, conduct, social relationship, ritual, and liberation in different ways.

#### South Asia

- Map **Vedic and Upaniṣadic vocabularies of mind, self, waking, dream, memory, and conduct** as historically layered textual arguments, not a single Hindu psychology. Dating and authorship must remain ranges; the history-of-psychiatry synthesis itself says chronology is a major problem for ancient Indian texts ([Jain et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6198594/)).
- Map **classical Ayurveda as a changing medical corpus**, including *manas*, *unmada*, *apasmāra*, bodily humors, qualities, causation, regimen, medicine, attendants, and household or practitioner care. A cautious chronology places the best-known Caraka and Suśruta compilations broadly between the third century BCE and third century CE rather than at legendary author-dates ([Agarwal et al., 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3146199/)). Use the [National Library of Medicine catalog record for the multilingual critical edition of the *Caraka Saṃhitā Nidānasthāna*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nlmcatalog/272532), not wellness-site paraphrases, for future primary-text extraction.
- Make **Buddhist analyses of suffering, no-self, perception, attention, intention, mental factors, and cultivation** a first-class intellectual history. Do not flatten early discourses, Abhidharma schools, Yogācāra, and later regional traditions: the philosophical synthesis dates systematic Abhidharma accounts roughly 150 BCE–450 CE and emphasizes their internal development and debate ([SEP, “Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/)).
- Include **Jain, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, and Vedānta** only with specialist sources showing their specific historical job in theories of self, cognition, affect, discipline, or care. This is a research gap in the present source set, not permission to subsume them into “Indian philosophy.”

#### East Asia

- Map the classical Chinese **xin** (“heart-mind”) problem: cognition, intention, desire, mood, emotion, and moral guidance were not divided along a modern reason-versus-feeling line. The sixth- through third-century BCE debates include Confucian, Mohist, Daoist, Mengzian, and Xunzian differences ([SEP, “Mind (Heart-Mind) in Chinese Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-mind/)).
- Give **emotion and relational self-cultivation** its own route. In early Chinese sources, *qing* has a broader and historically changing semantic range than the modern English “emotion,” and emotions may be cognitive, moral, relational, and political rather than private inner feelings ([SEP, “The Emotions in Early Chinese Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-chinese/)).
- Map **premodern medical plurality around madness**, including *dian*, *kuang*, wind, phlegm-fire, heart diseases, emotional disorders, needling, drug therapy, talking, and ritual. Hsiu-fen Chen’s open handbook chapter stresses that medical views and treatments were plural, which is a better anchor than projecting a single “TCM view” backward ([Chen, “Pre-modern madness”](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203740262-18)).
- Hold Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and the Ryukyu and island worlds out of a “Chinese diffusion” sidebar. Later work must trace what was translated, adapted, resisted, or institutionally reworked in each place and period.

#### Questions for the master outline

1. When does the history ask “Where is mind?” and when is that an imported framing?
2. How did traditions distinguish distress, moral failure, altered state, disability, possession, bodily disorder, and cultivated transformation?
3. Who could become a healer, scholar, monk, ritual specialist, physician, or patient—and whose labor is invisible in the texts?
4. Which practices were intended to relieve suffering, cultivate virtue, secure social order, attain liberation, or do several at once?

### Transition 2 — Translation zones: monasteries, ports, courts, and overland routes move mental and medical knowledge

**Proposed range:** c. first century BCE–c. 1200 CE; use route-specific dates rather than one “Silk Road era.”

**Why it changes the story:** Ideas did not stay inside modern borders. Translation changed concepts, and the same route could carry scriptures, medicines, ritual techniques, stories, commercial goods, and political authority.

- Use **Dunhuang, Kucha, Turfan, Gandhāra/Kashmir, the Tarim Basin, Chang’an, and maritime ports** as exchange nodes, not as peripheral dots between civilizations. UNESCO identifies Dunhuang as a crossroads whose manuscripts preserve Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Khotanese, Uighur, Sogdian, and other communities, while the Chang’an–Tianshan corridor documents Buddhism’s eastward movement through Central Asian sites ([UNESCO, “Dunhuang”](https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/content/dunhuang); [UNESCO World Heritage Centre, Chang’an–Tianshan Corridor](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1442/)).
- Trace **Buddhist models of mind and healing through translators and institutions**, not with a vague arrow labeled “Buddhism spreads.” Scholarship on medieval China shows that translation of Buddhist knowledge became part of the medical world, and work on tantric dissemination distinguishes maritime routes to Southeast Asia and China from overland routes through Tibet, Mongolia, and Central Asia ([Salguero, *Translating Buddhist Medicine in Medieval China*](https://www.pennpress.org/9780812246117/translating-buddhist-medicine-in-medieval-china/); [Dalton, “Dissemination and Reception of the Tantras”](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198873257.003.0003)).
- Treat manuscript finds as **evidence of contact, not proof of population-wide belief or practice**. The Bower Manuscript is a fragmentary Sanskrit medical collection associated with the Kucha region; it can anchor a page about transmission only after a manuscript specialist verifies provenance, dating, and relevant contents ([Zhu et al., 2015](https://rs.yiigle.com/CN112155201503/1248629.htm)).
- Add a **maritime South and Southeast Asian route** for Buddhist, Hindu, Islamic, Chinese, and local healing exchanges. It should not imply that external traditions erased Austronesian, mainland Southeast Asian, highland, and island knowledge; evidence will need port-, court-, inscription-, manuscript-, and ethnolinguistic-level research.
- Make **translation itself an episode**: Sanskrit/Pāli terms entering Chinese, Tibetan, Khotanese, Sogdian, Japanese, Korean, and Southeast Asian languages did not retain perfectly fixed meanings. The outline should allow “word journeys” for *citta*, *manas*, *vijñāna*, *smṛti/sati*, *duḥkha/dukkha*, *xin*, *shen*, and *qing* only after philological review.

### Transition 3 — Regional syntheses: Persianate medicine, Islamic philosophy, vernacular care, and continuing Asian traditions

**Proposed range:** c. 900–1700 CE, overlapping earlier and later traditions.

**Why it changes the story:** This period is neither an empty “medieval” gap nor the static preservation of classical canons. Medical, philosophical, religious, and practical knowledge was recomposed across languages and institutions.

#### Central and South Asian Persianate networks

- Anchor the Central Asian–Persianate route with **al-Akhawayni Bukhari and Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna)**, not as isolated “great men” but as physician-authors within Arabic- and Persian-language medicine. Al-Akhawayni’s tenth-century Persian *Hidāyat* discussed melancholia, while Ibn Sīnā (980–1037), born near Bukhara, integrated regimen, sleep, activity, environment, and psychological or mental control in the *Canon* ([Shoja & Tubbs, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4087154/); [Zargaran et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8999087/)).
- Treat **Unani in South Asia as entangled history**, not a foreign package that arrived unchanged. Historical scholarship describes Greek, Arabic, Persian, Central Asian, Western, and Ayurvedic influences meeting in accounts of the “Indian body” ([Attewell, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1524/para.2009.0008)). The future outline should follow Delhi Sultanate, Mughal, regional court, household, market, and colonial transformations separately.
- Do not repeat “the first psychiatric hospital” claims about **bīmāristāns** without institution-specific primary evidence. A page may compare general hospitals’ provision for people described as mad or melancholic, household care, waqf funding, and later memory, but should flag the triumphalist modern genre that turns heterogeneous hospitals into modern psychiatric wards.

#### East and Southeast Asian continuities and changes

- Trace **Song–Qing Chinese medical and philosophical change**, including changing *dian/kuang* explanations, emotion, body, family governance, ritual, drug practice, and Neo-Confucian arguments. Do not call all of this “TCM”; the modern systematized category is not identical to the heterogeneous history it selects from ([Chen, “Pre-modern madness”](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203740262-18)).
- Trace **Kampo in Japan, Korean medical lineages, and Vietnamese learned and vernacular medicine** as adaptations with their own institutions and texts. These are required research gaps; the present sources do not justify a chronology yet.
- Treat **Buddhist monasteries, temples, spirit mediums, astrologers, herbalists, kin, and local ritual specialists** as potential caregivers whose relationships varied by setting. Contemporary ethnography in Rakhine shows why “medical” and “religious” cannot be assumed to be separate local domains, but it must not be projected unchanged into earlier centuries ([Coderey, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022463420000259)).
- Use the **babaylan/katalonan** in the Philippines only through carefully contextualized early colonial sources and current historians. A microhistory of Manila–Mexico uses a c. 1590 account to show a babaylan as a local ritual specialist, but colonial description, translation, and persecution shape what survives ([Tremml-Werner, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/pastj/gtz031)).

### Transition 4 — Empire builds categories: asylums, police power, race, labor, and professional authority

**Proposed range:** c. 1740s–1940s, with different imperial timelines.

**Why it changes the story:** The asylum and “lunacy” law were not simply new treatments. They joined medicine to armies, policing, migration control, labor, race, welfare, and the colonial state.

#### British South Asia

- Map the early asylums as **colonial military and civic institutions**, then follow the 1858 and 1912 lunacy laws, overcrowding, professionalization, renaming, labor, mortality, and patient routes. The Indian synthesis locates early institutions in Calcutta, Kilpauk, and Monghyr and describes the later legal phases, but every date should be checked against original reports and statutes before publication ([Jain et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6198594/)).
- Put **case notes, patient letters, petitions, discharge, death, transfer, and family negotiation** beside superintendent reports. The colonial North India archive includes case registers, annual reports, official files, journals, and monographs, while many patient voices remain filtered through institutional writing ([Ernst, *Curing Madness?*, chapter 4](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780199497780.003.0004)). The [National Library of Scotland’s digitized Bengal asylum reports](https://digital.nls.uk/indiapapers/browse/archive/83377958) are a concrete primary-source collection.
- Map **academic psychology and psychoanalysis as colonial and anticolonial networks**, not imports copied without agency. Indian psychologists used international disciplines in projects of *swaraj*, while Girindrasekhar Bose created an Indian psychoanalytic institution and correspondence network ([Laskin, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1479244313000153); [Kakar, *Freud’s India*](https://academic.oup.com/book/8978)).

#### East Asia and Japanese empire

- Follow the **missionary asylum, municipal asylum, philanthropy, policing, and Chinese professional institution** as distinct forms. Work on Republican Shanghai and Canton shows that financing, refugee regulation, public safety, repatriation, missionaries, municipal authorities, and Chinese professionals all shaped institutions; Chinese-run neuropsychiatry was an active project, not mere reception ([Lu, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/hkad019)).
- Map **Meiji-era psychiatry and its adaptations**, then the Japanese imperial circuits through Taiwan and Korea. Morita therapy is a useful hinge: it was developed by Morita Masatake in an inpatient setting amid Japanese psychiatry’s engagement with German medicine, work discipline, and Buddhist interpretation, and later changed substantially ([Kitanaka et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11362480/); [Ogawa et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10078264/)).
- Treat colonial Korea and Taiwan as **sites where psychiatry served empire and was also debated or reworked locally**. The Korean history notes limited scope under Japanese rule but also preexisting and concurrent shamanic, Buddhist, traditional medical, press, and professional discussions ([Kim, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10556413/)).

#### Southeast Asian empires and regional exchange

- Use **Singapore, French Indochina, and the Dutch East Indies** to show that colonial psychiatry circulated regionally. French physicians studied Dutch “open door” care and patient labor in Java; cost, overcrowding, racial comparison, and therapeutic claims were inseparable ([Edington & Pols, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1017/S001041751600030X)).
- Make **patient labor and agricultural colonies** a power question, not a neutral therapy bullet. At Biên Hòa in 1934, patient work helped keep an underfunded institution operating, complicating official claims of voluntariness and therapy ([Edington, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8437878/)).
- Use Singapore’s 1841 asylum as a **port-city history** connecting migration, repatriation, class, race, opium, policing, and care outside the institution. Scholarship shows that Chinese sufferers sometimes contested asylum authority and pursued other routes of care ([Ng & Chee, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/09540260600775355); [Lin, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1215/18752160-3460689)).
- Follow **Bangkok/Siam separately from colonized neighbors**: the state founded organized mental-health care in 1889, later adopting somatic interventions, but the noncolonial state still participated in transnational medical networks ([Worakul et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19935095/)).

### Transition 5 — War, revolution, developmental states, and mass professions

**Proposed range:** c. 1930s–1980s.

**Why it changes the story:** Independence did not simply end colonial systems. War, partition, occupation, revolution, genocide, socialist governance, developmental policy, and new national professions rebuilt mental-health institutions in sharply different ways.

- **South Asia:** Map partition and independence, general-hospital psychiatry, NIMHANS, psychiatric nursing and social work, family participation, rural clinics, and community experiments. Community psychiatry in India emerged partly because hospitals could not meet population needs, not through the same deinstitutionalization trajectory as Europe and North America; projects from the 1950s through 1970s preceded the 1982 National Mental Health Programme ([Math et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6735056/)).
- **People’s Republic of China:** Use a staged rather than linear account—missionary institutions before 1949, indigenization after 1949, political control over diagnosis and detention during the Cultural Revolution, and later reintroduction and adaptation of international models ([Yip, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/0020764005056758)). Political misuse should be evidenced case by case rather than becoming a total description of all care.
- **Korea:** Treat the Korean War as a rupture that connected military psychiatry, refugees, social reconstruction, and state visions of a “cheerful” society; the profession’s growth was not simply a delayed copy of Japan or the United States ([Kim, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10556413/)).
- **Japan:** Map the 1950 Mental Health Law, institutional growth, family burden, community-care language, and later rights controversies. A 1962 WHO government report is a valuable period document because it presents the law as replacing custody with treatment, prevention, and community care; compare that claim with admissions, lived experience, and later critique rather than taking it at face value ([WHO Western Pacific, 1962 report](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/139060/WPR_RC013_00_GovRep_1962_en.pdf)).
- **Cambodia:** Make genocide and institutional destruction a central transition. Scholarship reports no statutory psychiatric services or training from 1975 to 1994, followed by a resource-constrained rebuilding from the 1990s ([Jegannathan et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6734772/); [Bertschy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634615261452815)). Survivor testimony, refugee-camp services, Buddhist care, NGOs, and the language of trauma need separate evidence streams.
- **Colonial/postcolonial Southeast Asian regionalism:** The 1937 Bandung conference on rural hygiene and psychiatry and later Bandung meanings invite a South–South and decolonizing route that does not make Europe the only connector ([Edington, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8437878/)).
- **Soviet Central Asia:** Map the hospital-centered, biologically oriented Soviet service model, political abuse, local professional training, and the relationship between formal psychiatry and traditional healers. The strongest review warns that evidence for the five post-Soviet Central Asian states is sparse and uneven, so the Soviet period requires Russian-, Kazakh-, Kyrgyz-, Tajik-, Turkmen-, and Uzbek-language archival work rather than inference from Russia alone ([Makhmud et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-021-02064-2)).

### Transition 6 — Rights, community care, plural pathways, and the gap between law and life

**Proposed range:** 1980s–present.

**Why it changes the story:** Recent history is not a simple march toward humane community care. Rights laws, survivor advocacy, family responsibility, underfunding, institutional persistence, coercion, and plural healing coexist.

- **India:** The National Mental Health Programme began in 1982, while the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 made rights and access explicit; section 18 defines a right to affordable, geographically accessible, nondiscriminatory care ([WHO South-East Asia repository](https://extranet.who.int/mindbank/collection/country/south_east_asia/all?page=all); [India Code, Mental Healthcare Act 2017](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/indiacode/handle/123456789/2249); [India Code, section 18](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/show-data?actid=AC_CEN_12_13_00024_201710_1517807327874&orderno=18&sectionId=6224&sectionno=18)). The outline must distinguish passage, enforcement, service availability, and lived access.
- **China:** The 2012 national mental-health law and subsequent service expansion belong in a longer path from hospital-centered psychiatry toward community provision, while rights, compulsory treatment, workforce, family obligation, and regional access require separate examination ([WHO China, “Mental health”](https://www.who.int/china/health-topics/mental-health); [WHO/Asia Pacific Observatory, *China Health System Review*](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/208229/9789290617280_eng.pdf)).
- **Southeast Asia:** Singapore’s general-hospital and community phases, the Philippines’ 2018 rights-based Mental Health Act, Cambodia’s rebuilding, and Indonesian reform should not be forced into one regional curve ([Ng & Chee, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/09540260600775355); [Philippines Supreme Court E-Library, Republic Act 11036](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/2/83255)).
- **Indonesia and coercion:** *Pasung*—shackling or confining people with actual or perceived psychosocial disabilities—shows how family fear, stigma, service scarcity, institutional abuse, religious care, and government reform can meet in one person’s life. Human Rights Watch’s 2016 report includes direct testimony and documented 175 recent cases; WHO has also recognized Indonesian work to end the practice ([Human Rights Watch, *Living in Hell*](https://www.hrw.org/report/2016/03/20/living-hell/abuses-against-people-psychosocial-disabilities-indonesia); [WHO Indonesia, 2020](https://www.who.int/indonesia/news/detail/10-12-2020-human-rights-day-indonesians-recognised-for-their-outstanding-achievements-in-mental-health-care)).
- **Central Asia after 1991:** The 1990s brought severe service collapse in the five former Soviet Central Asian republics; hospital concentration, limited psychosocial services, and low service-user involvement persisted unevenly, alongside revived and hybrid healing traditions ([Makhmud et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-021-02064-2)). The review found only 53 relevant items and just nine in internationally recognized journals, making the evidence gap itself part of the history.
- **Disaster and collective trauma:** Add route-specific histories rather than a universal PTSD template: Sri Lanka’s post-2004-tsunami community expansion and Japan’s post-1995 and post-2011 disaster systems are two contrasting anchors ([WHO South-East Asia Regional Committee, 2022](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/368126/sea-rc-75-23-eng.pdf); [WHO Kobe Centre, historical review](https://extranet.who.int/kobe_centre/sites/default/files/WHO%20Guidance_Research%20Methods_Health%20EDRM_2021_Chapter-1.3.pdf)).
- **Current regional policy:** WHO’s South-East Asia members adopted the 2022 Paro Declaration and a 2023–2030 action plan emphasizing person-centered community services; this is a current policy marker, not proof that deinstitutionalization has occurred ([WHO South-East Asia, 2024](https://www.who.int/southeastasia/publications/i/item/9789290211372)).

## Exchange routes the global outline must display

| Route | What may move | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| North India/Gandhāra–Kashmir–Tarim Basin–Dunhuang–China | Buddhist texts and scholastic models of mind; monastic practices; materia medica; translators; manuscripts | That “Buddhist psychology” was one system, translation preserved meanings, or manuscripts prove mass practice ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/); [UNESCO](https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/content/dunhuang)) |
| South Asian maritime ports–Sri Lanka–mainland and island Southeast Asia–southern China | Buddhist and Hindu traditions, medical substances, ritual specialists, commercial and court networks | A one-way Indianization story or erasure of local and Austronesian traditions; route needs specialist sourcing |
| Bukhara/Samarkand and Persianate networks–Iranian plateau–South Asia | Arabic/Persian medicine and philosophy, humoral categories, drugs, texts, practitioners, court patronage | That “Islamic medicine” or Unani was uniform or unchanged ([Attewell](https://doi.org/10.1524/para.2009.0008)) |
| Macau/Malacca–Indian Ocean and South China Sea drug routes | Pharmacological substances and names across Portuguese, Chinese, Sanskrit, Persian, and vernacular circuits | That a traded drug carried one stable theory; theriac and China-root histories show entanglement ([Arnold, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4986280/)) |
| European and American empires–Asian port cities and colonial interiors | Asylum plans, laws, racial science, professional credentials, statistics, therapies, personnel | A simple center-to-periphery transfer; colonial experts also learned regionally and local actors resisted or repurposed systems ([Edington & Pols](https://doi.org/10.1017/S001041751600030X)) |
| Japan–Korea–Taiwan and wider imperial network | German-derived psychiatry, Japanese professional institutions, colonial administration, local translations and therapies | That imperial systems displaced all prior care or affected colonies identically ([Kim](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10556413/)) |
| Soviet Union–Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan | Diagnostic schools, hospital organization, biological psychiatry, training, political authority | That Russian history supplies Central Asian lived experience or that post-1991 systems changed together ([Makhmud et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-021-02064-2)) |
| Bandung and international-health networks | Rural hygiene, social medicine, professional visits, development models, later decolonizing solidarity | That “global mental health” began only in the late twentieth century or that internationalism was politically neutral ([Edington](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8437878/)) |

## Evidence anchors to prioritize in the next research pass

1. **Sanskrit and regional medical manuscripts:** compare a critical *Caraka Saṃhitā* edition with manuscript histories and commentaries; the NLM catalog records a Sanskrit/English/Hindi/Marathi critical edition ([NLM](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nlmcatalog/272532)).
2. **Pāli, Sanskrit, Chinese, and Tibetan Buddhist corpora:** use critical translations tied to named recensions and passages; start with SEP bibliographies to identify editions, not as the final primary text ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/)).
3. **Early Chinese philosophical texts:** preserve the original term beside translation and compare at least two scholarly translations for disputed passages; SEP explains why *xin* and *qing* do not map cleanly to “mind” and “emotion” ([SEP mind](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-mind/); [SEP emotions](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-chinese/)).
4. **Dunhuang/Turfan and Silk Road manuscripts:** use the International Dunhuang Programme to reconnect digitized items dispersed among institutions. The Bibliothèque nationale de France describes IDP digitization of official, monastic, and personal archives, including its Pelliot collection ([BnF](https://www.bnf.fr/en/international-dunhuang-programme)).
5. **Colonial South Asian asylum reports:** use annual reports, case registers, laws, petitions, and newspapers together; begin with the digitized Bengal reports and Ernst’s archive map ([National Library of Scotland](https://digital.nls.uk/indiapapers/browse/archive/83377958); [Ernst](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780199497780.003.0004)).
6. **China municipal, missionary, philanthropic, and police archives:** trace institutions through financing, admission, repatriation, discharge, and family negotiation, following the multi-actor method used for Shanghai and Canton ([Lu](https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/hkad019)).
7. **French, Dutch, British, American, Japanese, and local Southeast Asian archives:** cross-read official reports against patient labor, repatriation, local press, family correspondence, and oral history ([Edington & Pols](https://doi.org/10.1017/S001041751600030X); [Lin](https://doi.org/10.1215/18752160-3460689)).
8. **Postwar laws and plans:** preserve the exact enacted text and implementation date. Good anchors include India’s 2017 Act and the Philippines’ 2018 Act ([India Code](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/indiacode/handle/123456789/2249); [Philippines Supreme Court E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/2/83255)).
9. **Survivor and disability evidence:** prioritize first-person interviews, testimony, organizations’ records, and ethically usable oral histories; HRW’s Indonesia investigation is an example with named methodology and direct testimony, but should not substitute for Indonesian survivor-led sources ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/report/2016/03/20/living-hell/abuses-against-people-psychosocial-disabilities-indonesia)).

## Archive and translation cautions

1. **Composite texts are not datable “books” in the modern sense.** Record composition range, recensions, commentary layers, manuscript date, critical editor, and translator separately.
2. **English terms can manufacture agreement.** Keep terms such as *xin*, *qing*, *shen*, *manas*, *citta*, *unmada*, *dukkha*, *malikhūliyā*, *dian*, *kuang*, and *pasung* visible until a specialist approves the translation. Scholarship on Chinese philosophy shows that translation differences can reflect the translator’s overall interpretation, not grammar alone ([SEP, “Translating and Interpreting Chinese Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-translate-interpret/)).
3. **“Psychology,” “psychotherapy,” and “psychiatry” are dangerous retrospective labels.** Use them in titles only when the page explicitly explains the analogy and the mismatch.
4. **Colonial diagnoses are administrative events as well as medical statements.** A case file may reveal what an official needed to justify admission, labor, transfer, or discharge—not the person’s unmediated experience.
5. **Survival is political.** Elite, male, literate, medical, monastic, court, missionary, and state records survive disproportionately. Ordinary household care, women’s labor, itinerant practice, caste and class conflict, and nonliterate knowledge may appear only indirectly.
6. **Collections are geographically dispersed.** Dunhuang and colonial collections were removed into European and Asian institutions; digitization improves access but does not erase provenance, ownership, or cultural-authority questions ([BnF International Dunhuang Programme](https://www.bnf.fr/en/international-dunhuang-programme)).
7. **Current national borders distort older networks.** Bukhara, Kucha, Gandhāra, Bengal, Punjab, Korea, Vietnam, Malaya, and island worlds changed political status. Index historical place, present location, language, and route separately.
8. **Modern ethnography is not a time machine.** It can show plural care today but cannot prove that the same practice or meaning existed centuries earlier.
9. **Institutional statistics require denominators and definitions.** Admission counts, “cure” rates, suicide rates, and diagnostic prevalence cannot be compared across jurisdictions until categories, population, recording, and missingness are checked.
10. **Rights law and implementation are separate evidence objects.** Publication date, commencement, regulations, budgets, workforce, access, court use, and survivor experience need distinct claims.

## Missing voices and required counter-archives

- **Ancient and classical patients:** almost never directly recoverable from prescriptive texts. State what a text recommends or imagines; do not turn it into a patient scene.
- **Household caregivers:** family care is frequently asserted but poorly described. Seek letters, petitions, legal disputes, expense records, ritual manuals, oral traditions, and material evidence rather than romanticizing “community.”
- **Women, servants, enslaved people, caste-oppressed communities, minority ethnic groups, and itinerant healers:** build a deliberate search plan by archive and language; canonical male authors cannot represent their experience.
- **Asylum inmates:** search patient letters, petitions, marginal notes, escape, refusal, labor records, visitor reports, discharge and readmission, court testimony, and death registers. Treat superintendent case summaries as mediated evidence.
- **Colonized and occupied populations:** pair Japanese, British, French, Dutch, Russian/Soviet, American, and missionary records with Korean, Taiwanese, Vietnamese, Indonesian, Malaysian, Filipino, Central Asian, and other local-language sources.
- **Refugees and war survivors:** prioritize testimony and community archives in Partition, the Korean War, Vietnam/Indochina wars, Khmer Rouge genocide, Afghanistan and borderland conflicts, Sri Lankan civil war and tsunami, and Japanese disasters; do not universalize one trauma vocabulary.
- **People with psychosocial and intellectual disabilities:** distinguish modern survivor, mad-pride, disability-rights, family, and professional organizations. Do not let service providers stand in as the only “lived experience” voice.
- **Central Asian service users:** this is an urgent evidence gap. The regional review found user involvement virtually absent outside limited Kyrgyz initiatives and very little internationally visible research ([Makhmud et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-021-02064-2)).
- **Languages outside English:** build a translator/reviewer roster for Sanskrit, Pāli, Classical Chinese, modern Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Tibetan, Persian, Arabic, Russian, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Tajik, Turkmen, Uzbek, Urdu, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Sinhala, Thai, Khmer, Vietnamese, Bahasa Indonesia/Malay, Tagalog and other Philippine languages, Burmese and minority languages. This is a starting audit, not a claim of sufficiency.

## Disputes the outline should preserve

| Dispute | Responsible treatment |
|---|---|
| Can classical categories be mapped to DSM/ICD diagnoses? | Usually no direct equivalence. Present phenomenological resemblances only as bounded scholarly comparisons and retain period language. |
| Were ancient systems “holistic” or “scientific”? | Define the comparison and avoid praise words. Describe the actual ontology, method, institution, and evidence claim. |
| Did religious explanations give way to rational medicine? | Reject the one-way ladder. Map coexistence, conflict, institutional authority, and combination in specific settings. |
| Were asylums humanitarian modernization or colonial control? | Show care, refuge, coercion, policing, race, labor, mortality, family negotiation, and professional goals together; do not force one verdict across all institutions. |
| Was community/family care more humane? | Compare autonomy, support, burden, abandonment, coercion, gendered labor, poverty, and service access; neither romanticize nor blame families. |
| Are Buddhism, Ayurveda, Chinese medicine, or Unani continuous unchanged traditions? | Trace edition, institution, practitioner, state standardization, revival, and commercialization. Continuity must be demonstrated, not assumed. |
| Did psychiatry travel one way from Europe and North America? | Preserve imperial power while documenting Asian adaptation, regional study tours, translation, refusal, and South–South exchange. |
| Did postwar rights laws transform care? | Separate enactment, implementation, resources, court enforcement, coercion, and lived access. |
| Were bīmāristāns the first psychiatric hospitals? | Treat as a priority claim requiring named institution, date, charter or administrative evidence, patient population, and comparison criteria; avoid civilizational scorekeeping. |
