# Outline foundations — Africa, the Americas, the Caribbean, and Oceania

**Status:** Research foundation for the master outline; not a publishable narrative
**Geographic remit:** Africa beyond an ancient-Nile-only frame; Indigenous Americas;
Caribbean; Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand, and the Pacific
**Time remit:** Earlier/local knowledge; colonial encounter and institutions;
post-independence reform; contemporary community care
**Sources opened:** Aug. 18, 2026

## What this note is for

This is a map of defensible topics and transitions for the eventual outline. It does not
claim that these enormous, internally diverse regions can be represented by one African,
Indigenous, Caribbean, or Pacific “worldview.” The recurring story is not a march from
“belief” to “science.” It is a history of multiple ideas about persons, minds, bodies,
spirits, families, lands, emotions, difference, responsibility, care, and coercion—ideas
that met through trade, migration, enslavement, conquest, missionization, medicine,
anti-colonial struggle, and reform.

The strongest outline will keep four threads visible in every era:

1. **Locally grounded personhood and care:** how particular communities located thought,
   feeling, distress, healing, and obligation.
2. **Institutions and power:** who acquired authority to name, confine, treat, educate,
   remove, or “normalize” people.
3. **Movement and exchange:** how concepts and practices traveled—and how conquest or
   forced migration changed them.
4. **Lived experience and refusal:** what patients, survivors, families, healers, disabled
   people, and communities said or did, including what the archive prevented them from
   saying.

## Research rules specific to these regions

- Do not translate a local term directly as “mental illness,” “mind,” “soul,” “therapy,”
  or “emotion” until a speaker or qualified linguistic/ethnographic source establishes the
  range of meaning. A recent Yorùbá practitioner-scholar, for example, argues that *wèrè*
  concerns an unraveling of bodily, environmental, ancestral, and spiritual relations,
  not simply an individual brain disorder; that is a valuable locally positioned argument,
  but it remains one situated analysis rather than a dictionary definition for all Yorùbá
  people or all periods ([Jegede 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11013-026-09992-1)).
- Do not project a twentieth- or twenty-first-century health framework backward as an
  unchanged ancient tradition. Te Whare Tapa Whā was developed by Tā Mason Durie, and
  Fonofale by Fuimaono Karl Pulotu-Endemann; both organize culturally grounded concepts,
  but both are also modern models created in specific policy and service contexts
  ([New Zealand Ministry of Health](https://www.health.govt.nz/maori-health/maori-health-models/te-whare-tapa-wha);
  [New Zealand Ministry of Health, *Pacific Peoples and Mental Health*](https://www.health.govt.nz/system/files/2011-12/pacific-peoples-and-menta-health-may08.pdf)).
- “Traditional healer” is not a single occupation, and use of a healer does not prove one
  uniform explanation of distress. Ghanaian care is pluralistic, with Indigenous, faith,
  and biomedical routes used in different combinations; the literature itself is uneven
  and often based on small studies
  ([Ae-Ngibise et al. 2010/2019 review](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S2071-29362019000100044&script=sci_arttext)).
- Written colonial sources are records of an encounter, not transparent windows onto the
  colonized. Spanish observers interpreted Andean healing through a European witchcraft
  lens, while Franciscan/Nahua contact changed the language through which animating forces
  were recorded ([Silverblatt 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6362989/);
  [Klaus 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/10609164.2018.1527525)).
- “Open” is not the same as ethically publishable. Indigenous authority may limit the use
  of knowledge, data, images, names, recordings, or ceremony. Apply Collective Benefit,
  Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics before reuse
  ([Global Indigenous Data Alliance, CARE Principles](https://www.gida-global.org/careprinciples)).
  For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander material, apply the AIATSIS ethics code and its
  direction to respect diverse knowledge systems
  ([AIATSIS Code of Ethics](https://aiatsis.gov.au/research/ethical-research/code-ethics)).
  For First Nations data in Canada, consult the relevant Nation and its interpretation of
  ownership, control, access, and possession rather than treating OCAP® as generic consent
  ([First Nations Information Governance Centre](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/)).
- Avoid images or detailed descriptions of ceremony merely because an archive has them.
  Historic recordings and photographs may have been made by outsiders under conditions
  that did not resemble present-day consent. Smithsonian Folkways identifies its Kalahari
  healing-dance recordings as the work of American anthropologists between 1968 and 1972;
  the catalog can anchor provenance, but it does not by itself authorize reuse or establish
  what should be public
  ([Smithsonian Folkways](https://folkways.si.edu/healing-dance-music-of-the-kalahari-san/world/album/smithsonian)).

## Track A — Africa beyond an ancient-Nile-only story

### A1. Before colonial psychiatry: persons, relationships, healing, and plurality

**Proposed outline transition:** Move from early material evidence and Nile/Red Sea worlds
to a warning: the absence of early written psychiatric categories does not mean an absence
of theories of persons, distress, or care. What survives most clearly is often later oral,
linguistic, ritual, material, or ethnographic evidence. It must not be frozen into an
imagined timeless “African mind.”

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **Ju/’hoansi communal healing dance as a case study in music, emotion, movement, and
  collective care—not as a fossil from prehistory.** A paper co-authored with Kunta Boo of
  Tsumkwe describes song, rhythm, heightened feeling, spontaneous movement, and shared
  healing in the Ju/’hoan dance. The evidentiary anchor is recent/late-twentieth-century
  practice and interpretation, even when rock art or long continuity is discussed elsewhere
  ([Keeney, Keeney, and Boo 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/14635240.2016.1142063)).
- **Yorùbá language as a challenge to brain-only histories.** Jegede’s practitioner-
  ethnographic account locates fear, happiness, and focus in different embodied sites and
  describes *wèrè* through ecological-cosmological relations. Use it to ask where a culture
  locates cognition and emotion, while preserving the author’s explicit insider/outsider
  position and avoiding claims of consensus or unchanged antiquity
  ([Jegede 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11013-026-09992-1)).
- **Health-care pluralism in West Africa.** A review of Ghana cautions that Indigenous,
  Christian/faith, and biomedical systems coexist; it also argues that colonial medicine
  and Christian missions disparaged or prohibited some local practices. This provides a
  bridge from community authority to colonial suppression and later mixed help-seeking
  ([Ae-Ngibise et al.](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S2071-29362019000100044&script=sci_arttext)).
- **Ubuntu/unhu must be treated as a family of philosophies and uses, not a continent-wide
  slogan.** A WHO African Health Monitor article places community acceptance of some
  clinical practices in particular traditions, cultures, norms, and philosophies such as
  *ubuntu* or *unhu*; it does not establish one universal historical model
  ([Mhame, Busia, and Kasilo](https://www.afro.who.int/sites/default/files/2017-06/ahm-special-issue-14.pdf)).
- **Southern African Indigenous dialogue and psychosocial care.** A KhoiSan community study
  used an Indigenous research framework and orature to examine healing dialogue. It can
  support an outline node on conversation, community, and locally governed methods, while
  its claims require later appraisal of sample, community authority, and generalizability
  ([Mothiba et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34522395/)).

**Regional caution:** The word “Africa” hides thousands of languages, political histories,
religious systems, and therapeutic specialists. The eventual outline needs separate
research lanes for at least Sahel/West Africa, Horn/East Africa, Central Africa, Great Lakes,
southern Africa, Indian Ocean Africa, and the Maghreb. Ethiopian church healing, Islamic
scholarly/therapeutic networks across the Sahel and Swahili coast, Central African and Great
Lakes traditions, and Malagasy/Indian Ocean histories remain major gaps in this pass.

### A2. Colonial states: confinement, race, labor, crime, and the making of “the African mind”

**Proposed outline transition:** European empires did not merely import hospitals. They
joined psychiatric authority to prisons, courts, labor regimes, racial theories, and the
administrative problem of governing colonized populations.

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **East African confinement grew through a crime-and-disorder apparatus.** Sloan Mahone’s
  history describes certification at the intersection of medicine, prisons, and colonial
  courts, all claiming authority to define collective African behavior
  ([University of Oxford research record](https://www.history.ox.ac.uk/publication/140844/pubmed)).
- **West African asylum histories must not be told only from colonial inspections.** Jock
  McCulloch notes that a 1936 colonial surveyor saw only part of a diverse Nigeria and had
  no reliable prevalence data; those limitations belong in the story, not only in a
  footnote ([McCulloch 1995](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511598548.003)).
- **Colonial institutions were uneven rather than one imperial template.** Research on
  Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal, and South Africa should compare laws, financing, racial
  segregation, prison transfers, labor, gender, and distance from family instead of using
  one “colonial asylum” paragraph. The Oxford East Africa account and McCulloch’s West
  African comparison provide starting points, not continental coverage
  ([Mahone](https://www.history.ox.ac.uk/publication/140844/pubmed);
  [McCulloch](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511598548.003)).
- **Algeria and anti-colonial psychiatry.** Frantz Fanon should connect Caribbean history,
  French colonial medicine, institutional psychiatry at Blida, and analyses of colonial
  violence. The outline must return to Fanon’s clinical writings and correspondence as
  primary sources; later histories should establish which reforms were actually practiced,
  rather than turning Fanon into a symbol without patients. A French archival finding aid
  identifies Fanon as a Fort-de-France-born psychiatrist, writer, and anti-colonial fighter;
  a university biography places his move to Algeria in 1953 and later involvement with the
  FLN ([IMEC Fanon archive](https://imec-archives.com/archives/fonds/178FNN);
  [London School of Economics](https://www.lse.ac.uk/africa/Hub-for-African-Thought/Thinkers/Frantz-Fanon)).
- **The record classified colonized people more readily than it preserved their speech.**
  A central research question for every asylum node should be: are we reading a patient’s
  words, a translated paraphrase, an attendant’s observation, a police report, or a doctor’s
  category? That question follows directly from the evidence model and from studies that
  expose how certification connected courts, prisons, and medicine
  ([Mahone](https://www.history.ox.ac.uk/publication/140844/pubmed)).

### A3. Decolonization did not end institutional inheritance

**Proposed outline transition:** Independence opened room for innovation, but buildings,
laws, professional hierarchies, diagnostic systems, donor priorities, and archives remained.

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **Fann in Senegal: innovation and its limits.** Henri Collomb described changing Fann
  from a closed asylum into a community open to families and requiring a family companion,
  but that is Collomb’s own retrospective account
  ([Collomb 1979](https://doi.org/10.1177/070674377902400515)). Ana Antić’s historiographic
  review shows why the outline must also include Senegalese criticism: later clinicians
  questioned whether Fann’s “culturalism” really escaped colonial power or Collomb’s own
  location within it
  ([Antić 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0952695121993702)).
- **Postcolonial Niger as a history “from below.”** A recent Medical History article uses
  Pavillon E to examine overcrowding, resource constraints, and the persistence of an asylum
  model after independence, while explicitly identifying a historiographical “black hole”
  in the postcolonial decades
  ([Beneduce and Collignon 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/medical-history/article/sources-of-madness-investigating-the-postcolonial-history-of-psychiatry-in-niger/669BDBC933C4E40FAAA25221FD60C3A8)).
- **Zimbabwe’s Friendship Bench as locally adapted, evidence-tested community care.** The
  project began in response to a district request for a no-cost intervention and uses trained
  lay health workers, Shona terminology, problem-solving therapy, and referral pathways
  ([Chibanda et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4865009/)). Chibanda’s own
  account says grandmothers co-developed terms such as *kuvhura pfungwa*, *kusimudzira*, and
  *kusimbisa*; it should be paired with trial and implementation evidence rather than told
  as a charming “grandmothers solve mental health” anecdote
  ([WHO interview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5996204/)).
- **South Africa’s Life Esidimeni disaster as a rights, state-capacity, and family-testimony
  node.** The official arbitration record concerns vulnerable mental-health-care users,
  government decisions, family evidence, and constitutional harms. It belongs in the
  outline as recent institutional history, with the award and Health Ombud report—not news
  summaries—as core evidence
  ([Life Esidimeni Arbitration Award](https://www.saflii.org/images/LifeEsidimeniArbitrationAward.pdf)).
- **Service users organized, not merely received reform.** A history of Ghana’s user
  movement describes community, district, and national groups of people with mental illness
  or epilepsy and carers. The current copy accessible through ResearchGate is only a lead;
  the publication and organizations must be independently obtained before claim-level use
  ([Kleintjes et al., discovery record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291577383_Lessons_from_the_African_user_movement_the_case_of_Ghana)).

**Disputes to preserve**

- Collaboration with religious or Indigenous healers can improve cultural fit and access,
  while some practices can be coercive or harmful. The outline should neither romanticize
  “tradition” nor assume biomedicine is the neutral comparator. Ghana’s plural-care review
  says the evidence base is limited; UN material documents continuing concern about
  shackling in prayer camps
  ([Ae-Ngibise et al.](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S2071-29362019000100044&script=sci_arttext);
  [UN Special Rapporteur country report](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/HRBodies/HRC/RegularSessions/Session31/Documents/A_HRC_31_57_Add_2_Eng_only.pdf)).
- Fann can be narrated as a breakthrough in family/social psychiatry and as an institution
  that continued to make African “culture” an object of a French-led science. Both positions
  have evidence and should appear together
  ([Collomb](https://doi.org/10.1177/070674377902400515);
  [Antić](https://doi.org/10.1177/0952695121993702)).

**Missing voices**

- Direct testimony from people confined in colonial African institutions, especially women,
  children, disabled people, and people recorded through interpreters.
- Nurses, attendants, family companions, religious leaders, and local healers in their own
  languages rather than as described by doctors.
- Lusophone and Belgian colonial archives; post-independence civil-war, displacement, and
  community-care histories; North African Arabic and Tamazight sources; African-led
  psychiatric-survivor and disability-rights archives.

## Track B — Indigenous Americas and Latin America

### B1. Before and across conquest: minds were not necessarily located in brains

**Proposed outline transition:** Begin with the body as a map of personhood. Ask how memory,
will, heat, breath, destiny, feeling, social relation, land, and spirit were joined before
asking which old term “means psychology.”

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **Nahua animating forces and the problem of translation.** Scholarship based on colonial
  texts describes *tonalli*, *teyolia/-yolia*, and *ihiyotl* in relation to heat, head,
  heart, breath, liver, vitality, memory, and emotion. The most important historical point
  is not a neat three-soul chart, but that Franciscan and Nahua authors worked in a contact
  zone where Christian “soul” language could change the concepts being recorded
  ([Klaus 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/10609164.2018.1527525);
  [Martínez González 2006, INAH](https://www.revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/cuicuilco/article/view/4314)).
- **Maya personhood should be researched on its own terms.** A comparative account of Maya
  animating entities notes both similarities and differences with Nahua frameworks; it is
  evidence against collapsing “Mesoamerican belief” into an Aztec template
  ([Martínez González 2007](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0185-25742007000200007&script=sci_arttext)).
- **The Cruz-Badianus manuscript as Indigenous medical knowledge in a colonial manuscript
  chain.** It was composed in Nahuatl by Martín de la Cruz and survives through translation
  and colonial book production. Use it to show Indigenous authorship and material/linguistic
  mediation at the same time
  ([“Cosmovision as Cognitive Technology”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12831609/)).
- **Colonial Andean “witchcraft” was also a Spanish interpretive frame.** Spanish conquest
  transformed the meanings and social position of Andean curers; European witch-hunt ideas
  shaped what colonizers thought they saw. This is a strong transition from translation to
  criminalization and resistance
  ([Silverblatt 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6362989/)).
- **Do not make *susto* an ancient pan-Latin diagnosis.** It appears in many later clinical
  and anthropological discussions, but its meanings, bodily experience, causation, and
  treatment vary by community and period. It should enter through local case studies and
  speakers, not a “culture-bound syndrome” box
  ([O’Nell 1975](https://doi.org/10.1525/eth.1975.3.1.02a00030)).

### B2. Settler colonialism: removal, schooling, land, language, gender, and distress

**Proposed outline transition:** Shift the unit of analysis from “an Indigenous patient” to
the policies that broke relations among children, families, nations, languages, and lands—and
to the communities that sustained or rebuilt those relations.

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **United States federal boarding schools as a mental-health history, not only an education
  history.** The Department of the Interior’s final investigation identifies 417 federal
  Indian boarding schools across 37 states or then-territories and frames the initiative
  around intergenerational effects and survivor healing
  ([U.S. Department of the Interior, July 30, 2024](https://www.doi.gov/pressreleases/secretary-haaland-announces-major-milestones-federal-indian-boarding-school)).
  The National Indian Boarding School Digital Archive is explicitly intended to support
  community-led truth-telling and healing, making it a preferred route to records and
  community context
  ([National Endowment for the Humanities](https://www.neh.gov/project/national-indian-boarding-school-digital-archive)).
- **Canada’s residential-school history must center survivor testimony and Nation-specific
  governance.** Use the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation and survivor-controlled
  routes for testimony; do not mine narratives because they are searchable. For research
  data, follow the relevant Nation’s protocols and OCAP® interpretation
  ([NCTR Archives](https://archives.nctr.ca/);
  [FNIGC](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/)).
- **Alaska Native boarding-school records can expose resistance as well as policy.** The
  Alaska State Archives points to a district-court file documenting a Tlingit mother’s
  attempt to free her child from the Presbyterian school at Sitka; that is a promising
  parent-agency node requiring direct file review
  ([Alaska State Archives](https://archives.alaska.gov/education/boarding.html)).
- **Two-Spirit history requires Nation-specific language and modern community authority.**
  A community-based study of urban Two-Spirit American Indian/Alaska Native adults links
  boarding-school experience to mental health and substance use, while also noting that
  diverse gender and sexual roles varied across Indigenous societies. It supports a modern
  lived-experience node, not a single precontact category
  ([Evans-Campbell et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5446670/)).
- **Land is not merely a “social determinant.”** A paper by Indigenous and settler scholars
  argues that land-based healing concerns relationships among self, family, community,
  nature, place, and culture, and critiques mental-health models that leave ongoing
  colonial occupation unexamined
  ([Redvers et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10002458/)).

**Dispute to preserve:** “Historical trauma” is influential but not a self-explanatory or
politically neutral label. A review of 30 publications found tension among evidence-based,
culturally relevant, and decolonizing research approaches. The outline should show what the
concept makes visible, what it can flatten, and why some Indigenous scholars foreground
ongoing colonial structures rather than a completed past event
([Gone 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24787478/)).

### B3. Latin American institutions: nation-building, race, and the asylum

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **Mexico: from colonial hospitals to La Castañeda.** A historical synthesis places early
  institutions in the Spanish colonial period and La Castañeda within Porfirian nation-
  building, later overcrowding, decentralization, and closure. Patient files, photographs,
  and Mexican historiography are needed before narrating lived experience
  ([Sacristán 2019](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61800/chapter-abstract/546403373)).
- **Brazil: imported theories were adapted, contested, and racialized.** Research in
  nineteenth-century Brazilian documents finds both moral-treatment and organicist
  approaches, with strong European influence
  ([Oliveira et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27402771/)). Juliano Moreira’s
  career offers a node on a Black Brazilian psychiatrist contesting racial-degeneration
  claims while building scientific psychiatry; the outline must retain the “lights and
  shadows” identified by later historians rather than turn him into an uncomplicated hero
  ([Oda 2021](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbp/a/xw3JNNjVrx8sMQxG6kTPvvG/?format=pdf&lang=en)).
- **Nise da Silveira and patient art.** Brazil’s Ministry of Health cultural center records
  that the Museum of Images of the Unconscious grew from painting and modeling workshops
  in the occupational-therapy section and opened in 1956. Public use of artists’ work still
  requires artist attribution, rights, diagnosis/privacy review, and avoidance of treating
  art as a transparent diagnostic image
  ([Centro Cultural do Ministério da Saúde](https://www.ccms.saude.gov.br/nisedasilveira/museu-de-imagens-do-inconsciente.php)).
- **Brazilian anti-asylum activism was a coalition.** Fiocruz locates a landmark national
  workers’ congress in Bauru in 1987 and the federal reform law in 2001; another Fiocruz
  account explicitly includes professionals, patients, and families in the movement. This
  supports an outline node on citizenship and social movements, not reform bestowed by a
  few psychiatrists
  ([Fiocruz 2022](https://www.epsjv.fiocruz.br/noticias/reportagem/35-anos-da-luta-antimanicomial-e-o-avanco-da-contrarreforma-psiquiatrica);
  [Fiocruz health-technicians observatory](https://www.observatorio.epsjv.fiocruz.br/os-tecnicos-em-saude-e-o-tratamento-psiquiatrico-humanizado)).
- **The Caracas Declaration as a regional exchange route.** Adopted Nov. 14, 1990, it called
  for community-based care, protection of dignity and civil rights, and a reduced central
  role for mental hospitals
  ([PAHO, primary text](https://www3.paho.org/hq/dmdocuments/2008/DECLARATIONOFCARACAS.pdf)).
  PAHO’s 30-year review says implementation was real but uneven and that large hospitals
  continued to consume scarce budgets
  ([PAHO 2021](https://www.paho.org/en/documents/30-years-caracas-declaration-situation-psychiatric-hospitals-latin-america-and-caribbean)).

### B4. Resistance, dictatorship, and collective coping

- **Mental-health work under Latin American dictatorships belongs in the political history
  of trauma and care.** Argentina’s National Library identifies a psychological-assistance
  team created for the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo in 1979 and frames its program around care
  for victims, repression, and exile. This is a research lead into the team’s records and
  testimony, not yet enough for a full narrative
  ([Biblioteca Nacional de Argentina](https://www.bn.gov.ar/agenda-cultural/salud-mental-en-dictadura-a-50-anos-del-golpe-de-1976)).
- **Collective action itself can be a coping and meaning-making history.** The official
  Mothers of Plaza de Mayo—Founding Line history says individual searches were transformed
  into a collective claim beginning April 30, 1977. The eventual page should distinguish
  organizational history, grief, political resistance, and clinical claims rather than
  diagnose participants retrospectively
  ([Madres de Plaza de Mayo Línea Fundadora](https://madresplazademayolf.org.ar/madres/historia/)).

**Missing voices**

- Indigenous-language community histories from Central and South America; Amazonian,
  Mapuche, Guaraní, Maya, Diné, Inuit, Métis, and hundreds of other Nation-specific accounts.
- Direct patient writing from La Castañeda and Latin American asylums; women, children,
  Afro-Latin American, queer, intellectually disabled, and rural people.
- How Indigenous and Afro-descendant midwives, herbalists, ritual specialists, mutual-aid
  networks, and families distinguished everyday trouble from conditions needing specialist
  intervention.
- Scholarship in Spanish, Portuguese, French, Dutch, Indigenous languages, and local
  archives not indexed in English-language databases.

## Track C — Caribbean: enslavement, creolization, asylum, migration, and decolonization

### C1. The Atlantic made new healing worlds under extreme violence

**Proposed outline transition:** The Caribbean should not appear as a late colonial appendix.
It is where Indigenous destruction and survival, forced African migration, European empire,
Asian indenture, Christianity, Islam, Hindu traditions, and new creole practices met under
plantation power.

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **Haitian Vodou as identity, explanation, and care—not a colorful obstacle to psychiatry.**
  A review describes Vodou as an African-derived Haitian tradition formed through encounter,
  with houngans and mambos among important care providers and with local idioms of distress
  that do not map cleanly onto North American categories
  ([Auguste and Rasmussen 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6880247/)). A mixed
  qualitative study found that Vodou explanations did not simply prevent use of biomedical
  care; access and perceived provider competence also mattered
  ([Khoury et al. 2012](https://hsrc.himmelfarb.gwu.edu/smhs_psych_facpubs/1605/)).
- **Obeah and related practices require an archive-of-power warning.** Colonial officials
  and slaveholders frequently used “obeah” as a broad criminalizing label for African-
  Caribbean ritual, healing, protection, and resistance. The eventual outline must use
  practitioner/community scholarship and original statutes alongside colonial descriptions;
  it should not reproduce the archive’s “superstition” vocabulary as neutral fact. Recent
  scholarship documents healing, protective, and justice-making practices rendered illegal
  under colonial laws, while legal history notes that “obeah” refers to varied African-
  derived spiritual practices rather than one religion
  ([Crosson 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/subterranean-unsettling-of-science-race-and-religion-obeah-petroleum-geology-and-risk-in-trinidad/AFA08683FFF28EAD8550D145D3B003D2);
  [Paton and Forde 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-law-and-religion/article/abs/obeah-vagrancy-and-the-boundaries-of-religious-freedom-analyzing-the-proscription-of-pretending-to-possess-supernatural-powers-in-the-anglophone-caribbean/11EEE1AD5948F72F423FE174FFE61F87)).
- **Disability and care under slavery.** Recent work argues that disabled and aged enslaved
  women performed essential care labor even as slaveowners devalued them; formerly enslaved
  narratives are rare and mediated through abolitionist publication. This belongs at the
  intersection of disability, labor, gender, and community survival
  ([Hunt-Kennedy 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0424.12761)).

### C2. The colonial asylum and a rare patient-authored challenge

- **Ann Pratt’s *Seven Months in the Kingston Lunatic Asylum* is a high-priority lived-
  experience anchor.** Margaret Jones’s study reports that Pratt’s 1860 pamphlet alleged
  physical and mental abuse of patients; the scandal, a patient death, and a manslaughter
  trial exposed conditions in Jamaica’s asylum. The primary pamphlet, authorship history,
  full trial/inquiry record, and Pratt’s own context must be checked before quotation
  ([Jones 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2880441/)).
- **The institution cannot be separated from slavery and emancipation.** Jones argues that
  asylum conditions carried a legacy of slavery while the scandal also provoked imperial
  review. This gives the outline a powerful transition: emancipation did not erase systems
  of coercion, racial hierarchy, or cheap custodial care
  ([Jones 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2880441/)).
- **Jamaican post-independence reform should be told as a contested decolonizing project.**
  Frederick Hickling describes a shift from British involuntary custodial policy toward
  community engagement, family therapy, short stays, and primary/secondary care. Because
  this is a leading participant’s interpretation, pair it with service-user, family, nursing,
  legal, and outcome evidence
  ([Hickling and Gibson 2012](https://www.mona.uwi.edu/fms/wimj/article/953)).

### C3. Caribbean ideas and people moved far beyond the islands

- **Frantz Fanon is an exchange-route chapter in one person:** Martinique, metropolitan
  French medical training, Algeria, anti-colonial struggle, and a global afterlife in
  psychiatry, psychology, political thought, and liberation movements. The outline should
  connect his clinical practice to—not replace it with—his political writing
  ([IMEC Fanon archive](https://imec-archives.com/archives/fonds/178FNN);
  [London School of Economics](https://www.lse.ac.uk/africa/Hub-for-African-Thought/Thinkers/Frantz-Fanon)).
- **Migration and “transcultural psychiatry” need Caribbean patient voices.** Caribbean
  migrants were frequently interpreted through British, French, Canadian, and U.S.
  psychiatric categories. A later research pass should connect migration, racism,
  diagnosis, family separation, and Caribbean clinicians such as Aggrey Burke without
  making the diaspora merely a receiving-country story.

**Regional cautions and gaps**

- Do not use “the Caribbean” as shorthand for Afro-Caribbean. Taíno and other Indigenous
  survival, Indo-Caribbean and Chinese-Caribbean communities, Maroon nations, mixed-language
  islands, and Dutch/French/Spanish territories require distinct research.
- Vodou knowledge may be initiatory, sacred, or context-bound. Public scholarship is not
  blanket permission to describe ceremonies or display objects.
- English-language Jamaican sources are unusually discoverable and can distort the outline.
  Haiti, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Dominican Republic, Trinidad and Tobago, Barbados, Guyana,
  Suriname, Guadeloupe, Martinique, Curaçao, and smaller island states need their own lanes.

## Track D — Oceania: Country, whānau, island networks, colonial removal, and resurgence

### D1. Personhood is relational, but the relations are not interchangeable

**Proposed outline transition:** Introduce Oceania through houses, land/sea, genealogy,
family, spirit, and collective balance—then immediately show that Māori, Aboriginal,
Torres Strait Islander, Samoan, Tongan, Fijian, Hawaiian, Marshallese, Papuan, and other
Pacific frameworks are not versions of one model.

**Topics and evidence anchors**

- **Te Whare Tapa Whā.** Tā Mason Durie’s model uses a wharenui to represent *taha wairua*,
  *taha hinengaro*, *taha tinana*, and *taha whānau*, with land as foundation. It is a
  twentieth-century Māori health model and a strong explanatory node for holistic policy
  and care—not a warrant to say “Māori always believed four walls”
  ([New Zealand Ministry of Health](https://www.health.govt.nz/maori-health/maori-health-models/te-whare-tapa-wha)).
- **Fonofale.** Fuimaono Karl Pulotu-Endemann developed the model from 1984, and a description
  appeared in a 1995 New Zealand health report after workshops with people from several
  Pacific communities. Family is foundation, culture the roof, and dimensions of health
  the posts, surrounded by environment, time, and context. Its Pan-Pacific use should not
  erase Nation-specific models
  ([New Zealand Ministry of Health](https://www.health.govt.nz/system/files/2011-12/pacific-peoples-and-menta-health-may08.pdf)).
- **Tongan communal metaphors in service design.** The *ūloa* model uses collective fishing
  to organize trust, harmony, working together, and spiritual as well as biomedical care;
  the study reports community/provider development and evaluation rather than an ancient
  clinical protocol
  ([Vaka et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9796273/)).
- **Samoan disaster response through *asiasiga*.** A Samoan-led intervention after a tsunami
  joined household visiting, pastoral workers, and mental-health supervision using Samoan
  concepts of self and wellbeing. It is a modern example of Indigenous design under disaster
  conditions
  ([Tamasese et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1039856219866321)).
- **Native Hawaiian resurgence and psychological self-determination.** A University of
  Hawaiʻi initiative describes belonging, kinship with ʻāina, kuleana, community, and
  decolonizing mental health as central to collective *mauli ola*. Use it as an openly
  published contemporary institutional/community voice, not a license to expose restricted
  knowledge
  ([Ka Malu a Waʻahila](https://kwaahila.org/)).

### D2. Settler institutions: asylums, removal, and records written in imported categories

- **Aotearoa New Zealand’s asylum system followed British legal and institutional models.**
  The Lunatics Ordinance 1846 enabled certification to jail or hospital, and provincial
  asylums followed. A historical review shows how public inquiries repeatedly shaped policy
  ([Brunton 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1351184/)). Research on Māori
  patients at Auckland Mental Hospital finds European categories, weak recording of tribal
  affiliation, attention to land rights in maintenance inquiries, cultural alienation, and
  poor outcomes—an explicit example of what institutional records reveal and erase
  ([Barry and Coleborne 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0957154X10390435)).
- **Australia’s Stolen Generations are a history of family separation, grief, survival, and
  healing.** *Bringing Them Home* drew on testimony about forced child removal and made 54
  recommendations; the Commission’s current presentation warns that the material may cause
  distress and may include people who have died
  ([Australian Human Rights Commission](https://humanrights.gov.au/bringing-them-home/index.html)).
  Those warnings and Indigenous-agency recommendations should shape any public page, excerpt,
  or image use
  ([report recommendations](https://humanrights.gov.au/bringing-them-home/the-report/report-recommendations.html)).
- **Fiji’s asylum history ties a small institution to an oceanic colonial network.** St.
  Giles began in 1884 as the Public Lunatic Asylum, one decade after British annexation;
  Jacqueline Leckie’s history uses patient and staff communities to connect local context
  with global colonial psychiatry
  ([Pacific Affairs review of *Colonizing Madness*](https://pacificaffairs.ubc.ca/book-reviews/colonizing-madness-asylum-and-community-in-fiji-by-jacqueline-leckie/)).
  A separate study shows how diagnoses of yaws, syphilis, and general paralysis could be
  transferred or confused, making diagnostic error itself a colonial-history topic
  ([Leckie 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/00223344.2019.1681262)).
- **Samoa inherited and repeatedly revised externally influenced mental-health policy.** A
  University of the South Pacific study identifies colonization, independence, and
  intergovernmental intervention as three policy-change episodes and argues for more
  domestically driven change
  ([Fadgen 2019](https://www.usp.ac.fj/journal-of-pacific-studies/all-issues/volume-39-issue-1-2019/explaining-policy-change-in-samoas-mental-health-system/)).

### D3. War, nuclear testing, displacement, climate, and the mind-place relationship

- **The Marshall Islands make displacement and environmental violence unavoidable mental-
  health history.** The U.S. Department of Energy states that U.S. nuclear testing ran from
  1946 to 1958 and that Castle Bravo fallout forced evacuation of Rongelap and Utrōk
  communities
  ([DOE Marshall Islands Program](https://www.energy.gov/ehss/marshall-islands-program);
  [Rongelap history](https://marshallislands.llnl.gov/affected-areas/rongelap-atoll)). The
  outline must seek Marshallese testimony, law, poetry, activism, and community archives;
  a U.S. technical history cannot carry the lived-experience narrative.
- **Climate change belongs as an extension of older displacement histories, not a sudden
  new “eco-anxiety” trend.** Future research should connect loss of land/sea access, burial
  places, food systems, language, sovereignty, and repeated relocation to mental wellbeing,
  using Pacific-led sources and avoiding universal symptom claims.
- **Banishment separated people from family across the Pacific.** A Pacific historian notes
  that colonial isolation policies for Hansen’s disease moved people from Hawaiʻi, Guåhan,
  Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Cook Islands, and elsewhere far from family; the source emphasizes
  separation as a central dread. This provides a careful bridge among disability, stigma,
  public health, institutional power, and relational wellbeing without equating Hansen’s
  disease with mental illness
  ([Salesa 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/00223344.2024.2326270)).

### D4. Contemporary resurgence and the limits of service-system evidence

- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander “social and emotional wellbeing” is broader than
  a psychiatric service category.** Australia’s national health-plan framework identifies
  culture as foundational and describes health across physical, social, emotional, and
  cultural wellbeing of individuals, families, and communities
  ([Australian Government 2021–2031 framework](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2021/12/national-aboriginal-and-torres-strait-islander-health-plan-2021-2031-framework_0.pdf)).
  The outline should prioritize Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations and
  local Nations when moving from national policy to practice.
- **Pacific services remain centralized and unevenly documented.** A scoping review across
  22 Pacific Island countries and territories found limited evidence from several places,
  heavy reliance on capital-city hospitals or units, and sparse outer-island services. It
  also identifies church, village, family, art, and post-disaster programs. Use it as a map
  of documented systems and gaps, not as proof of what communities do outside formal care
  ([Hewson et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13101603/)).

**Disputes to preserve**

- Holistic models can challenge individualizing biomedicine, but governments and clinicians
  can also turn living concepts into checklists. Compare model creators’ explanations,
  community application, and service-user experience.
- “Cultural adaptation” may mean community control, a translated imported therapy, or a
  superficial metaphor added to an unchanged service. The outline should label which one
  the evidence demonstrates.
- Institutional records can recover lives at scale, but a study of more than 4,000 Australian
  and New Zealand asylum records stresses ethics, access, record linkage, categories, and
  the constraints of the records themselves
  ([McCarthy et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5471986/)).

**Missing voices**

- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander psychiatric survivors, families, healers, disabled
  people, Stolen Generations elders, and community-controlled services in their own archives.
- Māori service users and whānau across institutional and deinstitutional eras; Māori nurses,
  tohunga, social workers, and legal advocates.
- Melanesian and Micronesian communities, especially Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands,
  Vanuatu, Kanaky/New Caledonia, Kiribati, Palau, Federated States of Micronesia, Nauru,
  and Marshallese-led nuclear archives.
- French colonial psychiatry and nuclear-testing history in Polynesia; Chamorro histories
  under Spanish, Japanese, and U.S. rule; wartime occupation and military-base effects.
- Everyday family and village care that never entered a clinic, hospital, or English-language
  research paper.

## Exchange routes the master outline should make visible

1. **Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan worlds:** Islamic scholarship, pilgrimage, trade,
   medicine, spirit traditions, and languages connected Africa internally and with Arabia,
   Persia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. This is a research gap in the present note and
   should be a dedicated cross-regional lane, not an aside to European colonization.
2. **The Atlantic slave trade:** forced migration moved people and healing traditions from
   West and Central Africa into the Caribbean and the Americas, where they were reassembled
   under plantation violence. Haitian Vodou’s African-derived and creolized history provides
   one evidence anchor, not a template for every African-diasporic practice
   ([Auguste and Rasmussen](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6880247/)).
3. **Mission and colonial law:** Christian missions, courts, police, prisons, schools, and
   medical departments criminalized or displaced practices and spread lunacy statutes and
   asylum forms across empires. Compare Ghana, East Africa, Jamaica, Aotearoa, Fiji, Samoa,
   and boarding-school systems rather than narrating each as isolated modernization.
4. **Professional and patient migration:** Caribbean clinicians and migrants in Britain;
   Fanon from Martinique through France to Algeria; African and Pacific medical trainees;
   and mobile colonial officials made psychiatric knowledge transregional. Fanon’s route is
   documented in French archival and university biographies; the other routes require
   dedicated prosopographic research
   ([IMEC](https://imec-archives.com/archives/fonds/178FNN);
   [LSE](https://www.lse.ac.uk/africa/Hub-for-African-Thought/Thinkers/Frantz-Fanon)).
5. **Anti-colonial and liberation networks:** Fanon, independence movements, Black
   consciousness, disability rights, Indigenous sovereignty, and liberation psychology
   changed what counted as a mental-health problem—from an individual defect to a relation
   of power, violence, land, race, and freedom.
6. **Deinstitutionalization networks:** Italian reform influenced Brazil; Latin American
   states articulated the 1990 Caracas Declaration; family and community models in Senegal,
   Jamaica, Brazil, and the Pacific moved through universities, WHO/PAHO, ministries, and
   activist coalitions. “Influence” must be proved in each case, not inferred from similar
   language.
7. **Global mental health and reverse travel:** mhGAP and donor frameworks traveled into
   African and Pacific systems, while Zimbabwe’s Friendship Bench later traveled outward.
   The outline should track who controlled adaptation, funding, data, and credit
   ([Chibanda et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4865009/)).
8. **Forced displacement and environmental change:** removal from Country, reservations,
   child removal, nuclear testing, island relocation, urbanization, war, and climate change
   altered relations central to wellbeing. Do not reduce these histories to individual
   “resilience.”

## Suggested master-outline transitions

- **From bodies to relations:** “Not every history put the mind inside the head. Some located
  memory, feeling, vitality, obligation, and danger across heart, breath, heat, land, kin,
  ancestors, and community.”
- **From archive to power:** “The people who built the record were often the people who
  confined the patient. Before we ask what a diagnosis meant, we have to ask who wrote it,
  who translated it, and whose words are missing.”
- **From conquest to classification:** “Empire did more than seize land. It created schools,
  prisons, hospitals, and categories for deciding who was civilized, educable, productive,
  dangerous, dependent, or insane.”
- **From emancipation/independence to inheritance:** “A flag can change faster than a law,
  a hospital, a professional hierarchy, or a file cabinet.”
- **From reformers to coalitions:** “Community care was not invented by one heroic doctor.
  Patients, relatives, nurses, attendants, grandmothers, activists, traditional healers,
  religious communities, and policymakers all contested what care should become.”
- **From culture to authority:** “The question is not only whether treatment is culturally
  adapted. It is who gets to define the culture, control the knowledge, judge the outcome,
  and decide what stays private.”
- **From trauma to continuity:** “Colonial harm is not only a wound handed down from the
  past. In many places, control of land, children, language, data, and services remains a
  present-tense question.”

## Candidate high-value article or chapter clusters

These are foundations for later outline decisions, not approved page titles.

1. **Where did people think the mind lived?** Nahua head/heart/liver concepts; Yorùbá
   distributed cognition; Māori and Pacific relational models; careful comparison without
   claiming equivalence.
2. **Before the psychiatrist: who cared for distress?** Families, healers, religious
   specialists, music/dance, herbs, dialogue, mutual aid, and the line between ordinary
   trouble and specialist care.
3. **The asylum follows the empire.** East and West Africa, Jamaica, Mexico, Brazil,
   Aotearoa, and Fiji; law/prison/hospital links; racial and labor governance.
4. **Can a colonial hospital be decolonized?** Fanon/Blida, Fann, Jamaica, and postcolonial
   Niger, with patient and staff voices wherever recoverable.
5. **Children removed, relationships broken.** U.S. boarding schools, Canadian residential
   schools, Australia’s Stolen Generations, language suppression, disability, gender, and
   community-led healing.
6. **A patient writes back: Ann Pratt and the Kingston asylum.** A tightly sourced
   lived-experience microhistory with authorship and archive mediation explicit.
7. **Art, benches, houses, and circles: when care changes form.** Nise da Silveira’s
   workshops, Zimbabwe’s Friendship Bench, Te Whare Tapa Whā, Fonofale, and community-
   controlled health—comparison of design, evidence, and authority.
8. **The right to leave the hospital.** Brazil’s anti-asylum movement, the Caracas
   Declaration, Ghanaian user organizing, Life Esidimeni, and the difference between
   deinstitutionalization and abandonment.
9. **Land, sea, and mental wellbeing.** Indigenous North America, Aboriginal Country,
   ʻāina, nuclear displacement in the Marshall Islands, and climate change; no generic
   “nature therapy” framing.
10. **When “culture” helps—and when it becomes a cage.** Fann’s cultural psychiatry,
    colonial stereotypes, culture-bound syndromes, modern adaptation, and Indigenous data
    sovereignty.

## Minimum evidence package before any of these nodes becomes an article

- One locally governed, community-authored, or creator/participant source where available.
- One primary source or material/archival object with provenance.
- One peer-reviewed historical synthesis by a regional specialist.
- One lived-experience, family, caregiver, nurse, healer, or survivor source—or an explicit
  archive-silence statement explaining why it could not be found.
- Original-language expertise and a named translation for all decisive terms.
- A colonial-mediation note for every colonial record.
- A cultural-protocol and rights review for every Indigenous story, image, recording,
  artifact, or ceremonial reference.
- A disagreement record where local and biomedical interpretations, or historians, differ.
- A “do not infer” field stating what the evidence cannot establish about prevalence,
  continuity, efficacy, belief, diagnosis, or subjective experience.

## Bottom-line recommendation for the actual outline

Do not place these regions in separate “non-Western beliefs” sidebars. Build them into the
chronological backbone at every hinge:

- personhood before specialist disciplines;
- colonial classification and confinement;
- enslavement, missionization, child removal, land loss, and resistance;
- independence with institutional inheritance;
- community, survivor, disability, and Indigenous-rights movements;
- contemporary debates over evidence, cultural authority, coercion, and data sovereignty.

The recurring editorial question should be: **Who had the power to define the problem, who
did the caring, whose explanation traveled, whose words survived, and who controls the story
now?**
