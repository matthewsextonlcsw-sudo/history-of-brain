# Outline foundation 01 — Prehistory and the ancient connected worlds of West Asia, North/Northeast Africa, and Mediterranean Europe

**Status:** Research support for Passes 3–10; not a final outline, article inventory, or public copy
**Geographic remit:** Prehistoric evidence in Africa, West Asia, and Europe; ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt and the Nile corridor, the Levant, Iran/Anatolia, Greece, Rome, Roman North Africa, and their exchange networks
**Working end point:** Late antiquity, roughly the fourth through sixth centuries CE, where the next foundation should take over
**Sources checked:** August 18, 2026

## Editorial recommendation

Do not call this span “the birth of mental health.” Build it as a sequence of changes in what the evidence lets us see:

1. material traces of survival, impairment, care, violence, and cranial intervention before writing;
2. the appearance of written records produced largely by literate specialists and institutions;
3. multiple, overlapping ways of locating thought, emotion, agency, suffering, and illness in bodies, relationships, gods, spirits, and moral orders;
4. increasingly systematized medical and philosophical models without a clean replacement of ritual healing;
5. imperial networks that moved people, texts, substances, cults, and techniques while preserving regional variation; and
6. late-antique reworkings of classical medicine, religious interpretation, introspection, charity, and institutions of care.

The public story should repeatedly show that “religious” and “medical,” “body” and “mind,” and “rational” and “magical” are modern sorting bins that often fit these sources badly. The University of Chicago’s overview of Mesopotamian medicine explicitly describes natural and supernatural explanations, material remedies, and ritual measures as parts of one field of practice; a study of Greek and Roman healing inscriptions likewise warns against assuming a definitive rational/irrational divide ([ISAC, pp. 76–77](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oimp36.pdf); [Iorio 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/23311983.2023.2286088)).

## Proposed chronological transitions and topic blocks

### A. Before written explanations: bodies, survival, and the limit of inference

**Approximate span:** Deep prehistory to the fourth millennium BCE, with later prehistoric comparators where useful.

**Proposed transition:** Begin not with a claimed belief about “madness,” but with the earliest evidence that injury, impairment, dependence, and skilled intervention formed part of social life.

**Topics to map**

- **Survival with impairment and the archaeology of care.** Healed injuries and survival with serious impairment can support a bounded inference that assistance or accommodation was likely; they cannot reveal a caregiver’s motive, the recipient’s feelings, or a society-wide ethic. The bioarchaeology-of-care method was designed to make each inferential step inspectable rather than treating survival alone as self-explanatory ([Tilley & Cameron 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29539577/)).
- **Neanderthal care as a case study, not an origin myth.** A peer-reviewed synthesis argues that Neanderthal health care was widespread and embedded in strong social bonds, while the Shanidar evidence includes survival with disabling injury; both are interpretations of material evidence, not access to Neanderthal emotion or intention ([Spikins et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/00438243.2018.1433060); [Pomeroy et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2019.207)).
- **Care and violence in the same record.** Shanidar contains evidence scholars read as both care (Shanidar 1) and interpersonal violence (the puncture injury of Shanidar 3), which prevents a sentimental “compassion steadily rises” narrative ([Pomeroy et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2019.207)).
- **Trepanation as physical evidence with an unknown indication.** Cranial surgery is attested from the Mesolithic and becomes widely represented in the Neolithic, but the purpose of particular operations—trauma treatment, pain relief, ritual, some other goal, or several goals—remains unresolved. It must not be presented as prehistoric psychiatric treatment without case-specific evidence ([Ramirez Rozzi & Froment 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-23914-1)).
- **Burial and symbolic behavior.** Burials, pigments, grave goods, and unusual treatment of bodies may illuminate social relationships or ritual action, but they do not by themselves establish beliefs about souls, grief, diagnosis, or an afterlife. This should be a recurring “How we know” lesson rather than a venue for confident mind-reading.

**Outline job:** Give the reader an evidence toolkit: what bones can show, what survival can imply, and what no prehistoric object can tell us directly about a person’s inner life.

### B. Writing changes the archive—not necessarily human experience

**Approximate span:** From the late fourth and third millennia BCE.

**Proposed transition:** Writing creates new evidence for named practitioners, categories, remedies, prayers, disputes, and literary accounts of suffering. It also creates a severe selection effect: scribes, temples, palaces, elite households, and durable media become much easier to see than ordinary speech, informal care, rural practice, or people excluded from literacy.

**Topics to map**

- invention and preservation of administrative, ritual, medical, literary, and legal genres;
- the difference between a prescription, diagnostic handbook, prayer, wisdom poem, law, letter, and burial object;
- compilation and copying across centuries: the date of a surviving manuscript is not automatically the date when all its contents originated;
- authorship, translation, damaged texts, uncertain readings, and the modern reconstruction of fragmentary corpora.

**Evidence anchor:** *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* now exists as a composite reconstructed from dozens of tablets and fragments; roughly three quarters of the poem has been reconstructed, and its protagonist’s first-person suffering cannot simply be equated with the biography of a demonstrably historical patient ([Lenzi 2023](https://doi.org/10.2307/jj.3919381)).

### C. Mesopotamian worlds: signs, relationships, ritual expertise, and material remedies

**Approximate span:** Third millennium BCE through the first millennium BCE.

**Proposed transition:** Written healing traditions reveal close observation and organized knowledge inside a cosmos where gods, ghosts, demons, wrongdoing, bodily processes, social loss, and material substances could all matter.

**Topics to map**

- **Plural practitioners.** The respective roles of the *asû* and *āšipu* remain debated; the archive supports neither a simple “doctor versus magician” split nor a claim that one displaced the other ([ISAC, p. 76](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oimp36.pdf)).
- **Diagnosis and prognosis as organized knowledge.** The diagnostic/prognostic series associated with Esagil-kīn-apli arranged observations into a long tablet series; a surviving Neo-Babylonian tablet links signs comparable to seizures or strokes with supernatural agency and ritual/material treatment ([ISAC, pp. 76–77](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oimp36.pdf)).
- **Distress in body, status, and relationship.** Babylonian medical and literary sources can link suffering with bodily signs, loss of property or standing, failed ritual relationships, divine displeasure, and frightening nocturnal experience. The point is not that Babylonians “already diagnosed depression,” but that their explanatory field joined social, bodily, and divine experience in ways modern diagnostic categories may split apart ([Geller 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1483204/); [Lenzi 2023](https://doi.org/10.2307/jj.3919381)).
- **First-person literary suffering.** Use *Ludlul* and the Babylonian Theodicy as evidence for learned ways of narrating apparently undeserved suffering, doubt, bodily affliction, and restoration—not as unfiltered patient charts. The electronic Babylonian Library supplies living critical editions, while Lenzi details their fragmentary reconstruction and scribal agenda ([eBL, *Ludlul*](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/2); [eBL, Babylonian Theodicy](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/1); [Lenzi 2023](https://doi.org/10.2307/jj.3919381)).
- **Altered states and possession.** Include ecstasy, dreams, seizures, frightening presences, and spirit agency as emic categories, but do not collapse them into one modern neuropsychiatric diagnosis.

**Outline job:** Replace the cartoon of “superstition before science” with a documented system of observation, causation, ritual, pharmacology, prognosis, social meaning, and expert authority.

### D. Egypt and the Nile worlds: heart, brain, body, family, and the dead

**Approximate span:** Early dynastic Egypt through the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, with Nubia/Kush treated separately where the evidence permits.

**Proposed transition:** Egyptian evidence joins anatomical observation, embodied models of thought and feeling, temple and household ritual, prescriptions, letters, and literature. It also exposes the danger of using Egypt as a substitute for all of Africa.

**Topics to map**

- **The injured brain as an observed structure.** The Edwin Smith Papyrus is a seventeenth-century BCE manuscript probably drawing on older material; it supports claims about careful trauma observation and Egyptian words for cranial structures, not a claim that Egyptian thought generally located mind in the brain ([National Library of Medicine](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html); [York & Steinberg 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0072-9752%2808%2902103-9)).
- **The heart and embodied mental life.** The Ebers tradition associates conditions modern writers may call affective with the heart, while Egyptian language and ritual gave the heart wide bodily, moral, intellectual, and emotional roles. Use the historical model on its own terms before comparing it with current neuroscience ([El-Assal 2014](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.13070860)).
- **Healing was not neatly divided into medicine and magic.** Prescriptions, spoken formulas, protective objects, and clinical observation can appear in the same medical culture; “rational” should describe a specific argument or method, not a whole papyrus or civilization ([The Metropolitan Museum of Art, *The Art of Medicine in Ancient Egypt*](https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/the-art-of-medicine-in-ancient-egypt)).
- **Personal and family strategies.** Letters to recently dead relatives ask for help with ill health and property disputes. They are unusually personal, include some of the earliest recorded messages from Egyptian women, and show coping and help-seeking within family and mortuary relationships outside a physician’s text ([UCL Digital Egypt](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/lettersdead.html)).
- **A literary crisis without retrospective diagnosis.** *The Debate between a Man and His Ba* presents a first-person narrator debating anguished life and uncertain death. James P. Allen reads the ending as an affirmation of life and explicitly rejects the inference that the narrator is contemplating suicide; that interpretation must be presented beside psychiatric readings that label the text depression or suicidality ([Allen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781316095881.007); [Ali & El-Mallakh 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/13811118.2021.1878079)).
- **Nubia and Kush as an evidence gap requiring their own lane.** Do not infer Nubian mental-life categories from Egyptian texts simply because Egypt and Nubia interacted. A Nubian-studies review describes an increasingly independent field in which new archaeology complicates the older picture of Nubia as subordinate to Egypt ([Smith 2014](https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/jaei/article/1099/galley/1094/download/)). The Meroitic script can be read phonetically, but only a small part of the language is understood, sharply limiting text-based reconstruction ([UCL Digital Egypt](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/nubia/mwriting.html)).

**Outline job:** Pair the famous medical papyri with domestic voices and the unequal Nile archive; distinguish “the brain was described” from “the brain was thought to be the mind.”

### E. Levantine and Iranian traditions: embodied selves, divine agency, suffering, and moral order

**Approximate span:** First millennium BCE into late antiquity.

**Proposed transition:** These traditions expand the outline beyond Greek philosophical vocabulary and show why English words such as “mind,” “soul,” “spirit,” and “emotion” cannot be mapped one-to-one onto ancient terms.

**Topics to map**

- **Hebrew Bible as a composite archive of embodied mental life.** *Rûaḥ* can mean wind, breath, or spirit and has contested anthropological, theological, ecstatic, and agency-related senses; bodily terms including heart, inner parts, bones, and kidneys participate in descriptions of distress and feeling ([Carlson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/1476993X241234908); [Van Hecke 2015](https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S1010-99192015000200009&script=sci_arttext)).
- **Laments, wisdom, narrative, and prayer.** Psalms, Job, Saul narratives, prophetic texts, and laments can anchor how suffering, fear, despair, altered behavior, blame, consolation, and communal response were narrated. They are literary and religious corpora with layered composition, not case histories, and should not be used to diagnose their characters.
- **Ancient Iranian healing in an ethical-cosmic framework.** Avestan traditions distinguish healing through the knife, plants, and sacred word, while later Zoroastrian sources closely connect care of body and soul. The relevant texts have difficult dating and transmission histories, so the outline must separate Avestan evidence from much later Middle Persian interpretation ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Health in Persia”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/health-in-persia-i/)).
- **Anatolia, the Levant, and multilingual imperial zones.** Add Hittite, Phoenician/Punic, Aramaic, Jewish, Iranian, and local cultic evidence as distinct research lanes; do not let the availability of Greek and Akkadian translations erase languages with thinner or harder archives.

**Outline job:** Make translation itself a central historical problem. A reader should learn why “soul,” “spirit,” “heart,” “breath,” “self,” and “mind” are interpretations, not interchangeable labels.

### F. Greek-speaking Mediterranean: systematization without disenchantment

**Approximate span:** Archaic and classical periods through the Hellenistic kingdoms.

**Proposed transition:** Greek medical and philosophical writers increasingly theorized brain, body, soul, perception, emotion, character, and causation, while sanctuary healing, household care, divination, and ritual remained active.

**Topics to map**

- **The Hippocratic corpus is plural, not one man’s system.** Use individual treatises with approximate dates and authorship uncertainty rather than attributing every claim to a historical Hippocrates.
- **“Sacred disease” as argument, not a secular revolution.** The treatise rejects healers who conceal inability behind divine explanations and offers a bodily account of seizures involving brain, breath, phlegm, and environment. It demonstrates one polemical naturalizing argument; it does not prove Greek society abandoned religious healing ([*On the Sacred Disease*, MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Hippocrates/sacred.html)).
- **Temples and patient narratives.** The Epidaurian *iamata* preserve about seventy sanctuary-curated healing stories naming pilgrims, origins, troubles, dreams, and cures. Their vividness offers access to hope and help-seeking, but their narrative amplification and promotional purpose mean they are neither neutral outcome data nor verbatim patient testimony ([Gregis 2017](https://doi.org/10.14277/2532-6848/Axon-1-2-17-08)).
- **Changing meanings of *psuchē*.** Greek “soul” expanded from Homeric usages toward roles in life, thought, perception, desire, emotion, planning, and moral character; Plato, Aristotle, Epicureans, and Stoics developed materially different theories rather than one “Greek view” ([Lorenz, “Ancient Theories of Soul”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ancient-soul/)).
- **Philosophy as care of self and emotion.** Plato’s divided soul, Aristotle’s embodied emotions, Epicurean freedom from disturbance, Stoic judgments and disciplines, Skeptical suspension, and later moral exercises belong in the coping/wellness path as historically situated practices—not as modern psychotherapy with ancient branding ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Ancient and Medieval Theories of Emotions”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-17th18th/LD1Background.html)).
- **Alexandria and Hellenistic anatomy.** Herophilus and Erasistratus advanced anatomical knowledge of brain and nerves that Galen later incorporated; the ethical, political, and evidentiary problems of human dissection must be included rather than treating knowledge as disembodied discovery ([Singer, “Galen,” §7.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/galen/)).

**Outline job:** Show several competing Greek projects—medical, philosophical, ritual, domestic, dramatic—not “the Greeks discovered the mind.”

### G. Roman and Hellenistic imperial worlds: a plural health marketplace, law, household practice, and synthesis

**Approximate span:** Late Republic through the third century CE.

**Proposed transition:** Empire connects local practices and Greek-language medicine across the Mediterranean and West Asia. Healing remains socially stratified and plural rather than becoming a unified Roman mental-health system.

**Topics to map**

- **Household and lay care.** Agricultural texts, papyri, ostraca, and writing tablets show that people learned remedies through family, friends, professionals, texts, and trial and error; professional medical literature is not the whole care system ([Draycott 2016](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004305564_019)).
- **Patients choosing among healers.** The Roman medical marketplace included physicians, temples, midwives, drug sellers, trainers, household experts, travel, and water cures. “Patient choice” must be bounded by wealth, legal status, gender, enslavement, military discipline, location, and availability ([Israelowich 2015](https://doi.org/10.1353/book.39295)).
- **Celsus and contested management of severe disturbance.** *De Medicina* 3.18 records disagreement over darkness, light, restraint, fear, fasting, drugs, conversation, and other management of *insania*. It is a learned Roman text about proposed treatments, not evidence that every described measure was routine or that patients consented ([Celsus, *De Medicina* 3.18](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:latinLit:phi0836.phi002.perseus-lat5:3.18/?right=perseus-eng2)).
- **Law, capacity, property, and responsibility.** Roman legal categories such as *furiosus* matter because they governed agency, guardianship, property, and culpability, but legal incapacity is not a clinical diagnosis. The later juristic tradition recognized differentiated legal consequences and, in some contexts, intervals of restored capacity; exact chronology and doctrine need a Roman-law specialist before public drafting ([Benincasa & Nowak 2024](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003463016-5); [McClintock 2025](https://hdl.handle.net/20.500.12070/74350)).
- **Galen’s synthesis and its internal tensions.** Galen related Plato’s rational, spirited, and desiderative soul parts to brain, heart, and liver, used bodily mixture to explain mental states, and left important soul-body questions unresolved. His work is a synthesis of medical and philosophical traditions, not a simple anticipation of modern localization ([Singer, “Galen,” §§7.1–7.2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/galen/)).
- **Patients inside medical writing.** Galen repeatedly grounded claims in clinical experience and case histories, but the patients’ words reach us selected, framed, and reported by Galen. This makes them valuable reported speech rather than direct testimony ([Mattern 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2024.41)).
- **Roman North Africa.** Inscriptions document Asclepius worship across sites including Carthage, Lambaesis, and Thuburbo Maius; this supports an African provincial lane inside Roman healing networks, not an assumption that Latin/Greek cult evidence exhausts local Punic, Libyan, or household practice ([Kleijwegt 1994](https://doi.org/10.3406/antaf.1994.1226)).

**Outline job:** Put household, sanctuary, legal, enslaved, military, philosophical, and professional settings on the same map, with power differences visible.

### H. Late antiquity: introspection, demons, ascetic disciplines, charity, and institutional change

**Approximate span:** Third through sixth centuries CE.

**Proposed transition:** Classical theories did not vanish when Christianity expanded. They were translated, contested, and recombined with biblical anthropology, monastic discipline, demonology, pastoral correspondence, and charitable institutions.

**Topics to map**

- **Augustine and inwardness.** Augustine developed accounts of attention, will, memory, self-awareness, and the mind’s relation to God in active dialogue with Platonism; present these as a late-antique Christian philosophy of mind, not a sudden discovery of an interior self ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Augustine”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/)).
- **Monastic categories of troubling thought.** Evagrian and related ascetic traditions systematized recurring thoughts and states—including anger, sadness, and *acedia*—inside a spiritual program. Do not translate *acedia* simply as depression; investigate its monastic setting, moral valence, bodily experience, and later transformations.
- **Natural and demonic explanations could coexist.** Sixth-century Gazan monastic letters interpret bodily and mental affliction through natural, demonic, biblical, and ascetic frames at once, offering unusually dialogic evidence while still reflecting a clerical archive ([Crislip 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/00209643221146234)).
- **Charitable institutions and the hospital debate.** Christian charitable facilities expanded between the fourth and seventh centuries, but their degree of medicalization and staffing varied; sources do not support a single date when a modern hospital was “invented.” Some institutions relied mainly on clerics, monks, and lay caregivers, while evidence for paid physicians is uneven ([Underwood 2018](https://doi.org/10.1525/sla.2018.2.3.342)).
- **Transition forward.** End with Greek, Syriac, Coptic, Latin, Hebrew/Aramaic, and Middle Persian traditions moving into Byzantine, Jewish, Christian, and later Arabic scholarly worlds. Do not frame the transition as classical knowledge disappearing and later being “rediscovered.”

**Outline job:** Hand the next period a set of living traditions—medical, philosophical, religious, institutional, domestic—not a fallen Roman shelf waiting for Europe to reopen it.

## Evidence anchors for the master outline

| Candidate anchor | What it can support | Evidence label | What it cannot support |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Shanidar injuries and survival | Bounded inference of assistance/accommodation and coexisting violence | Plausible to strongly supported, claim-specific | Motive, compassion as felt emotion, a universal Neanderthal norm |
| Healed trepanations | Cranial intervention and survival in particular remains | Documented | A psychiatric purpose without contextual evidence |
| Cuneiform diagnostic/prognostic tablets | Organized observation, prognosis, supernatural agency, treatment recipes | Documented | Modern diagnoses or the patient’s subjective experience |
| *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* | Learned first-person narration of bodily/social/divine suffering | Documented as literature; disputed as biography | A verbatim patient memoir or unqualified diagnosis |
| Edwin Smith Papyrus | Trauma observation and description of cranial structures | Documented | General Egyptian brain-centrism or modern neuroscience |
| Letters to the Dead | Family help-seeking around illness and misfortune; some women’s voices | Documented | Population prevalence or an unmediated account of emotion |
| *Debate between a Man and His Ba* | Literary crisis, anguish, debate over life/death/afterlife | Documented as literature; disputed interpretation | Certain suicidality, depression, or autobiography |
| Hebrew Bible bodily vocabulary | Culturally specific links among body terms, agency, distress, and feeling | Strongly supported within named texts | A single timeless “Hebrew psychology” |
| *On the Sacred Disease* | One naturalizing, brain-centered account of seizures and a polemic against ritual specialists | Documented | Greek secularization or universal Hippocratic practice |
| Epidaurian *iamata* | Sanctuary-curated stories of pilgrim suffering, dreams, hope, and cure | Documented | Neutral clinical outcomes or verbatim testimony |
| Celsus 3.18 | A learned catalogue of competing management strategies for *insania* | Documented | Frequency, patient consent, or universal Roman treatment |
| Galen’s works and reported patient speech | Medical-philosophical synthesis and clinician-framed clinical experience | Documented | Direct, representative patient voice |
| Roman legal texts on *furiosi* | Governance of capacity, property, guardianship, and liability | Documented, doctrine-specific | Clinical categories or everyday treatment |
| Late-antique monastic letters | Natural, demonic, bodily, and spiritual interpretations in one caregiving exchange | Documented | All Christian or popular practice |

## Exchange routes that must appear in the outline

- **Trade, migration, war, pilgrimage, and empire:** use these as mechanisms, not as proof that one culture “borrowed” a specific theory.
- **Egypt as a contact zone:** a study of archaic/classical circulation concludes that medical interaction between Greek and Babylonian spheres likely followed major contact routes, especially through Egypt, while direct lines of transmission remain difficult to prove ([Thomas 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17152173/)).
- **Hellenistic and Roman Egypt:** papyri and material culture show Greek, Roman, Egyptian, professional, domestic, magical, and religious strategies interacting rather than one simply replacing the others ([Draycott 2011](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/id/eprint/13064)).
- **Achaemenid and later Iranian networks:** Greek, Egyptian, Babylonian, Iranian, Anatolian, and Levantine experts moved within or alongside empires. Assertions of direct textual influence require linguistic and chronological evidence, not similarity alone.
- **Healing cult networks:** Asclepian practices spread across Greek, Anatolian, Roman, and North African sites, but local participation and translation need site-level evidence ([Gregis 2017](https://doi.org/10.14277/2532-6848/Axon-1-2-17-08); [Kleijwegt 1994](https://doi.org/10.3406/antaf.1994.1226)).
- **Textual afterlives:** Galenic, Platonic, Aristotelian, Stoic, biblical, Egyptian, Babylonian, and Iranian materials had different routes of preservation and translation. The outline should trace each route rather than using “the West” or “the East” as actors.

## Regional and terminological cautions

1. **Egypt is an African civilization and a Mediterranean/West Asian interlocutor; it is not a proxy for Africa.** Nubia, Kush, the Maghreb, and Saharan communities need distinct rows in the coverage matrix, including explicit “evidence insufficient” cells.
2. **“Mesopotamian,” “Egyptian,” “Hebrew,” “Greek,” “Roman,” and “Persian” each cover long periods and internally diverse populations.** Every public claim needs a bounded place, date, genre, and population.
3. **Ancient medical terms are not DSM/ICD diagnoses.** Modern comparisons may illuminate a symptom pattern but must be placed in a separate current-evidence layer.
4. **“Patient” is sometimes anachronistic.** Use “sufferer,” “pilgrim,” “client,” “supplicant,” “household member,” or the source’s own role where no clinical relationship is established.
5. **Do not use “primitive,” “superstitious,” “psychotic,” “hysterical,” or “possessed” as neutral narrator vocabulary.** When a source uses a historically consequential category, quote or translate it with context.
6. **Heart language may be anatomical, experiential, moral, relational, metaphorical, or several at once.** Do not silently translate every heart term as “mind.”
7. **Soul words are not equivalents.** Egyptian *ba/ka*, Akkadian terms, Hebrew *nepeš/rûaḥ/lēb*, Greek *psuchē/pneuma*, Latin *anima/animus/mens*, and Iranian terms require language-specific notes.
8. **Elite theory is not ordinary practice.** Plato, Galen, Augustine, and specialist handbooks need household, epigraphic, legal, material, and personal evidence beside them.
9. **Empire changes the archive.** Egyptian, Greek, Latin, and later Christian records may describe colonized or enslaved people without preserving their interpretations.
10. **“First” claims are especially hazardous.** Surviving text, earliest known object, earliest current find, earliest translated description, and actual historical origin are different claims.

## Missing voices and archive limits

- **Prehistory:** no direct verbal testimony; material survival may support care or accommodation but cannot report fear, hope, pain, stigma, or gratitude.
- **Cuneiform and papyrus archives:** literacy, scribal education, institutional collection, excavation history, antiquities markets, and preservation climate strongly shape survival. A text in first person may be literary role-playing rather than autobiography, as the *Ludlul* scholarship demonstrates ([Lenzi 2023](https://doi.org/10.2307/jj.3919381)).
- **Women:** women appear in some letters, healing inscriptions, household evidence, and reproductive care, but male-authored medical and philosophical texts dominate. UCL’s Letters to the Dead are valuable precisely because the corpus includes rare early messages from women ([UCL Digital Egypt](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/lettersdead.html)).
- **Enslaved people, children, rural poor, soldiers, and disabled people:** their bodies or legal status may be documented while their words are not. A separate lived-experience pass must search papyri, ostraca, curse tablets, epitaphs, military records, household texts, and archaeology before declaring them absent.
- **Sanctuary voices:** *iamata* and dedications preserve names and problems but were selected and displayed by cult institutions; treat them as mediated testimony ([Gregis 2017](https://doi.org/10.14277/2532-6848/Axon-1-2-17-08)).
- **Medical voices:** even when a physician reports a patient’s words, the clinician controls selection and framing. Galen offers important clinical speech, but it remains reported speech ([Mattern 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2024.41)).
- **Nubian, Punic, Libyan, Anatolian, and Iranian evidence:** language loss, partial decipherment, colonial excavation priorities, and reliance on outsider sources create unequal resolution. “No evidence found” must name the searched corpus and languages.

## Disputes to preserve rather than flatten

1. **Neanderthal care:** compassionate/prosocial care, practical mutual dependence, and the precise amount of assistance required remain matters of interpretation even when survival with impairment is physical evidence.
2. **Trepanation:** indications varied or are unknown; a hole in a skull is not proof of a belief about spirits or mental illness ([Ramirez Rozzi & Froment 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-23914-1)).
3. **Medicine versus magic:** the dichotomy often obscures how ancient systems joined observed signs, remedies, prayer, ritual, and divine agency ([ISAC](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oimp36.pdf)).
4. **Retrospective diagnosis:** identifying depression, OCD, psychosis, epilepsy, PTSD, or sleep paralysis in ancient descriptions may be a useful comparative exercise, but it is not equivalent to recovering an ancient diagnosis or subjectivity.
5. ***Dialogue between a Man and His Ba*:** suicide/depression readings conflict with Allen’s reading of a literary debate that ultimately affirms life ([Allen 2014](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781316095881.007); [Ali & El-Mallakh 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/13811118.2021.1878079)).
6. **The “Greek miracle”:** naturalistic argument did not begin all at once, did not end ritual healing, and developed amid long Mediterranean and West Asian contacts.
7. **Soul/body dualism:** neither “the Greeks were dualists” nor “ancient Hebrew thought was holistic” is adequate without named texts, dates, and competing interpretations.
8. **Who held power in the Roman medical marketplace:** patients could select among healers in some settings, but status and resources profoundly constrained choice; do not generalize an elite pilgrim’s agency to an enslaved or institutionalized person.
9. **The birth of the hospital:** late-antique Christian charity contributed major institutional changes, but medical staffing varied and the relationship to earlier Greek/Roman care is debated ([Underwood 2018](https://doi.org/10.1525/sla.2018.2.3.342); [Kapsambelis 2011](https://repository.uwtsd.ac.uk/id/eprint/403/)).

## Recommended handoff into the actual outline

The master outline should create at least these eight chronological nodes: **material lives before writing; first written healing archives; Mesopotamian systems; Egypt and the Nile worlds; Levantine/Iranian embodied selves; Greek systematizations and sanctuary healing; Roman imperial pluralism and Galenic synthesis; late-antique transformations**. Each node should receive the same subfields:

- ideas of mind, self, soul, agency, and body;
- descriptions of distress, difference, altered states, and emotion;
- healing, coping, ritual, household knowledge, and everyday care;
- control, law, property, exclusion, coercion, and institutional authority;
- first-person or near-first-person evidence;
- material evidence and its limits;
- exchange routes and translation;
- current-evidence comparison kept separate from historical explanation;
- missing populations and languages;
- named disputes and evidence labels.
