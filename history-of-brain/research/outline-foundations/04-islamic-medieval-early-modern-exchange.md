# Outline foundation 04 — late antiquity through early modern exchange

**Research status:** Provisional foundation for the chronological outline; source-backed but not yet claim-ledgered or editorially approved
**Coverage:** Approximately 300–1700 CE, with region-specific period labels
**Primary emphasis:** Islamic and Islamicate worlds; African, Asian, European, Jewish, Christian, and Indigenous exchanges
**Evidence note:** Links below were opened and checked on August 18, 2026. A link supports the sentence or paragraph immediately before it unless the note says otherwise.

## Editorial recommendation

Do **not** give this span one global chapter called “the medieval mind.” “Medieval” is a useful regional label for parts of Europe and, with explicit qualification, for some scholarship on Islamic history. It is not a universal stage through which China, India, Tibet, West Africa, Ethiopia, Japan, the Americas, and Europe passed together.

Build the outline as overlapping regional chronologies joined by exchange routes:

1. **Late-antique transformations, approximately 300–700** — eastern Roman/Byzantine, Sasanian, Syriac Christian, Jewish, North African Christian, Indian, Chinese, and early Buddhist worlds.
2. **New religious and translation ecologies, approximately 600–1000** — early Islam and the caliphates; Syriac- and Arabic-language scholarship; Tang and early Song China; Buddhist transmission across Asia; early Tibetan textual culture; continuing Sanskrit and regional medical traditions.
3. **Institutional and philosophical syntheses, approximately 900–1250** — bīmāristāns, court medicine, philosophical “sciences of the soul,” Jewish Arabic scholarship, Latin and Hebrew translations, Song China, Tibetan synthesis, South Asian medical change, and new religious disciplines of emotion.
4. **Regional consolidation and long-distance circulation, approximately 1200–1500** — Mamluk, Mongol and post-Mongol, later Song/Yuan/early Ming, Delhi Sultanate and other South Asian polities, West African Islamic scholarly networks, Ethiopian Christian traditions, European scholastic and vernacular worlds, and the growth of custodial institutions.
5. **Entangled early modernities, approximately 1450–1700** — Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming/Qing-transition, Sengoku/Tokugawa, Ethiopian and West African scholarly worlds; European print, anatomy, confessional conflict and state formation; Atlantic conquest, enslavement, missionization, and colonial institutions in the Americas and Africa.

The section’s through-line should be:

> Between late antiquity and 1700, people did not march from supernatural explanations to scientific ones. They combined, disputed, translated, institutionalized, spiritualized, naturalized, and repurposed many accounts of soul, body, brain, emotion, distress, and care. The most important change was not the victory of one model. It was the multiplication of learned systems, care settings, written genres, and routes along which knowledge and power travelled.

That is a **proposed synthesis**, not a sourced historical claim. Each regional chapter must test it.

## Working vocabulary

- **Islamic** should describe a religious teaching, practice, text, or institution when Islam is directly relevant.
- **Islamicate** can describe the larger societies and cultures historically shaped by Muslim rule but inhabited and made by Muslims, Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and others. Define the term on first public use.
- **Arabic-language medicine/philosophy** is usually more precise than “Arab medicine.” Arabic was a scholarly language used by people of many ethnicities and religions.
- **Greek–Syriac–Arabic**, **Arabic–Latin**, and **Arabic–Hebrew** name translation routes, not sealed civilizations. Translators were also editors, teachers, physicians, patrons, and authors.
- **Madness**, **melancholy**, **mania**, **possession**, **acedia**, **junun**, **nafs**, **qalb**, **rūḥ**, **xin**, **qing**, **rlung**, and similar terms should remain historically specific. Do not silently substitute a current diagnosis.
- **Patient** is acceptable when a source describes a person receiving care. Elsewhere prefer the source’s status term (“inmate,” “pauper,” “possessed person,” “petitioner,” “holy fool”) with humane explanation.
- Treat “psychiatry,” “psychology,” “psychotherapy,” “psychosomatic,” and “mental hospital” as modern analytic terms. Use them only when explicitly explaining the comparison.

## Proposed outline movement

### Transition from the ancient-world section: no clean fall, no sealed inheritance

Open by breaking the “Rome fell, knowledge vanished, Islam preserved it, Europe rediscovered it” story. Greek medical and philosophical texts remained active through late-antique Greek and Syriac communities; Christian and Jewish physicians participated in the later Arabic-language learned world; Abbasid patrons financed large translation programs; Arabic readers criticized and reorganized inherited material; and later Latin and Hebrew readers translated Arabic versions, commentaries, and original works.

The history of medicine was already multilingual before the Abbasid period. Hunayn ibn Ishaq’s ninth-century account of Galen translations lists Greek manuscripts, prior Syriac and Arabic versions, revisions, patrons, and differences in translator competence. It is unusually strong primary evidence that translation was collaborative textual criticism, not mechanical copying. [Primary translation and editorial context, National Library of Medicine/NCBI Bookshelf](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK622612/)

**Evidence label:** Documented for Hunayn’s account; strongly supported for the multilingual translation ecology.
**Caution:** Hunayn’s epistle is also a self-presentation of expertise and patronage. It does not represent every translator or reader.

### 1. Late antiquity remakes the inner person

#### 1A. Body, soul, and conversion

Late-antique Christian authors did not simply discard Greek psychology. They reworked terms for soul, passions, temptation, assent, memory, and self-transformation inside new relationships to God, community, sin, and salvation. A recent history of psychology describes a late-antique language of the *psychē/anima* as relational and especially important to accounts of conversion. [Youval Rotman, “The Relational Mind,” *History of Psychology*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33166166/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported as a scholarly interpretation.
**Outline use:** Establish conversion, confession, spiritual direction, prayer, and discipline as histories of attention and emotion—not as substitutes for medicine.

#### 1B. Acedia is not an old word for depression

Evagrius of Pontus described *acedia* as a troublesome “demonic thought” in the environment of Egyptian monastic asceticism; John Cassian helped transmit and transform this framework in Latin Christian communities. The category later changed substantially and was incorporated into teachings on vice and sloth. Scholars have compared it with boredom, despondency, and depression, but the comparison is not identity. [Andrew Crislip, “The Sin of Sloth or the Illness of the Demons?”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/12A802C25408AC574A610748B3D49C95/S0017816005000908a.pdf/sin_of_sloth_or_the_illness_of_the_demons_the_demon_of_acedia_in_early_christian_monasticism.pdf); [Siegfried Wenzel, “Acedia 700–1200”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/acedia-7001200/E2CFC90A06FC1CB8C7485EE0DE72F39C)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported.
**Translation caution:** “Sloth,” “listlessness,” “carelessness,” and “despondency” each capture only part of different historical uses.
**Modern-comparison caution:** Do not diagnose monks from a literary-spiritual genre.

#### 1C. Medicine and possession coexist

In Byzantine and other Christian settings, naturalistic, social, moral, and spirit explanations could coexist. A study of the emperor Zeno shows how later confessional polemic turned a poorly founded accusation into apparently repeated “fact,” a warning that hostile chronicles cannot be read as clinical records. [“Zeno, the epileptic emperor,” *Byzantine and Modern Greek Studies*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/978094F845E808EAD24CCEE312CBE140/S0307013100003335a.pdf/zeno-the-epileptic-emperor-historiography-and-polemics-as-sources-of-realia.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Documented for the polemical transmission studied; plausible as a larger outline warning.
**Outline use:** Create a recurring “Who wrote the case?” box for saints’ lives, chronicles, medical works, court records, and hostile biographies.

### 2. The Abbasid-era translation web: patrons, physicians, and languages

#### 2A. Baghdad as a funded knowledge ecology

From the mid-eighth through tenth centuries, Abbasid Baghdad became a major center for translating nonliterary Greek learning into Arabic. Dimitri Gutas’s influential synthesis argues that court, administrative, scholarly, and practical demand—not an abstract love of “preservation” alone—sustained the movement. [Dimitri Gutas, *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture*](https://www.routledge.com/Greek-Thought-Arabic-Culture-The-Graeco-Arabic-Translation-Movement-in/Gutas/p/book/9780415061339)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported for the movement and patronage; disputed in detail over scale, social causes, and what “almost all” translated works means.
**Do not say:** “The House of Wisdom translated all Greek knowledge.” The institution’s exact role is contested, and the work was distributed across patrons and circles.

#### 2B. Syriac Christian scholars were not a footnote

Hunayn’s record demonstrates that Syriac versions sometimes preceded or accompanied Arabic versions, and that the intended reader and commissioner mattered to the form of a translation. A recent study argues that Hunayn often translated medicine into Syriac because leading physicians read Syriac; those versions were not merely disposable stepping-stones to Arabic. [“Why Did Hunayn … Make Translations into Syriac?”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/65485C6EEAD14A90ED09E5B7D4930513/9783959941310c10_p363-388.pdf/why_did_hunayn_the_master_translator_into_arabicmake_translations_into_syriac_on_the_purpose_of_thesyriac_translations_of_hunayn_and_his_circle.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported.
**Representation rule:** Name Hunayn and other Christian translators as intellectual agents and clinicians, not “bridges” between supposedly self-contained Greek and Muslim civilizations.

#### 2C. Translation changes concepts

Aristotle’s *On the Soul* was translated fully into Arabic in the ninth century, while related short works and Hellenistic commentaries circulated unevenly. Arabic-language philosophers therefore inherited a layered and sometimes inconsistent Aristotle mixed with later commentary and Neoplatonic material. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Arabic and Islamic Psychology and Philosophy of Mind”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arabic-islamic-mind/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported.
**Outline use:** Show a manuscript route with forks, missing works, paraphrases, and corrections—not a single arrow labelled “Greek ideas.”

### 3. Sciences of the soul: mind, body, memory, and imagination

#### 3A. Al-Kindi, al-Razi, al-Farabi, and Ibn Sina

Arabic-language *falsafa* joined questions now separated into psychology, physiology, epistemology, ethics, and metaphysics. The *nafs* could be analyzed through sensation, common sense, imagination, estimation, memory, intellect, habit, and desire without ceasing to be a theological and ethical concern. Ibn Sina (Avicenna) developed a highly influential faculty psychology and argued for an immaterial, self-aware rational soul while assigning several perceptual and imaginative operations to internal senses associated with the brain. [SEP, “Arabic and Islamic Psychology and Philosophy of Mind”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arabic-islamic-mind/); [SEP, “Ibn Sina”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported.
**Caution:** “Islamic psychology” is a modern umbrella. Philosophers, theologians, physicians, jurists, mystics, and literary authors used overlapping but nonidentical models.

#### 3B. The floating person as an outline hook

Ibn Sina’s thought experiment often called the “Floating Man” asks a person to imagine being created fully formed, suspended, and deprived of sensory contact, then consider whether self-awareness would remain. Use it as an engaging entry into self-consciousness and embodiment, but source it from a reliable modern translation and explain that it argues about the soul’s self-awareness, not sensory deprivation, neuroscience, or a modern “brain in a vat.” [SEP, “Ibn Sina”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina/)

**Evidence label:** Documented text and strongly supported interpretation; exact English naming and philosophical implications require a critical translation.

#### 3C. Al-Balkhi and the danger of “first CBT” claims

Abu Zayd al-Balkhi’s *Masalih al-Abdan wa al-Anfus* treats care of body and psyche in paired sections, classifies forms of fear, anger, sadness, and intrusive or obsessive thought, and recommends prevention and forms of self-management. An open translation exists, but its title explicitly frames the work as “the Cognitive Behavior Therapy of a Ninth Century Physician,” and the translator repeatedly maps the text onto twentieth-century diagnoses. [Open translation on JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvh4zfhk)

**Evidence label:** Documented for the translated text’s topics; disputed for “CBT,” “neurosis,” “OCD,” or “first psychologist” equivalence.
**Outline wording:** “Al-Balkhi described techniques that a modern reader may compare with cognitive and behavioral coping strategies.” Follow immediately with the differences in cosmology, genre, audience, diagnosis, and evidence.

### 4. Distress is medically plural

#### 4A. Galenic bodies and treatable disturbance

Galenic medicine linked mental activity and emotion to bodily processes and treated disturbances through qualities, humors, regimen, drugs, evacuation, sleep, bathing, and environment. Arabic-language physicians elaborated categories including melancholy, mania-like states, phrenitis, and love-sickness. Michael Dols’s foundational study shows both the strength of physiological explanations and the simultaneous presence of jinn, the evil eye, religious healing, saints, family care, law, prison, and tolerated social roles. [Michael W. Dols, “Insanity and Its Treatment in Islamic Society”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/68093D50DC7C71DA81376E896F8F0F05/S0025727300046287a.pdf/insanity-and-its-treatment-in-islamic-society1.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported as a model of pluralism.
**Do not frame:** medicine versus superstition, or tolerant Islam versus cruel Christianity. Both binaries erase variation and harm.

#### 4B. Prophetic medicine and learned medicine

The National Library of Medicine distinguishes the Greek-based learned traditions associated with physicians such as al-Razi and Ibn Sina from the genre later called Prophetic Medicine, whose compilers drew therapies and conduct from reports about Muhammad and Islamic scripture. The genres could disagree, overlap, and address different audiences. [NLM, “Prophetic Medicine”](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/hmd/arabic/prophetic_med1.html)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported for the literary genre.
**Caution:** Do not turn a textual distinction into a claim that ordinary people chose only one system.

#### 4C. Sufi disciplines of the self

Al-Ghazali joined philosophical moral psychology, Sunni theology, and Sufi practices in a program of disciplining the self, moderating anger and desire through reason, habituation, worship, and ethical training. His *Deliverance from Error* recounts a crisis that interrupted his public career, but scholars treat the work as an intellectual-spiritual self-presentation, not an unfiltered clinical autobiography. [SEP, “al-Ghazali”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/al-ghazali/); [W. Montgomery Watt translation and introduction](https://www.ghazali.org/books/gz-wat-del.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Documented for al-Ghazali’s account and teachings; disputed for a modern medical diagnosis and for how literally the retrospective narrative maps his experience.
**Lived-experience use:** A rare elite first-person-adjacent account of distress, doubt, bodily incapacity, vocation, and recovery—clearly labeled as a crafted religious text.

### 5. Hospitals, households, shrines, courts, and streets

#### 5A. Bimaristan does not mean “psychiatric hospital”

Hospitals in Islamic societies became important sites of charitable care, medical practice, teaching, and state or elite patronage. Dols found strong evidence that the Mansuri Hospital in Cairo, founded in 1284, provided for people judged insane, drawing on its endowment deed, descriptions, and architecture; Ibn Jubayr’s 1183 travel account described confined people being examined daily. [Dols, pp. 2–3](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/68093D50DC7C71DA81376E896F8F0F05/S0025727300046287a.pdf/insanity-and-its-treatment-in-islamic-society1.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Documented for named institutions and accounts; plausible but not universal for ward arrangements elsewhere.
**Terminology:** *Bimaristan/maristan* means hospital or place of the sick in a broad sense. Do not translate it automatically as asylum.

#### 5B. “First psychiatric ward” is not ready for publication

Popular and some recent medical-history articles credit al-Razi with founding the “first psychiatric ward” in Baghdad. The origin and organization of the earliest Abbasid hospitals are much less secure than that slogan implies. The outline should treat the claim as a historiographic case study and require contemporary or near-contemporary institutional evidence before using “first.” [Dols, “The Origins of the Islamic Hospital: Myth and Reality,” bibliographic record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/The-origins-of-the-Islamic-hospital-%3A-myth-and-reality/oclc/33825752); [Ahmed Ragab, *The Medieval Islamic Hospital*, publisher record](https://www.cambridge.org/us/universitypress/subjects/history/history-medicine/medieval-islamic-hospital-medicine-religion-and-charity)

**Evidence label:** Disputed/unknown.
**Reject for now:** “Al-Razi founded the world’s first psychiatric ward.”

#### 5C. Care could also be confinement

Sources describe daily care, food, drugs, baths, music, and visitors, but also iron windows, stocks, chains, prisons, public viewing, and political detention. A 1352 chronicle preserved by al-Maqrizi describes a man whose royal claim was interpreted through recurring madness and political danger; he was whipped, stocked, chained, interrogated, and imprisoned. The record contains his mother’s reported testimony but not his own unmediated account. [Dols, pp. 1–2](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/68093D50DC7C71DA81376E896F8F0F05/S0025727300046287a.pdf/insanity-and-its-treatment-in-islamic-society1.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Documented reported case; unknown as a general rate of treatment.
**Empathy rule:** Show the care/control tension within the same institution rather than assigning whole societies to “humane” or “barbaric” columns.

#### 5D. Music and gardens: evidence, not tourism copy

Medical texts recommended music; Dols cites evidence for musicians in hospital endowments and later travel descriptions, including performances at Cairo’s Mansuri Hospital and Ottoman Edirne. The sources do not establish that every hospital used music, that particular musical modes treated particular diagnoses, or that outcomes were effective by modern standards. [Dols, pp. 7–9](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/68093D50DC7C71DA81376E896F8F0F05/S0025727300046287a.pdf/insanity-and-its-treatment-in-islamic-society1.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Documented in selected prescriptions, endowments, and descriptions; unknown efficacy and prevalence.
**Asset idea:** An annotated hospital plan distinguishing architecture, endowment language, traveler observation, and modern reconstruction.

#### 5E. Most care was not institutional

Hospitals are visible because buildings, endowments, and elite accounts survive. Dols explicitly warns that hospital history does not explain people living outside hospitals. Family support, charity, shrines, saints, wandering, work, courts, incarceration, and tolerated roles such as wise or holy fools also shaped life. His sources suggest some harmless unusual behavior could be accommodated, even while people were mocked, fettered, or dependent on charity. [Dols, pp. 9–13](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/68093D50DC7C71DA81376E896F8F0F05/S0025727300046287a.pdf/insanity-and-its-treatment-in-islamic-society1.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported for plurality; prevalence unknown.
**Coverage rule:** Every institutional page needs a “Beyond the walls” section.

### 6. Jewish thought and translation inside Arabic, Hebrew, and Latin worlds

#### 6A. Maimonides: medicine, philosophy, law, and emotion

Moses Maimonides wrote medical works in Arabic while serving as a physician in Ayyubid Egypt. In *On the Regimen of Health* (1193–1198), written for al-Malik al-Afdal, he advised attention to the “movements of the psyche,” anxiety, grief, obsessions, fear, loss of enjoyment, and what the consulted translation calls hypochondria and morbid melancholy. He located some remedies in practical philosophy and religious law as well as medical regimen. [Curated text and translation, Posen Library](https://www.posenlibrary.com/entry/regimen-health)

**Evidence label:** Documented in a named translation.
**Translation caution:** Preserve the Arabic term behind “psyche,” “obsession,” “depression,” and “morbid melancholy” before using any quotation publicly. Do not claim he “discovered psychosomatic medicine.”

#### 6B. Translation was multidirectional

Around 1199, Jewish translators in southern France produced Hebrew versions of Galen’s *Tegni* from both Constantine the African’s Latin version and Hunayn’s Arabic version with Ibn Ridwan’s commentary. The translations met the needs of Jewish physicians reading Hebrew and show Arabic, Latin, and Hebrew scholarly worlds interacting within one region. [Freudenthal and Fontaine, *Arabic Sciences and Philosophy*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/arabic-sciences-and-philosophy/article/abs/philosophy-and-medicine-in-jewish-provence-anno-1199-samuel-ibn-tibbon-and-doeg-the-edomite-translating-galens-tegni/A63BCF05F22839442E1DDCC0AEE2E064)

**Evidence label:** Documented.
**Outline use:** Follow a single work through Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Latin, and Hebrew transformations.

### 7. Europe: layered explanations, law, charity, and coercion

#### 7A. Do not write a “demonic dark age”

Latin Christian Europe contained humoral medicine, monastic and lay care, legal guardianship, saints’ shrines, prayer, exorcism, domestic care, hospitals, and punishment. Theories of emotion drew on Augustine, Aristotle, Avicenna, monastic psychology, humors, bodily spirits, will, and moral theology. Aquinas’s thirteenth-century taxonomy treated passions as psychosomatic movements involving evaluation, bodily change, and action. [SEP, “Medieval Theories of the Emotions”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/medieval-emotions/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported.
**Outline use:** Pair religious and medical records from the same location rather than making a civilizational comparison.

#### 7B. Bethlem begins as one thing and becomes another

The Priory of St Mary of Bethlehem was founded in London in 1247. It is first referred to as caring for “insane” patients in 1403. Therefore the public outline may call Bethlem an institution with a continuous later mental-health history, but not a psychiatric hospital founded in 1247. [Bethlem Museum of the Mind](https://museumofthemind.org.uk/projects/european-journeys/asylums/bethlem-royal-hospital)

**Evidence label:** Documented institutional chronology.
**Outline use:** A transformation timeline: religious house → charitable hospital → specialized custody/care → public spectacle and reform debates in later periods.

#### 7C. Witch hunts were not mass psychiatric treatment

Older histories of psychiatry claimed that European witch prosecutions were chiefly persecution of mentally ill people. Historical reviews find that this psychopathological interpretation selected evidence and exaggerated mental disorder’s role. [Thomas Schoeneman, “The Role of Mental Illness in the European Witch Hunts”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227913313_The_role_of_mental_illness_in_the_European_witch_hunts_of_the_sixteenth_and_seventeenth_centuries_An_assessment); [“Criticisms of the psychopathological interpretation of witch hunts”](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/pdf/10.1176/ajp.139.8.1028?download=true)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported rejection of the simple thesis.
**What belongs instead:** Witchcraft and possession as legal, religious, gendered, political, and community histories that sometimes intersected with distress—without retrodiagnosing defendants or erasing torture and coercion.

### 8. South, Central, East, and Southeast Asian continuities and exchanges

#### 8A. South Asia: Sanskritic, Buddhist, Persianate, and local medicine

Do not make South Asia pause between “ancient Ayurveda” and colonial psychiatry. Vagbhatta’s *Astangahrdaya* is generally dated to the seventh century, and Sanskrit medical writing continued to change in early modern settings. Dominik Wujastyk demonstrates creativity among seventeenth-century Sanskrit authors and the limits of European travelers’ descriptions of Indian medicine. [“Change and Creativity in Early Modern Indian Medical Thought”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2633698/)

Persian became an important medical language in South Asia, and works in the Greco-Arabic tradition were adapted for that environment; Muhammad Akbar Arzani’s early eighteenth-century *Mizan-i Tibb* falls just beyond this section but offers a strong transition into the next period. [Neil Krishan Aggarwal, “On the Diseases of the Head”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24572801/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported for continued textual change; source-specific for individual works.
**Terminology caution:** Avoid equating *bhutavidya* with modern psychiatry or psychotherapy. “Unani” as a stable system was also reshaped in later colonial and nationalist contexts.

#### 8B. Buddhist analyses of mind travel and change

Indian Buddhist traditions analyzed consciousness, feeling, attention, desire, affliction, no-self, and disciplined practice over many centuries. Yogacara theories of consciousness influenced later traditions in China, Japan, and Tibet; Chan developed in China from the sixth century and was later adapted elsewhere in East Asia. [SEP, “Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2011/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/); [SEP, “Yogacara”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/yogacara/); [SEP, “Chan Buddhism”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2019/entries/buddhism-chan/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported.
**Outline rule:** Meditation belongs as a historical discipline of attention, ethics, embodiment, community, and liberation. Do not market it as timeless “ancient mindfulness.”

#### 8C. Tibet shows exchange without a single source

Medical manuscripts from Dunhuang, sealed by the early eleventh century, contain Tibetan, Chinese, Indic, Turkic, Persian, and possibly Arabic traces. Ronit Yoeli-Tlalim finds Indic and Chinese foundations with some western/Galenic inputs and explicitly rejects a simple “Galen in Tibet” origin story. [“Re-visiting ‘Galen in Tibet’”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/medical-history/article/revisiting-galen-in-tibet1/4ECE5969EF74F5766FC2861BCC07BC38)

**Evidence label:** Documented manuscript features; disputed pathways and degrees of influence.
**Asset idea:** A Dunhuang route map where each arrow is labeled “text,” “term,” “drug,” or “technique” and confidence is visible.

#### 8D. China and Japan need their own conceptual center

In Chinese philosophy, *xin* is often translated “heart-mind” because it carries cognitive, affective, intentional, and bodily associations. *Qing* also changed meanings across periods and cannot always be translated simply as “emotion.” Song-Ming thinkers developed influential accounts of heart-mind, nature, moral cultivation, and feeling; Japanese traditions adapted Confucian, Buddhist, and local frameworks rather than merely importing them unchanged. [SEP, “Mind (Heart-Mind) in Chinese Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-mind/); [SEP, “The Emotions in Early Chinese Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-chinese/); [SEP, “Song-Ming Confucianism”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2020/entries/song-ming-confucianism/); [SEP, “Japanese Confucian Philosophy”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-confucian/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported.
**Translation caution:** Do not imply that “the Chinese mind was located in the heart” as a single timeless belief. Separate philosophical, medical, religious, literary, and popular uses across periods.

### 9. African intellectual and healing worlds

#### 9A. North Africa is central, not peripheral

Fustat/Cairo, the Maghreb, al-Andalus, and trans-Saharan routes connect the medical, philosophical, Jewish, Christian, and Islamic histories above. Figures such as Ishaq ibn Imran, Maimonides, Ibn Rushd, and later travelers must be located in African and Mediterranean geographies, not automatically filed under “Middle East” or “Europe.”

**Evidence label:** This is an editorial geography rule. Each biographical claim needs its own source.

#### 9B. Timbuktu manuscripts make written African medicine visible

Timbuktu was a major West African center of Islamic learning connected through trade, scholarship, and pilgrimage. A digitized seventeenth-/eighteenth-century manuscript, *Curing Diseases and Defects Both Apparent and Hidden*, combines diagnosis and animal, plant, and mineral medicines with prayers, Quranic verses, and amulets. [Library of Congress object record](https://www.loc.gov/item/2021667145/); [Islamic Manuscripts from Mali collection](https://www.loc.gov/collections/islamic-manuscripts-from-mali/about-this-collection/)

**Evidence label:** Documented object and collection context.
**Caution:** A learned Arabic manuscript does not represent all West African healing, patient experience, or practice. Its exact composition date is catalogued broadly, and the English title was supplied by library staff.

#### 9C. Ethiopian manuscripts refuse the medicine/magic split

Ethiopian Christian healing scrolls and manuscripts join prayer, images, protective writing, plants, and accounts of spiritual danger. Wellcome’s catalogue describes a group of scrolls as “magico-medical” and notes that the category is ethnomedical rather than medicine in a narrow modern sense. [Wellcome Collection catalogue](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/bvfavs4n/items); [British Library catalogue, an eighteenth-century Ethiopian therapeutic manuscript](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-003387073)

**Evidence label:** Documented objects; community meaning and use require Ethiopian scholarship and, where appropriate, cultural consultation.
**Ethical caution:** Protective objects may be personal or sacred. Digitization does not automatically authorize decorative reuse.

#### 9D. Atlantic Africa is an exchange history, not only a European encounter

For West-Central Africa from 1500 onward, surviving sources include African knowledge filtered through botanical texts, missionaries, administrators, and travelers. Kalle Kananoja emphasizes that local African knowledge shaped healing encounters and that colonial writers’ symptom descriptions often do not permit modern diagnosis. [*Healing Knowledge in Atlantic Africa*, chapter summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/0E6627303E0A532A5C9A0232F5DD2D91/9781108491259c6_155-186.pdf/treating-their-symptoms.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported methodological warning.
**Archive caution:** European-language records are not neutral windows onto African patients or healers.

### 10. Early modern empires and colonial institutions

#### 10A. Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal worlds

Outline medicine, Sufi practice, family care, shrines, court patronage, charitable complexes, and legal control together. The Bayezid II complex at Edirne, founded in 1488, is a useful material anchor for hospital, medical-school, food-aid, religious, architectural, and sensory histories; claims about diagnosis-specific musical modes require primary endowment and practice evidence, not modern heritage-site repetition. [Cambridge/BJPsych institutional vignette](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/kulliye-of-sultan-bayezid-ii-psychiatry-in-history/B7502C3D97BFF8C121495695E6480FAC); [Dols’s more source-critical discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/68093D50DC7C71DA81376E896F8F0F05/S0025727300046287a.pdf/insanity-and-its-treatment-in-islamic-society1.pdf)

**Evidence label:** Documented complex and selected music provisions; disputed prevalence, precise treatment protocol, and outcomes.

#### 10B. Colonial Mexico: charity and governance together

The Hospital de San Hipolito in Mexico City, founded in 1567, provided charitable and basic medical care to people categorized as *pobres dementes*. Christina Ramos argues that it also reproduced Catholic charity and Habsburg paternal authority, making it an imperfect instrument of colonial governance. [Ramos, “Caring for pobres dementes”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/abs/caring-for-pobres-dementes-madness-colonization-and-the-hospital-de-san-hipolito-in-mexico-city-15671700/14B65398C072BF78A041BE18472392DE)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported by archival research.
**First-claim caution:** It “holds a claim” to being the first specialized hospital for mentally disturbed people in the Americas; do not flatten that into an uncontested world-first.

#### 10C. Nahua concepts did not simply survive unchanged or disappear

Sixteenth-century Nahua and Franciscan contact generated translations and conceptual change around body, heat, wind, heart, animating forces, and soul. Scholarship on *-yolia/teyolia* shows that even concepts commonly described as preconquest “souls” may be refracted through Christian colonial texts and later scholarly expectations. [Bultman, “Winds, heart, and heat”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10609164.2018.1527525); [“De(re)constructing the Nahua ‘Soul’”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ancient-mesoamerica/article/abs/animating-principle-in-confrontation-with-christianity-dereconstructing-the-nahua-soul/9151B77ACEBDAFDF779D42B50876FCCD)

**Evidence label:** Disputed/actively interpreted.
**Outline rule:** Put Indigenous-language evidence before Spanish diagnostic labels, and make colonial mediation visible.

### 11. Print, anatomy, and the mechanical body in Europe

#### 11A. Anatomy changes what can be pictured—not instantly what everyone believes

Andreas Vesalius’s *De humani corporis fabrica* (1543) transformed anatomical illustration and the authority of direct dissection in learned European medicine. It did not by itself create modern neuroscience or eliminate Galenic physiology. [NLM overview of clinical history](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK458/); [review of Vesalius and Willis brain illustration](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7966481/)

**Evidence label:** Strongly supported for anatomy and illustration; reject triumphalist “modern science begins” language.

#### 11B. Melancholy expands through print

Robert Burton’s *Anatomy of Melancholy* (1621) is an enormous literary, theological, medical, philosophical, and political compilation. German Berrios warns that “anatomy” was metaphorical and “melancholy” polysemous; the book should not be treated as an early textbook of depression. [Berrios, “The Anatomy of Melancholy by Robert Burton”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/anatomy-of-melancholy-by-robert-burton/B2EB32F1A0A439B4964459C53BBB0429); [1621 public-domain text, University of Michigan](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A17310.0001.001?view=toc)

**Evidence label:** Documented publication; strongly supported semantic caution.
**Lived-experience caution:** Burton’s “I write of melancholy by being busy to avoid melancholy” is memorable self-positioning, not enough to diagnose him.

#### 11C. Descartes is a transition, not a lone inventor of dualism

In *Passions of the Soul* (1649), Rene Descartes described passions as arising through bodily processes and brain activity while also maintaining a distinct thinking mind. He proposed the pineal gland as the “principal seat” involved in mind-body interaction, using anatomical assumptions that were wrong even by some knowledge available in his own time. [SEP, “Descartes’ Ethics”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-ethics/); [SEP, “Descartes and the Pineal Gland”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pineal-gland/)

**Evidence label:** Documented theory; strongly supported contextual criticism.
**Outline use:** End with a question, not a victory: what changes when the body is described as a machine but experience still belongs to a soul?

### 12. Lived experience: recover voices without pretending the archive is neutral

Build a recurring evidence strand with four clearly marked levels:

1. **Direct or near-direct self-expression:** letters, petitions, autobiographical spiritual writing, poems, testimony, marginalia.
2. **Reported speech:** relatives, healers, judges, chroniclers, or visitors quote or paraphrase a person.
3. **Institutional description:** admission, endowment, court, prison, hospital, or welfare record categorizes a person.
4. **Modern reconstruction:** a historian infers experience from context and material evidence.

A newly studied group of letters sent to Elizabethan authorities between about 1570 and 1600 was later organized and labelled as “mad” by secretaries, antiquarians, and cataloguers. Jonathan Willis argues that asking whether the writers were “really mad” is less productive than reading their letters as historically situated expressions of distress, need, reputation, imprisonment, religion, and appeals for care. [Willis, “your poore distressed suppliant”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/transactions-of-the-royal-historical-society/article/your-poore-distressed-suppliant-madness-emotion-and-the-archive-in-early-modern-england/0A678BA1593C9B2E037B8021B9D6881B)

**Evidence label:** Documented corpus and strong methodological argument.
**Outline use:** Pair one petition with the later catalogue label to show how archives manufacture “patient” identities.

Other candidate lived-experience anchors:

- [al-Ghazali’s crafted account](https://www.ghazali.org/books/gz-wat-del.pdf) of crisis and vocational change;
- [al-Maqrizi’s chronicle case](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0025727300046287), explicitly marked as third-party and political;
- [Maimonides’s advice to a named elite patient](https://www.posenlibrary.com/entry/regimen-health), which preserves the physician’s voice far better than the patient’s;
- hospital endowment and traveler descriptions, which reveal provision and observation rather than interior experience;
- [Timbuktu](https://www.loc.gov/item/2021667145/) and [Ethiopian](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/bvfavs4n/items) objects with use context, while avoiding claims about an individual user’s feelings;
- [colonial Mexican records](https://doi.org/10.1017/tam.2020.41) in which racial and legal categories structure whose distress becomes legible;
- letters, legal petitions, household accounts, and court examinations from Europe, Africa, and Asia, prioritized over retrospective diagnosis.

## Required regional follow-up before the actual outline is approved

This note supports placement, not comprehensive coverage. The outline needs dedicated research for:

- Byzantine hospitals, law, household care, saints’ shrines, and patient-adjacent sources;
- Sasanian and post-Sasanian Persian medical institutions without repeating the “Jundishapur academy” legend;
- Syriac Christian medicine beyond famous translators, including convents, monasteries, and local practitioners;
- Jewish women’s, family, and non-elite healing records across the Mediterranean and West Asia;
- Arabic manuscript traditions of North and West Africa, including locally held collections and community authority;
- healing, distress, and social roles in East, Central, Southern, and forest-zone Africa before colonial archives dominate;
- Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, Tamil, Sinhala, and vernacular South Asian sources, plus intersections among Ayurveda, Siddha, Buddhist, Islamic, court, shrine, and household practices;
- Tang/Song/Yuan/Ming medical case histories, emotion vocabularies, family care, law, and religious healing;
- Korean and Japanese medicine, Buddhist institutions, household care, and early modern Tokugawa classifications;
- Southeast Asian manuscript and oral traditions within Indian Ocean, Islamic, Buddhist, and local networks;
- Indigenous American concepts and care north and south of Nahua evidence, with Indigenous-language and community-controlled sources;
- Pacific/Oceanic histories, which should not be forced into Eurasian period labels or left invisible;
- gender, sexuality, reproductive distress, disability, enslavement, war, famine, plague, migration, and poverty in every region;
- how children, elders, caregivers, attendants, nurses, enslaved workers, and poor people appear—or disappear—in the record.

## Disputes and red flags to carry into the outline

| Claim likely to appear in popular history | Project ruling for the provisional outline |
|---|---|
| “Islam created the first psychiatric hospital in the eighth century.” | **Disputed/too broad.** Map named hospitals and dated evidence. Do not award a world-first. |
| “Al-Razi opened the first psychiatric ward.” | **Unknown/disputed.** Requires near-contemporary institutional evidence; do not publish as fact. |
| “Islamic hospitals treated everyone humanely with music, water, and gardens.” | **Romanticized.** Evidence supports selected provisions and settings alongside confinement, chains, and unequal access. |
| “Al-Balkhi invented CBT.” | **Anachronistic.** Compare selected techniques, then explain the differences. |
| “Maimonides discovered psychosomatic medicine.” | **Priority claim rejected.** Present his specific integrated account in its Galenic, philosophical, Jewish, and Islamic contexts. |
| “Medieval Christians thought madness was always demonic.” | **False simplification.** Medical, legal, familial, moral, and spiritual accounts coexisted. |
| “Witches were mentally ill women persecuted instead of treated.” | **Historically distorted.** Study witch hunts on their legal, gendered, religious, political, and social evidence. |
| “Bethlem was a mental hospital from 1247.” | **False chronology.** Foundation in 1247; mental patients clearly attested in 1403. |
| “Tibetan medicine came from Galen.” | **Rejected meta-narrative.** Indic and Chinese foundations with additional western traces. |
| “The Renaissance discovered the brain.” | **Eurocentric and triumphalist.** Anatomy and illustration changed in Europe while multiple brain/mind traditions continued globally. |
| “Descartes invented mind-body dualism.” | **Misleading.** Place him inside longer Greek, Christian, Islamic, Jewish, and scholastic debates and specific seventeenth-century mechanism. |
| “Burton wrote the first book about depression.” | **Anachronistic.** *Melancholy* was polysemous and the book was a vast compilation, not a modern clinical monograph. |
| “The early modern period saw a Great Confinement of the insane.” | **Influential but contested and region-specific.** Present Foucault’s thesis later as historiography, then test it institution by institution. |
| “Patient voices tell us what mental illness was really like.” | **Too simple.** First-person material is invaluable but rhetorical, selected, translated, preserved, and catalogued through power. |

## Handoff to the chronological-outline editor

This foundation supports a backbone that treats approximately 300–1700 as:

1. late-antique remaking of soul and self;
2. multilingual translation and patronage;
3. philosophical and medical sciences of the soul;
4. plural explanations and therapies;
5. institutions embedded in households, law, religion, and streets;
6. multidirectional Hebrew, Latin, Persian, Sanskrit, Chinese, Tibetan, and Arabic circulation;
7. regionally distinct African and Asian intellectual worlds;
8. empire, mission, slavery, and colonial care/control;
9. print, anatomy, mechanism, and expanding self-writing; and
10. a transition into eighteenth-century classification, confinement, commerce, colonialism, and reform—without claiming a universal break at 1700.

**Approval gate:** Before these topics become an actual outline, the editor should require (a) coverage owners for every region, (b) at least one lived-experience or archive-silence question per segment, (c) a no-world-first review, (d) translation-term review, and (e) a separate pass for women, children, disabled people, caregivers, enslaved people, and non-elite practitioners.
