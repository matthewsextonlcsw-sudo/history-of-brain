# Modern Disciplines, Systems, and Rights, c. 1700–Present

**Foundation note:** 05

**Status:** Source-backed research for the master outline; not the master outline and not publication-ready copy

**Public destination:** Couch Commons, Understanding shelf

**Authority:** This note applies `../../PROJECT-BRAIN.md`, `../../outline/SCOPE-MAP.md`, and `../../sources/EVIDENCE-MODEL.md`. Those files win if this note conflicts with them.

**Research date:** August 18, 2026

## Purpose

This note proposes the historical backbone from roughly 1700 to the present. Its subject is
not simply the arrival of modern psychiatry. It is the formation, travel, collision, and
contestation of many systems: psychiatry, psychology, neuroscience, social work,
psychotherapy and counseling, public health, diagnosis, institutions, community care,
medicines, trauma frameworks, addiction responses, disability politics, survivor knowledge,
and digital mental health.

The central editorial rule is:

> **Tell the modern period as a history of interacting systems and contested authority, not
> as a Western science that gradually replaced everybody else's error.**

Institutions and professional categories often moved through empires, missionary networks,
universities, war, migration, international organizations, and markets. They were also
translated, refused, repurposed, and rebuilt locally. Colonial psychiatry was not peripheral
to an already completed European project: scholarship on French Indochina argues that
colonial practice helped constitute global psychiatry and social medicine, while patients
continued to move among hospitals, prisons, poor houses, families, and communities
([Edington, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8437878/)). Work on the post-1945
“global psyche” likewise shows actors in the Global South and Eastern Europe reshaping
transcultural psychiatry, even as ostensibly antiracist universalism retained older
hierarchies ([Antić, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1740022821000115)).

## What this note can and cannot establish

This is a **foundation for outlining**, not a claim that every country, healing tradition,
profession, diagnosis, or movement has been covered. It supports a six-transition backbone,
cross-cutting domain threads, and a first set of evidence anchors. It also records where the
source base remains uneven.

The source set favors original institutional documents, professional archives, WHO and UN
materials, museum and university collections, and peer-reviewed historical scholarship.
Organizational histories are useful for dates and institutional self-understanding, but they
are not neutral accounts of impact. Current WHO documents establish present policy and
reported system conditions, not the historical success of every intervention. English-
language discovery remains a major constraint.

## Proposed bridge from the early-modern material

The transition into this period should not say that “superstition ended and science began.”
It should ask a more revealing question:

> **What changed when states, hospitals, professions, laboratories, statistical offices,
> charities, schools, courts, and international organizations acquired new power to define
> distress and act on people?**

Older religious, household, community, Indigenous, humoral, and bodily explanations did not
vanish. New institutions layered on top of them. Families still made decisions; communities
still cared for, hid, expelled, protected, or exploited people; spiritual and medical
specialists continued to overlap; and patients moved between plural systems. The modern
story is therefore not one replacement but a changing distribution of authority.

## Recommended six-transition backbone

These are **historical transitions**, not finished page titles. Dates overlap deliberately.
Each transition needs at least one lived-experience route, one institutional route, one idea
or knowledge route, and more than one region.

### Transition 1 — c. 1700–1850: Reform, refuge, confinement, and medical jurisdiction

**Guiding question:** When care, custody, poor relief, and medicine met inside new and
reformed institutions, what changed for the people placed there?

Proposed topics:

- Expansion and reform of hospitals, madhouses, poor-law institutions, charitable refuges,
  prisons, and purpose-built asylums; different mixtures of treatment, shelter, discipline,
  labor, segregation, and custody.
- “Moral treatment” and related reforms as historically situated practices that could combine
  humane aspiration, ordered routines, paternal authority, and institutional control. It
  emerged in late eighteenth-century Europe in more than one form, and recovery was central to
  both the Pinel and Tuke traditions
  ([Charland, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/med/9780199691319.003.0005)). Avoid turning Pinel or
  the Tukes into solitary founders or treating their models as the history of every region.
- The growth of medical jurisdiction over madness and the changing labels *alienist* and
  *psychiatrist*. Even the familiar “first use” of *psychiatry* is disputed: recent lexical
  research identifies Karl Friedrich Burdach's use in 1800, challenging the conventional
  attribution to Johann Christian Reil in 1808
  ([Janssen, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/0957154X231167330)).
- Law, certification, property, family petitions, admission, discharge, and the economics of
  institutional care.
- Patient letters, petitions, memoirs, and institutional case records as evidence of agency,
  suffering, negotiation, and contradiction. A Weill Cornell exhibit preserves nineteenth-
  century patient writing, while a Western University collection shows that some letters
  praised and others criticized asylum conditions—and that letters could be confiscated
  ([Weill Cornell](https://oskardiethelm.omeka.net/exhibits/show/patient-perspectives-and--anti/19th-century-voices);
  [Western University](https://www.lib.uwo.ca/archives/virtualexhibits/londonasylum/patientletters.html)).

**Transition out:** The asylum helped make “madness” legible to states and professions. In the
nineteenth century, laboratories, censuses, schools, armies, colonial administrations, and
professional associations would widen that classificatory reach.

### Transition 2 — c. 1850–1914: Disciplines, laboratories, measurement, and empires

**Guiding question:** How did institutions turn mind, behavior, heredity, and brain into
objects that could be observed, measured, classified, and governed?

Proposed topics:

- Psychiatry's professional consolidation, asylum medicine, neuropathology, degeneration
  theories, and the uneven promise that diagnosis would make institutional care scientific.
- Experimental psychology's laboratories, journals, teaching posts, congresses, and tests.
  The first International Congress of Psychology met in Paris in 1889, but the later union's
  own history makes clear that formal global organization came much later
  ([IUPsyS](https://www.iupsys.net/about/iupsys-history/)).
- Do not reduce psychology to a Wundt-to-America relay. Japanese historians trace teaching in
  psychophysics from 1888 and a formal Tokyo laboratory in 1903
  ([Sato et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-5884.2005.00273.x)); a 2016 international
  history-of-psychology symposium documented a 1917 Beijing laboratory and later Brazilian
  behavior analysis as locally adapted rather than simply imported
  ([IJP symposium proceedings, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijop.12326)). Indian psychologists also
  combined modern doctrines, philosophy, ancient texts, and anticolonial nation-making rather
  than simply copying a finished European discipline
  ([Laskin, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1479244313000085)).
- Psychometrics and intelligence testing as both scientific techniques and instruments of
  schooling, military administration, migration policy, racial hierarchy, and eugenics.
  Their history was transnational: tests were circulated, appropriated, and transformed, not
  merely exported intact
  ([Carson, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150810/)).
- The neuron, localization, physiology, and new instruments, while avoiding a lone-genius
  parade. The International Association of Academies established a Brain Commission in 1903,
  one early attempt to coordinate brain research across borders
  ([Richter, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0361-9230%2800%2900294-X)).
- Charity organization, settlement work, mutual aid, medical social work, and early casework.
  These histories must distinguish the later profession of social work from the much older
  history of social care.
- Colonial hospitals and asylums as sites of coercion, scarcity, negotiation, and translation.
  In late nineteenth-century Lima, medicalization involved conflict and bargaining among
  alienists, families, and the state, while imported alienism was remade under local material
  pressures ([Amaya Nuñez, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2025.10034)).
  Ottoman reforms associated with Luigi Mongeri likewise offer a history of professional and
  institutional change within an empire, not a footnote to France or Britain
  ([Artvinli, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30080098/)).

**Transition out:** Measurement and administration promised order, but war and mass politics
made the risks unmistakable. The same professions that treated suffering could classify
populations, select soldiers, police borders, and declare some lives unfit.

### Transition 3 — c. 1914–1945: War, mental hygiene, mass administration, and eugenics

**Guiding question:** What happened when psychological and psychiatric knowledge became part
of military, educational, welfare, colonial, and population policy?

Proposed topics:

- “Soldier's heart,” shell shock, war neurosis, traumatic neurosis, and competing bodily and
  psychological explanations. This is not a straight march to PTSD; the labels, affected
  populations, causal theories, and politics changed
  ([Crocq and Crocq, 2000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3181586/)).
- Military screening and intelligence testing; rehabilitation; occupational and psychiatric
  social work; nursing; and the transfer of wartime techniques into civilian institutions.
- The international mental-hygiene movement, outpatient and child-guidance clinics, schools,
  workplaces, families, and prevention. Reject a simple “Clifford Beers invented global mental
  health” narrative. An off-centered history finds multiple national and regional projects,
  including Brazil and Argentina, and shows how the 1930 international congress remained
  heavily weighted toward US leadership despite broad attendance
  ([Scholten, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2025.10036)).
- India as an active site of mental-hygiene institution building, outpatient care,
  psychology, psychiatry, and psychiatric social work—not merely a recipient of a movement
  formed elsewhere
  ([Rajpal, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1017/mdh.2025.10035)).
- Transcolonial professional exchange. A 1937 conference in Bandung connected psychiatrists
  in the region, while French Indochinese institutions experimented with agricultural
  colonies and reliance on family and community networks
  ([Edington, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8437878/)).
- The professionalization of social work through international networks. The first
  International Conference of Social Work met in Paris in 1928, but the federation's own
  history records an initially narrow Europe-and-US institutional base; global reach cannot
  be read backward into the founding meeting
  ([IFSW](https://www.ifsw.org/golden-jubilee/)).
- Eugenics as an international and locally adapted network joining heredity, public health,
  migration, child welfare, disability, race, class, gender, and reproduction. Colombia was
  part of Pan-American eugenic networks even without a single national eugenics society;
  eugenic premises entered debates and policies concerning immigration and child welfare
  ([Olaya-Peláez, 2022](https://doi.org/10.15446/hys.n42.91951)).
- Forced sterilization, segregation, marriage restriction, institutional neglect, and mass
  killing. Nazi Aktion T4 murdered more than 70,000 institutional patients in its centralized
  phase, with physicians and administrators participating
  ([Memorial and Information Centre for the Victims of the Nazi “Euthanasia” Killings](https://www.t4-denkmal.de/eng/Start)).

**Transition out:** The catastrophes of war, fascism, racism, and colonial rule discredited
some claims to biological hierarchy but did not erase them. After 1945, medicines,
psychotherapies, welfare states, decolonization, and international organizations rearranged
the field again.

### Transition 4 — c. 1945–1970: Medicines, international systems, decolonization, and professional expansion

**Guiding question:** How did mental health become an international project—and whose idea of
the person traveled inside it?

Proposed topics:

- WHO and the World Federation for Mental Health as new nodes in postwar mental-health and
  public-health networks. WHO's 1948 records already included mental health, alcoholism, and
  addiction in the new organization's program
  ([WHO, 1948](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/85595/Official_record16_eng.pdf?sequence=1)).
  The International Congress on Mental Health that year brought approximately 2,000
  participants from about 50 countries, but numbers alone do not prove equal authority
  ([WHO, 1948](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/85597/1/Official_record18_eng.pdf)).
- The transfer of the International Classification of Diseases to WHO responsibility in 1948
  and the growing use of diagnosis for clinical communication, statistics, reimbursement,
  administration, and public policy
  ([WHO, ICD history](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/classification-of-diseases/international-classification-of-diseases)).
- New international professional infrastructures: IUPsyS was founded in 1951 after a UNESCO
  initiative ([IUPsyS](https://www.iupsys.net/about/iupsys-history/)); the modern IFSW was
  formed in 1956 ([IFSW](https://www.ifsw.org/golden-jubilee/)); UNESCO-supported discussions
  following a 1958 Moscow colloquium led to IBRO
  ([Marshall, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/0306-4522%2895%2900073-9)); and international counseling
  organization took institutional form in 1966
  ([International Association for Counselling, 2025](https://www.iac-irtac.org/sites/default/files/Member-Approved%20Revised%20Constitution%2011th%20June%202025%20%28F%29.pdf)).
- Chlorpromazine and the growth of neuropsychopharmacology. It was synthesized in 1951 and
  entered French psychiatric use in 1952 before spreading internationally; the safe claim is
  that it transformed practice and research, not that one pill single-handedly emptied every
  asylum
  ([Ban, 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2655089/)).
- Lithium, antidepressants, anxiolytics, and the pharmaceutical industry; benefit, adverse
  effects, access, marketing, regulation, patient adherence, and withdrawal must travel
  together in the later treatment outline.
- Psychoanalytic, psychodynamic, behavioral, cognitive, humanistic, group, family, and
  community therapies; counseling psychology; pastoral counseling; and culturally rooted
  healing. Do not write psychotherapy as Freud followed inevitably by CBT.
- Decolonization and transcultural psychiatry. Postwar experts in Africa, Asia, the Caribbean,
  Eastern Europe, and Latin America did more than provide “cultural examples”; they contested
  the location of normality, universality, and expertise
  ([Antić, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1740022821000115)).
- Jamaica as a counterpoint to a US-centered deinstitutionalization story: after independence,
  reformers reduced reliance on the colonial mental hospital and developed community and
  primary-care approaches
  ([Hickling, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1363461519893142)).

**Transition out:** New drugs and services made life outside institutions more possible for
some people, while fiscal withdrawal and weak housing or social support exposed others to
neglect, policing, homelessness, and incarceration. At the same time, diagnostic systems
became more standardized—and more publicly contested.

### Transition 5 — c. 1960–2000: Community care, standardized diagnosis, and movements from below

**Guiding question:** What changed when patients, survivors, disabled people, families,
anticolonial thinkers, feminists, queer activists, and community practitioners challenged
professional monopoly?

Proposed topics:

- Deinstitutionalization as many national and regional histories, not one policy. Italy's
  Law 180 in 1978 became a prominent legal and service transition toward community care
  ([Italian Ministry of Health](https://www.salute.gov.it/new/it/tema/salute-mentale/salute-mentale-dalla-legge-basaglia-ad-oggi/)).
  The 1990 Caracas Declaration called for restructuring psychiatric care in Latin America and
  the Caribbean around primary and community care
  ([PAHO, 2020](https://www.paho.org/en/events/regional-symposium-marking-30th-anniversary-declaration-caracas)).
- Community care must be evaluated through housing, income, family burden, peer support,
  crisis response, primary care, legal rights, and continuity—not bed closure alone. In the
  United States, one historical assessment links poorly supported deinstitutionalization to
  homelessness and incarceration; that national finding should not be universalized
  ([Scull, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33746131/)).
- DSM-II (1968), DSM-III (1980), later DSM revisions, and evolving ICD editions. APA's history
  describes DSM-III's explicit criteria, multiaxial system, and etiological neutrality
  ([APA](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/dsm/about-dsm/history-of-the-dsm)).
  A major historical interpretation argues that DSM-III reshaped US psychiatry while
  narrowing its clinical gaze; that is an interpretation to debate, not a settled global
  verdict ([Wilson, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.150.3.399)).
- PTSD's entry into DSM-III in 1980 and the activism, war, sexual violence, Holocaust, disaster,
  and refugee histories surrounding trauma recognition. Do not retrodiagnose every older
  account of fear or injury as PTSD
  ([Crocq and Crocq, 2000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3181586/)).
- Cultural concepts of distress and the limits of exporting one trauma template. A systematic
  review found both recurring themes and substantial variation in local trauma constructs,
  with PTSD often failing to map cleanly onto local categories
  ([Rasmussen et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4070307/)).
- Addiction as a field shaped by diagnosis, medicine, mutual aid, criminal law, colonial trade,
  international drug control, abstinence, medications, recovery movements, and harm
  reduction. WHO replaced older “addiction” and “habituation” terminology with dependence in
  1964 and later developed the dependence-syndrome concept
  ([WHO historical note](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/262129/PMC2396054.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)).
  Alcoholics Anonymous' own history records its US origin and subsequent international spread;
  use it as an organizational source, not a neutral verdict on recovery
  ([AA](https://www.aa.org/the-start-and-growth-of-aa)).
- Consumer, ex-patient, survivor, antipsychiatry, and peer-support movements. Historical
  scholarship places organized consumer/survivor challenges within the civil- and human-rights
  ferment of the 1960s and 1970s; later participation can still become tokenistic unless power
  is shared
  ([Daya et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/inm.12653)).
- Disability rights and the early neurodiversity movement. A recent review locates collective
  autistic online activism in the 1990s and cautions that the familiar sole-founder attribution
  to Judy Singer is historically disputed; Jim Sinclair's 1993 speech and other autistic
  self-advocacy are central
  ([McLennan et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40489-025-00493-2)).
- The removal or reframing of diagnoses connected to sexuality, gender, race, women, children,
  and political dissent. These are not interchangeable episodes: each requires its own
  evidence of professional debate, social movement, law, and lived consequence.
- Political abuse of psychiatry and coercion under multiple governments. This belongs in the
  rights thread, but it needs country-specific sources rather than a generic Cold War sidebar.

**Transition out:** Rights movements changed who could claim expertise. In the twenty-first
century, global programs and digital tools promised greater access, while old questions about
coercion, cultural authority, commercial power, and unequal resources persisted.

### Transition 6 — c. 2000–present: Rights, global public health, lived expertise, and digital systems

**Guiding question:** Can mental-health systems expand access without reproducing coercion,
diagnostic imperialism, exclusion, and surveillance?

Proposed topics:

- The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), adopted in 2006 and in
  force in 2008, as a shift from charity and treatment objects toward rights-bearing subjects,
  accessibility, participation, legal capacity, community inclusion, and free and informed
  consent ([United Nations](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention.html)).
  Disabled people's organizations were decisive participants in drafting and implementation,
  not merely stakeholders invited after the fact
  ([CRPD Committee, General Comment No. 7](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=j3guiQd0Hp2byJvITGx2HcxeXZdrqM4IeLGjasXGdMDUF4hnY9iaCr5CAsA7mWka21pAHThmMFo8I%2B59OiVpQQ%3D%3D)).
- WHO's mhGAP program, launched in 2008, as a strategy for scaling care for mental,
  neurological, and substance-use conditions, especially in lower-resource settings
  ([WHO, 2008](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241596206)). Its benefits, limits,
  adaptation, workforce effects, and relationship to local healing systems require evidence,
  not either celebration or dismissal in advance.
- The Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan, adopted by all WHO member states, with goals
  concerning governance, integrated community care, promotion and prevention, and information
  systems ([WHO, 2013](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241506021)).
- Rights-based community services. WHO's 2021 guidance presents person-centered examples in
  crisis services, hospitals, community centers, peer support, outreach, supported living,
  and service networks
  ([WHO, 2021](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240025707)). WHO and OHCHR's 2023
  legislative guidance explicitly links law reform to deinstitutionalization, community care,
  and reduction of coercion
  ([WHO and OHCHR, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080737)).
- Current system reality as unfinished transformation. WHO's 2024 Mental Health Atlas drew on
  144 countries; among countries reporting expenditure distribution, psychiatric hospitals
  still received the largest share, and the report warns that different respondent sets limit
  trend inference
  ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240114487)).
- Psychological interventions delivered by specialists, general health workers, trained
  community workers, peers, groups, and digital platforms. WHO's implementation manual treats
  contextual adaptation, workforce selection, supervision, safeguarding, and outcome
  monitoring as necessary parts of implementation
  ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240087149)).
- Tele-mental health, internet-delivered therapy, smartphone apps, text interventions,
  wearables, online peer communities, large language models, and algorithmic triage. A
  meta-review found small or modest benefits for some smartphone and text interventions but
  did not justify treating “digital mental health” as one proven treatment
  ([Goldberg et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0000002)).
- AI requires its own evidence and governance story. WHO says potential benefit does not
  displace concerns about autonomy, safety, transparency, accountability, equity, privacy,
  and bias ([WHO, 2021](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)); it has also
  warned against routine use of untested large language model tools in health
  ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-05-2023-who-calls-for-safe-and-ethical-ai-for-health)).
- Lived experience, peer work, and co-production as forms of knowledge and labor. The outline
  should ask who is paid, who controls the question, who owns the data, and whether dissenting
  survivor voices remain welcome—not merely count advisory-board seats.

**Present-day landing:** WHO's 2022 World Mental Health Report describes both high global need
and inadequate responses, and calls for transformed environments, strengthened systems, and
deeper commitment while incorporating lived experience
([WHO, 2022](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240049338/)). The history
should therefore end with open choices, not a victory screen.

## Current-evidence boundary for the eventual public pages

The historical collection will repeatedly meet a reader's “But does it work now?” question.
That question should be answered, but it requires a different evidence lane from historical
origin claims.

- Use present systematic reviews, guidelines, and comparative service evidence for current
  effectiveness. Do not infer effectiveness from longevity, professional endorsement, a
  founder story, or historical popularity.
- Specify intervention, population, outcome, comparator, follow-up period, harms, access, and
  setting. “Psychotherapy,” “medication,” “community care,” “peer support,” and “digital mental
  health” are categories, not single treatments.
- Treat adaptation, trained workforce, supervision, safeguarding, and outcome monitoring as
  parts of an intervention, not optional implementation details
  ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240087149)).
- Treat rights as an outcome and a condition of care, not a tradeoff that disappears when a
  symptom measure improves. Current WHO guidance joins service reform to person-centeredness,
  participation, community inclusion, and reduction of coercion
  ([WHO, 2021](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240025707);
  [WHO and OHCHR, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080737)).
- State evidence uncertainty. The mobile-phone meta-review, for example, supports modest
  benefits for some intervention/outcome pairings, not a blanket verdict on apps
  ([Goldberg et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0000002)).
- Keep system capacity visible. The 2024 Atlas' country reporting shows that the existence of
  international standards does not guarantee community services, equitable financing, a
  sufficient workforce, or comparable national data
  ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240114487)).

This boundary lets the history connect to present care without turning Couch Commons into a
treatment-advice page or using a historical narrative as medical evidence.

## Cross-cutting domain threads the outline must preserve

These threads should recur across transitions. They should not be isolated into one late
chapter and forgotten.

| Domain | Historical question that carries the thread | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Psychiatry | Who gained authority to name, confine, treat, certify, medicate, discharge, and testify? | Do not equate psychiatry with all mental healing or one national profession. |
| Psychology | How did philosophy, physiology, education, laboratories, tests, clinics, industries, and local intellectual traditions make multiple psychologies? | Replace a founder list with institutions, methods, uses, adaptations, and critics. |
| Neuroscience | How did anatomy, physiology, microscopy, lesions, electrophysiology, imaging, computation, and international coordination change claims about brain and person? | A brain correlate is not automatically a cause, diagnosis, or destiny. |
| Social work | How did poor relief, mutual aid, settlement, casework, welfare states, hospitals, psychiatric services, community organizing, and rights advocacy become a profession and a field of practice? | Do not make one US or British origin genealogy universal. |
| Counseling and psychotherapy | Who could offer structured healing conversation, in which settings, under what theories and credentials? | Preserve pastoral, community, Indigenous, family, group, and culturally adapted routes; not everything is Freud or CBT. |
| Public health | When did prevention, population statistics, schools, workplaces, housing, war, migration, and development policy become mental-health concerns? | Population benefit can coexist with surveillance, paternalism, or eugenic control. |
| Diagnosis | What did categories enable, conceal, reimburse, stigmatize, or make contestable? | DSM is primarily a US professional manual; ICD is an international statistical classification. Neither is the whole history of distress. |
| Institutions and community care | When were institutions shelter, treatment, abandonment, discipline, or violence—and what infrastructure made community life possible? | Do not treat bed closure as care, or institution/community as a simple moral binary. |
| Pharmacology | How did drugs alter symptoms, institutions, professional power, everyday life, markets, and patient movements? | Benefits, harms, access, regulation, marketing, and patient reports belong in the same account. |
| Trauma | How were overwhelming events understood through body, memory, morality, war, violence, family, culture, and diagnosis? | Use “PTSD-like” cautiously for the past; do not export one symptom model as universal. |
| Addiction | How did substance use become sin, habit, crime, disease, dependence, disability, coping, market, and public-health target? | Keep treatment, mutual aid, criminalization, colonial commerce, and harm reduction together. |
| Disability and neurodiversity | When did impairment-centered models encounter social, interactional, identity, and rights models? | Do not flatten internal debates, including high-support needs, cure, care, identity, and access. |
| Colonial systems | How did institutions classify colonized people, support administration, and also become sites of translation and resistance? | Colonial psychiatry is constitutive, not an exotic sidebar; colonized actors were not passive recipients. |
| Eugenics | How did heredity claims connect mental disability and distress to reproduction, immigration, education, race, poverty, and state violence? | Do not stop at Nazi Germany or imply eugenics vanished in 1945. Use country-specific mechanisms and victims' records. |
| Rights and survivor movements | How did people subjected to systems organize, publish, litigate, refuse, create peer alternatives, and redefine expertise? | “Patient voice” is not a decorative quotation. Show power, material demands, disagreement, and authorship. |
| Digital mental health | Which older practices—self-tracking, remote advice, screening, triage, peer communication, behavioral measurement—were recombined through networks and data? | Digital is a delivery category, not one intervention; ask about evidence, access, privacy, commercial incentives, and failure modes. |

## Global coverage rules and regional counterweights

### Minimum coverage rule

No transition should ship if all of its main causal action occurs in Europe and North America.
That does **not** mean inserting one “meanwhile elsewhere” example. The outline should show
connections: imperial law, migration, translation, religious and medical pluralism,
professional travel, socialist networks, liberation movements, international congresses,
pharmaceutical markets, diaspora, war, development aid, and internet communities.

For every transition, the research plan should attempt evidence from:

- Africa, including regional and country-specific distinctions rather than “Africa” as a unit;
- East Asia;
- South Asia;
- Southeast Asia;
- West Asia and North Africa;
- Europe, including Eastern and Southern Europe rather than treating Western Europe as Europe;
- Latin America and the Caribbean;
- North America, including Indigenous nations and colonial-state differences;
- Oceania and the Pacific;
- at least one transregional network or diaspora route.

Not every published page needs every region. The **collection architecture** does need to make
the absences inspectable and schedule region-led work rather than use the best-known English
case as a universal stand-in.

### Counterweights already supported by this source pass

- **Latin America and Caribbean:** Lima asylum negotiations; Colombian Pan-American eugenic
  networks; Jamaican postcolonial community care; the Caracas Declaration.
- **South Asia:** Indian psychology as an anticolonial intellectual project; Indian mental-
  hygiene clinics and psychiatric social work.
- **Southeast Asia:** French Indochinese institutions, family/community routes, and the
  Bandung network.
- **West Asia:** Ottoman institutional and professional reform through the Mongeri case.
- **Eastern Europe and decolonizing networks:** postwar transcultural psychiatry as an arena in
  which actors outside Western Europe and North America shaped claims about a universal psyche.
- **Transnational professional systems:** ICD, WHO, IUPsyS, IBRO, IFSW, WFMH, CRPD, and PAHO.

### Coverage still too weak for a global outline

This pass does **not** yet provide a defensible modern-period foundation for:

- country- and community-led histories in most of sub-Saharan Africa, particularly work in
  African languages and histories of nurses, families, religious communities, and users;
- Chinese psychiatry and psychology across late Qing, Republican, Mao-era, reform-era, and
  contemporary periods from accessible primary and locally authored sources;
- Japan beyond early disciplinary formation;
- Korea, Central Asia, the Caucasus, and most of the Arab world;
- Iran, despite its importance to psychiatry, psychology, addiction treatment, and public
  health;
- Indigenous nations in the Americas, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, Māori,
  and Pacific communities, especially community-controlled accounts of colonial harm and
  healing sovereignty;
- the Caribbean outside Jamaica;
- socialist and non-aligned international mental-health networks beyond the initial postwar
  transcultural-psychiatry source;
- global histories of nursing, occupational therapy, psychiatric rehabilitation, and family
  caregiving;
- the political abuse of psychiatry across the Soviet Union, China, Latin American
  dictatorships, apartheid South Africa, and other regimes, using victim-centered and
  country-specific evidence;
- eugenics in Scandinavia, Japan, China, South Asia, Southern Africa, Oceania, and the Americas
  beyond the current Colombia anchor;
- non-English survivor and Mad Pride movements and the Global South history of peer work;
- the uneven global histories of harm reduction, methadone and buprenorphine, therapeutic
  communities, Indigenous recovery movements, and criminalization;
- digital mental-health infrastructures and data governance outside high-income app markets.

These are research assignments, not optional inclusivity polish.

## Lived experience and missing voices

The archive is structured by power. Institutional records often preserve what officials,
physicians, missionaries, police, courts, and family petitioners wrote *about* a person. The
UK National Archives notes that many mental-health patient files were destroyed, a concrete
warning against treating archival silence as patient consent or absence of resistance
([UK National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/mental-health/)).

Every substantial era page should therefore seek at least two of the following, with the
limits of each source named:

- first-person letters, diaries, memoirs, testimony, oral history, art, newspapers, blogs, or
  recorded speech;
- records of admission, escape, refusal, petition, complaint, litigation, discharge, work,
  family negotiation, and collective organization;
- nurses, attendants, social workers, community health workers, peer workers, interpreters,
  and domestic workers;
- families and chosen families, including burden, conflict, support, coercion, and advocacy;
- disabled people's, survivor, user, Mad, neurodivergent, queer, feminist, antiracist,
  Indigenous, and abolitionist organizations;
- people affected by prisons, colonial administration, slavery and its afterlives, migration,
  war, institutionalization, poverty, and homelessness;
- children and older adults, whose records are especially likely to be mediated by adults or
  institutions;
- people who report benefit from treatment or institutions as well as people who report harm.

The writer should never invent the inner experience of a person from a diagnosis or case
note. “The record says,” “the clinician interpreted,” “the family petitioned,” and “the
patient wrote” are different evidence statements.

## Disputes the final outline should preserve

These are productive historical questions, not problems to flatten into false balance.

### Did the asylum begin as reform or repression?

Both framings can be true in different places and moments. Institutions could offer shelter,
food, removal from jail, and treatment while also imposing labor, segregation, surveillance,
violence, and loss of liberty. The answer must be local, chronological, and attentive to who
is speaking.

### Did psychiatry and psychology diffuse from “the West”?

Institutions and concepts often traveled through European and US power, but travel was not
copying. Ottoman, Peruvian, Indian, Japanese, Chinese, Brazilian, Vietnamese, Jamaican, and
other actors selected, translated, resisted, and transformed them. Colonial sites also
generated knowledge that returned to metropolitan and international debates
([Edington](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8437878/);
[Antić](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1740022821000115)).

### Did psychopharmaceuticals cause deinstitutionalization?

Medicines changed treatment possibilities, but bed reductions also depended on law, budgets,
welfare policy, housing, activism, labor, family care, and local service capacity. The outline
should not assign a multi-causal transformation to chlorpromazine alone.

### Did DSM-III make diagnosis scientific?

DSM-III increased operational specificity and reliability ambitions, while critics argue it
narrowed clinical attention and did not solve questions of validity or context
([APA](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/dsm/about-dsm/history-of-the-dsm);
[Wilson](https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.150.3.399)). The page should explain reliability versus
validity in public language and keep DSM's US setting distinct from ICD's international role.

### Is PTSD universal?

Overwhelming events can produce severe and lasting distress across societies; that does not
mean one diagnostic pattern exhausts how people experience, name, or respond to it.
Cross-cultural reviews find both recurring phenomena and substantial mismatch between PTSD
and local constructs ([Rasmussen et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4070307/)).

### Was deinstitutionalization liberation or abandonment?

It could be either or both. Freedom without housing, income, accessible health care, crisis
support, and belonging can become abandonment. Community care with coercive or carceral
substitutes is not automatically community inclusion. Compare Italy, Jamaica, Caracas, and
country-specific failures rather than declaring a universal verdict.

### Are survivor knowledge and professional knowledge opposed?

No single bloc speaks for every survivor, patient, family, or professional. The historical
question is who controls resources and definitions, whose testimony counts, and whether
systems can hold disagreement without tokenizing it
([Daya et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/inm.12653)).

### Does neurodiversity deny disability or support needs?

The movement is internally diverse. Scholarship distinguishes social, political, and
interactional claims and records disputes about profound disability, care, cure, identity,
and high support needs
([McLennan et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40489-025-00493-2)). Do not let either a celebratory
slogan or a deficit-only medical account substitute for those debates.

### Is global mental health access or empire?

Task-sharing and scalable interventions may expand care where specialist services are scarce;
unadapted categories and programs can also displace local authority or ignore structural
conditions. WHO's current implementation manual itself requires contextual adaptation,
workforce planning, safeguarding, and outcome monitoring
([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240087149)). The outline should test
specific programs and partnerships instead of assigning the whole field one moral label.

### Is digital mental health democratization or surveillance?

It can lower distance and scheduling barriers and can also expose intimate data, reproduce
bias, fail in crisis, exclude people, or sell engagement as care. Evidence belongs at the
level of a tool, population, outcome, comparator, and implementation context—not the label
“digital” ([Goldberg et al.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0000002);
[WHO AI guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)).

## Proposed narrative transitions

These sentences are conceptual bridges, not final copy.

1. **Household and institution:** “When care moved behind an institution's walls, it did not
   stop being family business; families petitioned, paid, visited, resisted, and took people
   home.”
2. **Institution and measurement:** “The asylum gathered people in one place. The laboratory,
   test, census, and case file turned their differences into data.”
3. **Measurement and population policy:** “Once minds could be ranked on paper, schools,
   armies, border officials, and eugenic planners found uses for the rankings.”
4. **War and internationalism:** “World war exposed the scale of psychological injury—and the
   power of mental-health expertise to heal, screen, classify, and exclude.”
5. **Internationalism and decolonization:** “After empire, the language of a universal psyche
   promised common humanity, while the institutions carrying it still contained imperial
   assumptions.”
6. **Medicine and community:** “A pill could change what was possible inside a ward. It could
   not by itself create a home, an income, a relationship, or a right.”
7. **Diagnosis and protest:** “A category can open a door to care and close a door to
   citizenship; the people categorized began demanding a say in both.”
8. **Rights and implementation:** “A treaty can redefine the person in law. Services, budgets,
   courts, and daily practice decide whether that person can live the right.”
9. **Digital present:** “The newest clinic may fit in a pocket, but its oldest questions remain:
   Who listens, who decides, who benefits, and who can leave?”

## Research sequence before the actual outline is frozen

1. Build a region-by-transition coverage matrix and assign every empty cell a status:
   required, connective, separate regional page, or documented absence.
2. Run region-led source passes with multilingual search terms and locally based historians,
   archivists, professional associations, user/survivor organizations, and Indigenous or
   community authorities.
3. Build parallel source ledgers for psychiatry, psychology, neuroscience, social work,
   counseling/psychotherapy, trauma, addiction, disability/neurodiversity, and digital care.
4. For every institution or treatment, collect evidence from at least three perspectives when
   available: administrator/professional, person subjected to or using it, and family/community
   or outside observer.
5. Verify dates and “firsts” against original documents or specialist histories; label
   definition-dependent claims.
6. Separate **history evidence** from **current effectiveness evidence**. A treatment's origin
   story does not establish its present benefit, and a modern trial does not establish what a
   historical patient experienced.
7. Add current systematic reviews and guidelines only after the historical outline makes the
   intervention, outcome, population, comparator, and harms visible.
8. Seek review from historians or community experts for regions where English-language
   scholarship is sparse, and from lived-experience reviewers for institutional, coercion,
   disability, addiction, and digital-surveillance sections.

This research supports advancing to the actual outline **only if** the outline remains
