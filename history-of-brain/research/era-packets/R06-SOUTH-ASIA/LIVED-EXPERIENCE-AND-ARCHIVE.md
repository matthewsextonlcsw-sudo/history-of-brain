# R06-SOUTH-ASIA — Lived experience and archive

**Use:** archive-bias and empathy control; no public prose

## What kinds of voices survive in the checked core

| archive layer | surviving voice | what it can show | what it cannot show |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upaniṣadic passages (`EVD-009000–EVD-009001`) | authored/redacted teaching, dialogue, and analogy preserved in Sanskrit tradition and a colonial-era English translation | one text's categories, pedagogy, and soteriological argument | ordinary people's self-description, actual emotional care, gender/caste access, or patient experience |
| Buddhist discourses (`EVD-009002–EVD-009004`, `EVD-009007`) | formulaic monastic teaching and idealized interlocution preserved through oral/canonical lineages | named practice instructions and classifications of experience | verbatim speech, prevalence, adherence, clinical outcome, or the full range of lay experience |
| *Therīgāthā* (`EVD-009005–EVD-009006`) | rare first-person-form verse attributed to women renunciants, mixed with other speakers and later framing | poetic self-presentation, sensory detail, suffering, serenity, and liberation as the collection transmits them | authenticated autobiography, complete life history, diagnostic status, or an unmediated interview |
| Jain philosophical sources (`EVD-009008`) | specialist doctrinal and commentarial analysis | technical relations among *manas*, cognition, *jīva*, *pudgala*, and karma | common usage, distress narratives, institutional access, or one pan-Jain view |
| Caraka pathology/treatment (`EVD-009009–EVD-009014`) | professional, normative medical classification and prescription in the verified core | what compilers/transmitters classified and prescribed; what Pecchia's bounded manuscript sample preserves | whether treatments occurred, how often, with consent, with what outcome, or how a treated person felt |
| Caraka access/training lead (`EVD-009015`) | anonymous modern webpage rendering | passages to seek in a named edition | verified ancient status, disability, exclusion, gender/guardian, access, or implementation claims |
| Yoga (`EVD-009016–EVD-009018`) | Brahmanical soteriological system and later commentarial interpretation | taxonomies of cognition, obstacles, affliction, discipline, and changing reception | general practice, therapeutic outcome, popular accessibility, or direct continuity with modern studios/therapy |

## Patient and practitioner experience

### Direct or near-direct voice

- **Strongest available voice:** Thig 5.10 (`EVD-009005`) uses first-person grammar and concrete acts—washing feet, watching water, sitting, drawing a lamp wick—before a liberation claim. It can be handled as a **transmitted poetic voice attributed to Paṭācārā**.
- **Why it is not “raw testimony”:** collection, attribution, oral transmission, redaction, manuscript variation, and translation mediate the poem (`EVD-009006–EVD-009007`). Its value does not depend on pretending it is a diary.
- **No checked *unmāda* patient voice:** the medical text describes the patient from the practitioner's viewpoint. No person subjected to restraint, threats, purification, ritual, or comfort speaks in the opened core.

### Experience represented by authorities

- SN 36.6 represents an ordinary person's sorrow and a trained disciple's response through a didactic contrast. It is an idealized teaching situation, not observation of two sampled patients.
- Caraka enumerates grief, fear, anxiety, anger, loss, and behavioral change, but the sufferer's language is absorbed into a professional taxonomy. Even apparently compassionate instructions remain prescriber voice.
- Caraka's violent measures must be recorded without spectacle or euphemism. The humane historical statement is: **the text prescribes them; the archive presently cannot tell us whether, how often, or with what lived consequences they were used.**

## Gender, caste/status, body, and access

### Gender

- The *Therīgāthā* makes women's attributed renunciant voices unusually visible, while its mixed speakers and later male commentary complicate attribution (`EVD-009006`). Female monastic voice is not the same as a representative sample of women.
- An anonymous webpage rendering flags possible male-addressed and husband/guardian or male-companion wording in *Vimānasthāna* 8 (`EVD-009015`). Because no named Sanskrit edition or translator was identified, even the ancient normative proposition remains **Unknown**; it is a follow-up lead, not outline evidence.
- No checked source provides denominators for women's access to medical treatment, renunciation, education, or textual authorship.

### Caste and status

- The Siva.sh rendering appears to mention Brahmins/physicians, medical family/familiarity, and treatment exclusions (`EVD-009015`), but those exact ancient-language claims are held until a named critical edition/translation verifies them.
- The checked text does **not** establish a simple rule that only one caste could study medicine. A caste-specific access claim is therefore held.
- Pecchia's later manuscript archive foregrounds courts, patrons, libraries, learned centers, physicians, and professional/learned copyists (`EVD-009014`). These preservation institutions filter what survives; they are not a census of ancient healers or sufferers.

### Disability and bodily norm

- The anonymous rendering appears to list bodily and sensory criteria (`EVD-009015`). It is not yet verified textual evidence of an able-bodied admission ideal. A named scholarly translation and source-language review must establish each term before the packet specifies exclusion or disability meaning.

### Household and political access

- The anonymous *Vimānasthāna* rendering flags household entry, confidentiality, rulers/power, and women as questions for follow-up; the packet cannot yet use it to characterize the ancient normative imagination.
- It does not establish price, geographic reach, who could pay, who refused treatment, how families negotiated care, or whether people under coercive measures had alternatives.

## Translation and category cautions

| source term | tempting English bridge | control |
|---|---|---|
| *manas* | mind | Keep the source system visible; in Jain, Buddhist, Upaniṣadic, and medical sources it does different relational work. |
| *buddhi* | intellect | Historical translation, not IQ or executive function. |
| *citta* | mind / mind-stuff | Woods's “mind-stuff” is an interpretive historical rendering, not neutral scientific vocabulary. |
| *hṛdaya* | heart / brain | The checked Caraka Sanskrit says *hṛdaya*; “brain” is inserted by one translation. Positive meaning needs specialist review. |
| *unmāda* | madness / insanity / psychosis | Retain *unmāda*. Modern diagnostic mappings belong to reception history, not silent translation. |
| *sati* / *satipaṭṭhāna* | mindfulness | A useful search-language bridge only when monastic setting, technique, and liberation aim are named. |
| *dukkha* | suffering / pain | Context-sensitive; not automatically depression, trauma, or a symptom score. |
| *kleśa* | affliction | Soteriological category, not a DSM diagnosis. |
| *rasāyana* | rejuvenation / wellness / longevity treatment | Meanings and interpretations changed; do not imply a continuous commercial wellness practice. |

## Empathy rules for later writing

1. Name the sufferer as a person represented by a source, not as a diagnosis.
2. Do not use comic, lurid, or “primitive” framing for disturbance, restraint, ritual, or coercion.
3. Do not make benevolent claims from prescriber intent alone.
4. Pair every coercive-treatment statement with the archive limit: prescription does not prove use, and the treated person's response is absent.
5. Do not turn the *Therīgāthā* into trauma recovery case studies. Preserve poetic and religious aims.
6. Do not make modern practitioners responsible for every historical text, or ancient texts responsible for validating current clinical products.
7. Keep uncertainty human-readable: “the record does not tell us” is preferable to invented emotional color.

## Archive absences to carry into the outline

- No securely identified patient-authored account of *unmāda* treatment.
- No prevalence or outcome data for the named historical treatments.
- No reliable access, cost, refusal, consent, or satisfaction data across caste, class, gender, disability, rural/urban location, or political status.
- No vernacular or material-culture corpus in the narrowed core.
- No archaeological evidence tied securely to the five selected textual laboratories.
- No current critical Upaniṣad translation opened for word-level publication.
- No named scholarly English translation opened for Caraka *Vimānasthāna* 8.8 and 8.13–14.
- Regional imbalance: the packet is text-centered and cannot stand for the subcontinent, Sri Lanka, or South Asian diasporas.

## Safe handoff formulations

- “A transmitted Pāli poem attributed to the nun Paṭācārā presents…”
- “The medical compendium prescribes…”
- “The checked archive does not reveal how a person subjected to this treatment experienced it.”
- “A modern English translation inserts ‘the brain’; the checked Sanskrit reads *hṛdaya*.”
- “Modern authors have mapped *unmāda* onto psychosis, but that mapping is not an ancient diagnosis.”
- “A separate 2023 systematic review found a small, heterogeneous, methodologically limited evidence base and did not support a firm effectiveness conclusion; current clinical claims require a fresh review.”
- “These selected sources preserve competing and non-equivalent categories, not one South Asian theory of mind.”
