# R06-SOUTH-ASIA — Findings

**Use:** internal research findings only; not publication copy
**Core:** five textual laboratories within the brief's broad first-millennium-BCE to first-millennium-CE horizon. This packet did not establish a shared `500 BCE–500 CE` chronology; corpus-specific composition and redaction dates remain open, and manuscript, edition, and translation dates stay separate.

## Evidence records

### `EVD-009000` — Kaṭha's chariot is a hierarchy of faculties, not a brain diagram

- **Evidence:** Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.3.3–11 compares self, body, *buddhi*, *manas*, senses, and sense objects to rider, chariot, charioteer, reins, horses, and roads. Discipline differentiates the one who reaches the goal from the one whose senses are uncontrolled.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009000`, printed pp. 12–13.
- **Rating:** L1-A for Müller's witness; B/C for ancient semantics.
- **Limit:** an analogy in a soteriological text is not anatomical evidence, a cognitive experiment, or “the Indian model of mind.” Müller's “mind” and “intellect” are translation choices.

### `EVD-009001` — Bṛhadāraṇyaka links desire, resolve, action, and result

- **Evidence:** Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.5–7 describes the self with knowledge, *manas*, desire/no desire, anger/no anger, and a sequence in which desire is related to resolve/action and consequence; the next movement privileges freedom from desire.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009000`, printed pp. 176–177.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the translation; C for a general psychology.
- **Limit:** one passage cannot carry a linear history of motivation, cognition, or therapy. The packet did not open a current critical translation for word-level publication.

### `EVD-009002` — Not-self discourse is aggregate-specific and practice-directed

- **Evidence:** SN 22.59 treats form, feeling, perception, choices/formations, and consciousness as not-self, liable to affliction and change, and directs the mendicants to regard each as not mine/not me/not my self.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009006`, segments 2.1–10.1.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the opened Pāli/English edition; C for earliest wording.
- **Limit:** this does not warrant “Buddhism says no self exists” across periods and schools, nor a modern theory of personality.

### `EVD-009003` — The “two arrows” distinguish pain from added mental distress

- **Evidence:** SN 36.6 says an untrained and trained person both experience pleasant, painful, and neutral feelings; its arrow simile contrasts a bodily painful feeling with sorrow, resistance, and further distress.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009007`, segments 1.1–6.4.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the edition; C for historical practice/outcome.
- **Limit:** doctrinal instruction and an ideal practitioner are not clinical pain science or evidence that practice produced the stated outcome in a population.

### `EVD-009004` — MN 10 prescribes structured observation

- **Evidence:** MN 10 organizes practice around observation of body, feelings, mind, and principles, including recognition of mind with or without greed, hate, and delusion. The explicit audience is mendicants and the stated end is soteriological.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009008`, segments 2.1–47.2, especially 3–12, 32–38, 44–47.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the edition; D for modern therapeutic continuity.
- **Limit:** the English title “Mindfulness Meditation” and modern clinical term “mindfulness” must not collapse transmission, institutional setting, aim, or technique.

### `EVD-009005` — Paṭācārā's verse preserves a mediated first-person voice

- **Evidence:** Thig 5.10 moves from ordinary work and disciplined practice through a sensory scene to a first-person declaration of serenity and a lamp-extinguishing simile for liberation.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009009`, segments 1.1–5.4.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the verse as transmitted; C for individual authorship/biography.
- **Limit:** this is canonical poetry, not a diary, interview, diagnostic case, or unedited patient testimony.

### `EVD-009006` — Therīgāthā attribution and commentary require separation

- **Evidence:** Sujato estimates that roughly 100 of the collection's verses are not spoken by nuns, notes attribution conflicts, and distinguishes early verses from a commentary about a millennium later. He also documents the 1883 Pischel edition's four manuscripts and shared corruption.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009010`, paras. 13–35, 47–48, 124–129.
- **Rating:** L3-B.
- **Limit:** this is a translator's critical introduction and sometimes polemical argument. Its count is approximate; it does not settle every poem's date or authorship. Allon's study of early Buddhist prose (`EVD-009007`) supplies general oral-transmission context only and is not independent evidence for *Therīgāthā* verse attribution or manuscript corruption.

### `EVD-009007` — Early Buddhist canonical prose is crafted and transmitted, not recorded speech

- **Evidence:** Allon argues that early Buddhist prose is highly stylized, formulaic, and organized for memorization/communal recitation; comparative Pāli, Gāndhārī, Sanskrit, and Chinese witnesses show both conservatism and substantial change. He explicitly denies that surviving texts are tape-recorder records.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009015`, pp. 523–556, especially online paras. 1–18 and conclusion.
- **Rating:** L2-A.
- **Limit:** Allon's fixed-text model is a specialist argument with counter-readings in the literature; it does not precisely date SN 22.59, SN 36.6, or MN 10.

### `EVD-009008` — Jain *manas* is a technical, relational category

- **Evidence:** Soni reads TAS 1.9–14, 5.19–20, and 6.1–2: *manas* participates in cognition, is treated by Pūjyapāda as an internal/quasi-sense instrument, and is materially related to *pudgala* while activity of body, speech, and *manas* conditions karmic influx to *jīva*.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009005`, pp. 315–321, 326–327.
- **Rating:** L2-A for the cited Jain textual analysis.
- **Limit:** neither “mind” nor “brain” is an adequate one-word equivalence. Digambara and Śvetāmbara numbering and later commentary must remain visible.

### `EVD-009009` — Caraka defines *unmāda* across several faculties

- **Evidence:** *Nidānasthāna* 7.3–5 classifies humoral, combined, and extraneous forms and describes disturbance of *manas*, *buddhi*, *saṃjñā/jñāna*, *smṛti*, *bhakti*, *śīla*, *ceṣṭā*, and *ācāra*.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009011`, §§7.3–5, checked against Sanskrit in `SRC-009013` where relevant.
- **Rating:** L1-A for what the opened witnesses contain; C/D for modern diagnosis.
- **Limit:** the category spans thought, recognition, memory, disposition, conduct, bodily signs, humors, and social/moral judgments. It is not coextensive with psychosis or schizophrenia.

### `EVD-009010` — Caraka does not split “physical” from “psychological” and “supernatural” causes

- **Evidence:** *Nidānasthāna* 7.4 and 7.10–23 combine food/regimen, bodily disturbance, desire, anger, greed, excitement, fear, grief, anxiety, humors, vows/ethical transgression, karma, and nonhuman agents. *Cikitsāsthāna* 9 combines medical, affective, and ritual actions.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009011`, §§7.4, 7.10–23; `SRC-009012`, §§25–32 and 87–96.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the textual repertoire; D for actual explanatory prevalence.
- **Limit:** do not narrate a progress from “superstition” to “science,” and do not assume every healer or sufferer accepted every cause.

### `EVD-009011` — Caraka's treatment repertoire includes comfort and coercion

- **Evidence:** *Cikitsāsthāna* 9 prescribes diet, ghee/oils, drugs, emesis/purgation, ritual actions, coaxing, reassurance/comfort, and counter-emotion; it also prescribes confinement, restraint, frightening, threats, beating/flagellation, and scalding measures in selected situations.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009012`, §§25–32 and 78–86 for the key comfort/coercion/counter-emotion contrast; §§87–96 for ritual and selection context; compare `SRC-009002`, pp. 28–30.
- **Rating:** L1-A for textual prescription; D for actual practice, frequency, outcome, and experience.
- **Limit:** never soften violent prescriptions into “holistic care,” but never imply that every named act was ordinarily performed. Weiss explicitly says spread is unknown.

### `EVD-009012` — “The brain” is a translation insertion

- **Evidence:** the Sanskrit clause in *Nidānasthāna* 7.4 reads `hṛdayam upasṛtya manovahāni srotāṃsy āvṛtya`; the Jamnagar English inserts “heart (the brain).” No Sanskrit word for brain occurs in that clause.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009013`, Nidāna 7.4; compare `SRC-009011`, §7.4.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the displayed Sanskrit and translation comparison; B for the correct English rendering of *hṛdaya* in its full historical physiology.
- **Limit:** this check blocks “Caraka located madness in the brain.” It does not by itself settle whether *hṛdaya* was anatomical heart, central/vital locus, or a context-dependent concept.

### `EVD-009013` — *Cikitsāsthāna* 9 carries a restoration notice

- **Evidence:** the chapter colophon says the chapter on treating *unmāda* had disappeared and was restored by Dṛḍhabala.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009012`, recovery signs at §97, summary at §98, and chapter colophon following §98 on the opened Jamnagar/Wisdomlib page; historical context in `SRC-009004`, pp. 74–78.
- **Rating:** L1-A for the transmitted notice; B for reconstruction of stages.
- **Limit:** “Caraka wrote” is unsafe for every verse; compilation, redaction, restoration, manuscript, edition, and translation must not be collapsed.

### `EVD-009014` — Pecchia's chiefly *Vimānasthāna* sample is late, partial, and socially selective

- **Evidence:** Pecchia reports 236 known Caraka manuscripts at the study's survey level. Within the available sample used for the paper's precise analysis, chiefly *Vimānasthāna*, only a minority contain the complete work; 49 *Vimānasthāna* copies were available, and the oldest dated available *Vimānasthāna* witness was copied in 1592. Many sample records lack dates/provenance; royal/court libraries, learned centers, physicians, patrons, and professional or learned copyists shaped survival.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009003`, pp. 142–159.
- **Rating:** L2-A.
- **Limit:** *Vimānasthāna* evidence cannot be transferred mechanically to every chapter. Survival patterns do not directly reveal ancient use, patient access, or oral practice.

### `EVD-009015` — Anonymous webpage flags possible access passages for verification

- **Evidence:** the consulted Siva.sh pages display Sanskrit and an unnamed English rendering that appears to describe a medical-family/familiarity preference, bodily/sensory criteria, a ritual/teacher hierarchy, treatment exclusions, and husband/guardian or male-companion rules. The site does not identify the Sanskrit edition, translator, variant policy, or editorial method needed to verify those ancient-language propositions.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009016`, complete pages for 8.8, 8.13–14.
- **Rating:** **L5-A for what the anonymous pages display; D for ancient semantics or social implementation.**
- **Limit:** discovery lead only. It does not support an outline claim about lineage, disability/status, exclusion, gender, guardianship, access, or practice until a named critical edition/translation and source-language review verify the wording.

### `EVD-009016` — Maas reconstructs PYŚ as a late-fourth/early-fifth-century author-redactor's work

- **Evidence:** Maas describes the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* as the oldest surviving systematic Sanskrit exposition of Yoga from a Brahmanical perspective and argues that an author-redactor Patañjali partially composed/compiled it in the late fourth or early fifth century CE.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009004`, pp. 66–67.
- **Rating:** L2-A.
- **Limit:** this is Maas's scholarly reconstruction, not an authorial colophon or independently established field consensus. “Yoga is 5,000 years old” and an undifferentiated “Yoga Sūtras” chronology are blocked.

### `EVD-009017` — PYŚ organizes mental events and cultivation within soteriology

- **Evidence:** Woods's edition renders PYŚ 1.2 as restriction of *citta-vṛtti*, classifies five *vṛtti* (valid cognition, error, conceptual construction, sleep, memory), gives practice and dispassion as disciplines, identifies obstacles with pain/despondency/breath disturbance, and prescribes friendliness, compassion, joy, and equanimity; 2.3–11 lists five *kleśa*.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009001`, translation pp. xxx–xxxiv, PYŚ 1.2, 1.5–16, 1.30–39, 2.3–11.
- **Rating:** L1-A for Woods's witness; B/C for terminology.
- **Limit:** “mind-stuff,” “affliction,” and “concentration” are historical translations. The system aims at liberation and discriminative insight, not symptom management alone.

### `EVD-009018` — Maas argues that *rasāyana* reception is interpretively discontinuous

- **Evidence:** Maas argues that PYŚ's brief *rasāyana* references were interpreted differently by Vācaspati, Bhoja, and Śaṅkara; later commentators linked the term to alchemy or Āyurveda in ways not necessarily present in PYŚ. He further argues that Aruṇadatta's much later medical etymology relies on a standardized bodily-element scheme not yet present in Caraka's time.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009004`, pp. 66–72, 74–78, 80–82.
- **Rating:** L2-A.
- **Limit:** shared vocabulary across centuries is not an unbroken unchanged wellness practice. Later explanation can be reception rather than original meaning.

### `EVD-009019` — Modern authors map *unmāda* to psychosis

- **Evidence:** Weiss (1981) calls technical *unmāda* “psychosis” and compares subtypes with Western nosology. Kulamarva et al. (2023) describe *unmāda* as equivalent to psychosis/related disorders. These publications document a modern diagnostic mapping; they do not establish an ancient identity.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009002`, pp. 25–31, especially pp. 25 and 27–30; `SRC-009014`, full-text introduction/background and eligibility framing.
- **Rating:** L2-A for documenting what the modern authors map; D for historical identity.
- **Limit:** both are modern reception sources that ultimately interpret the Caraka tradition, not independent ancient witnesses. State “modern authors map,” never translate *unmāda* silently as psychosis.

### `EVD-009020` — The 2023 clinical review cannot support a firm effectiveness conclusion

- **Evidence:** Kulamarva et al. include 12 reports and describe heterogeneous regimens, unclear methods in most trials, unvalidated measures in older studies, short observation, and incomplete blinding, follow-up, and dropout reporting; the review says the evidence is insufficient for a firm effectiveness conclusion.
- **Source / locator:** `SRC-009014`, full-text Methods, Results, Discussion/Limitations, and Conclusion.
- **Rating:** L2-A for the review's own sample and methodological findings; D for general efficacy, safety, or treatment advice.
- **Limit:** this is one 2023 systematic review with a time-sensitive search horizon. It does not evaluate all current evidence indefinitely, establish safety, or authorize clinical recommendations. It is separate from the historical fact that modern authors map *unmāda* to psychosis.

## Cross-corpus findings

1. **Plurality is the finding.** *Manas*, *citta*, *buddhi*, *jīva*, *hṛdaya*, *vṛtti*, *kleśa*, aggregates, and *unmāda* operate in different systems and cannot be flattened into “mind,” “brain,” or “mental illness.”
2. **Cultivation is not automatically therapy.** Buddhist and Yoga prescriptions address suffering, attention, affect, cognition, and liberation, but the texts do not supply modern clinical endpoints or population outcomes.
3. **Care is ethically mixed.** Caraka contains comfort, reassurance, diet, drugs, purification, ritual, hierarchy, exclusion, restraint, fear, and violence. “Holistic” and “barbaric” are both inadequate summaries.
4. **The archive has power.** Most records are elite, monastic, philosophical, or professional prescriptions. Women's first-person-like verse is rare and mediated. Patients subjected to *unmāda* treatment do not speak in the checked corpus.
5. **Transmission is part of the history.** Oral recitation, redaction, restoration, late manuscripts, colonial translations, contemporary digital editions, and modern diagnostic mappings all change what can responsibly be said.

## Disagreement records

### `DIS-009000` — What does *hṛdaya* denote in Caraka *Nidāna* 7.4?

- **Question:** should *hṛdaya* in this clause be rendered anatomical heart, brain, a central/vital locus, or another context-dependent concept?
- **Positions:** the Jamnagar English witness prints “heart (the brain)”; the checked GRETIL Sanskrit has *hṛdaya* and no separate word for brain; the packet does not adjudicate among positive historical interpretations of *hṛdaya*.
- **Evidence / source relationships:** `EVD-009012` compares `SRC-009011` with `SRC-009013`; both are witnesses to the same transmitted Caraka tradition, not independent confirmation of physiology.
- **Why disagreement persists:** translation, textual history, and historical anatomy/physiology are entangled, and no current critical edition or specialist semantic study was opened.
- **Known consensus / safe floor:** “brain” is not literal wording in the checked Sanskrit clause.
- **Adopted wording:** `CLM-009011` states only the negative witness comparison; positive meaning remains disputed.
- **Unresolved gap / review trigger:** current critical Sanskrit plus an Āyurvedic Sanskrit/history-of-medicine review of the clause.

### `DIS-009001` — How fixed were early Buddhist oral texts?

- **Question:** how should composition, memorization, recitation, improvisation, and change be related in accounts of early Buddhist transmission?
- **Positions:** Allon argues for composition into substantially fixed, memorized texts with transmission change; his full article identifies McGovern's improvisational and Shulman's creative/formulaic alternatives.
- **Evidence / source relationships:** `EVD-009007` / `SRC-009015` documents Allon's argument and his presentation of the alternatives. The counter-readings were not independently opened, so they are not additional verified sources in this packet.
- **Why disagreement persists:** different models weigh formula, parallel witnesses, oral performance, and creativity differently.
- **Known consensus / safe floor:** surviving canonical prose is crafted and transmitted rather than a tape-recorded transcript, and transmission includes both stability and change.
- **Adopted wording:** attribute the fixed-text mechanism to Allon; use only the safe floor for cross-packet synthesis.
- **Unresolved gap / review trigger:** open McGovern and Shulman in full and add an independent specialist synthesis.

### `DIS-009003` — How should modern *unmāda*/psychosis mapping be described?

- **Question:** is “psychosis” a useful modern comparison, an English translation, or an historical identity?
- **Positions:** Weiss and Kulamarva et al. explicitly map *unmāda* to psychosis/related disorders; the checked ancient category spans multiple faculties and explanatory registers that do not establish one-to-one identity.
- **Evidence / source relationships:** `EVD-009019` documents the modern mapping through `SRC-009002` and `SRC-009014`; `EVD-009009` documents the ancient chapter's broader organization through `SRC-009011`/`SRC-009013`. The modern sources depend on the ancient tradition and are not independent ancient witnesses.
- **Why disagreement persists:** retrospective diagnostic comparison and historical translation answer different questions, while modern clinical categories and ancient textual classifications have different boundaries.
- **Known consensus / safe floor:** the mapping is modern; historical identity is not established by the opened evidence.
- **Adopted wording:** `CLM-009018` says modern authors map the terms and explicitly rejects silent identity/translation.
- **Unresolved gap / review trigger:** a dedicated history-of-psychiatry comparison using a current critical Caraka edition and explicit diagnostic-historiography method.
