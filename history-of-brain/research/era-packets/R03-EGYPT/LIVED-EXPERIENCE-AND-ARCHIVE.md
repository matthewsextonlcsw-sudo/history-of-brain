# R03-EGYPT lived experience and archive

Status: internal evidence note; not public prose.

## Voice-proximity scale

The packet distinguishes a person’s own surviving words from a literary speaker, a practitioner’s case, a household authority’s report, and modern analysis. None is automatically more “true”; each answers a different question.

## Evidence profiles

### `EVD-003010` — UC 16244: a woman's appeal for her daughter

- **Object / locator:** UC 16244, First Intermediate Period; UCL translation lines 1–6 ([record](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/hu/letterdead.html)).
- **Speaker position:** a woman addresses Nefersefkhi and asks for help regarding harm to her daughter. UCL's headline identifies her as a widow, while the transliteration begins `snt Ddt n sn.s` and translation line 1 says “A sister speaks to her brother.” This is one unresolved source/translation ambiguity, not two independently established identities.
- **Experience documented:** grief, perceived injustice, pain, family responsibility, exchange/obligation between living and dead, and an attempt to act under constrained circumstances.
- **Mediation:** the object preserves a written ritual appeal; the named speaker may not have physically written it. English wording is a modern institutional translation whose translator is not named on the page. Gardiner/Sethe 1928 and Wente 1990 are references and must not be silently assigned as the webpage translator.
- **Representativeness:** one surviving letter. UCL’s wider overview lists only about 15 letters to the dead across a long period (`SRC-003015`); scarcity is an archive fact, not proof that the practice or concerns were rare.
- **Empathy rule:** center the woman’s protective action and uncertainty. Do not turn the event into a colorful example of “superstition.”
- **Claims supported:** `CLM-003010`; archive limit `CLM-003013`.

### `EVD-003011`–`EVD-003012` — the *Dialogue*: distress in a literary composition

- **Object / locator:** Berlin P 3024 joined conceptually with Amherst III fragments; TLA persistent ID `N3YSOH4QK5EINPHSB5F2WSDRXI` (`SRC-003016`); Amherst fragments Amh. Egy. Pap. 3 (`SRC-003019`).
- **Text locators:** Nederhof lines 11–19, 103–130, 131–155 (`SRC-003017`).
- **Experience represented:** conflict with the `ba`, inability to find a trustworthy companion, misery, social corruption, and recurring images of death.
- **Genre:** literary debate/poetry, copied by a scribe. The speaking “man” is not securely identifiable as a historical patient.
- **Dispute:** Parys, pp. 217–218, shows that the title/tradition of a “life-weary” suicidal man began with an influential interpretation; the text does not explicitly state an intention to take his life (`SRC-003018`).
- **Archive fracture:** the beginning is missing; Amherst/Mallorca-related fragment work changes the narrative frame. The papyri entered European/American collections through antiquities-market histories rather than a secure modern excavation.
- **Empathy rule:** take the misery and isolation seriously without converting a literary speaker into a diagnosis, spectacle, or “first suicide.” If used publicly, include a self-harm-sensitive editorial review and a plain explanation that interpretation is disputed.
- **Claims supported:** `CLM-003011`, `CLM-003013`.

### `EVD-003013` — Heqanakht’s wife: harm seen through household power

- **Object / locator:** Letter II, Met 22.3.517; discovered by Met excavators in 1921–22 in the tomb of Meseh within TT315 ([object record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545446?od=on)).
- **Text locators:** Allen’s translation, Letter I verso 13–16, printed pp. 15–16; Letter II 38–44, p. 17; analysis pp. 109–110 (`SRC-003021`).
- **Experience represented:** people in the household are ordered not to keep a friend, hairdresser, or domestic from the wife, are accused of doing “bad things,” and are told to respect her; Allen infers harassment and possibly verbal abuse. “Isolation” may be a synthesis inference from denied access but is not the direct translated wording.
- **Voice imbalance:** her husband defends her while also describing the household and its resources as under his authority. Her own account, name certainty, wishes, and assessment of his intervention do not survive.
- **Interpretive dispute:** earlier studies inferred sexual advances from difficult phrases; Allen rejects that grammar. Public wording must not upgrade uncertain harassment to sexual violence.
- **Empathy rule:** name the evidentiary asymmetry. Do not cast Heqanakht simply as a modern heroic husband; he is also the commanding property/household authority through whom the episode is recorded.
- **Claims supported:** `CLM-003012`, `CLM-003013`.

### `EVD-003000` — the unnamed injured person in Case 6

- **Object / locator:** Edwin Smith Papyrus recto II.17–III.1; Breasted OIP 3, pp. 164–171 (`SRC-003001`).
- **Experience represented:** a body is examined after catastrophic head injury; the text tracks visible tissue, movement, bleeding, stiffness, prognosis, and what the practitioner will or will not do.
- **Experience missing:** no name, age, cause of injury, pain description, consent, family, social position, emotional response, disability course, or outcome.
- **Empathy rule:** refer to “the injured person/patient in the case,” not merely “a skull.” Avoid reenactment gore. Prognostic refusal (“not to be treated”) should be contextualized as a textual category, not celebrated as clinical detachment.
- **Claims supported:** `CLM-003000`–`CLM-003001`.

### `EVD-003009` — child mummies and modern research ethics

- **Defined sample:** 21 people who died as children, now in seven museums in Switzerland, Germany, and Italy; 19/21 date mainly to 664 BCE–395 CE, and seven have unknown archaeological sites (`SRC-003012`, Materials and methods, “Materials,” first three paragraphs; Table 1).
- **Research ethics recorded by authors:** minimal destructive sampling, respectful handling, and explicit acknowledgment that visualizing human remains has individual and cultural sensitivities (`SRC-003012`, “Materials,” first paragraph beginning “Twenty-one mostly intact bodies…”).
- **Archive/market effects:** for case 21, the Discussion paragraph beginning “Older textiles were reused…” says the mummy/coffin ensemble may reflect ancient reuse or early nineteenth-century antiquities-market assembly; the next paragraph, beginning “This sample was not appropriate for a demographic evaluation…,” bars demographic inference.
- **Empathy rule:** these are deceased children, not props illustrating a bizarre technique. Do not reproduce CT or mummy imagery automatically. Image use requires a dignity review, clear research purpose, rights check, non-sensational caption, and attention to Egyptian/community perspectives on display and repatriation.
- **Claims supported:** `CLM-003008`–`CLM-003009`, `CLM-003013`.

## Archive filters

### Survival and genre

- Papyrus survives selectively in dry environments; stone and mortuary objects are overrepresented.
- Technical compilations preserve author/copier/practitioner categories, not necessarily what occurred in every encounter.
- Mortuary texts and amulets disproportionately represent people with resources and formal burial investment.
- Literary compositions preserve culturally intelligible themes but not prevalence or autobiographical fact.
- A surviving letter is not automatically typical; its material preservation can make an unusual event look culturally central.

### Literacy, status, gender, age, disability

- Most voices reach the archive through scribes, officeholders, institutions, or object patrons.
- Women appear in UC 16244 and the Heqanakht archive, but only the former supplies a grammatical first-person appeal; even that may be scribally mediated.
- Children in this packet are primarily bodies studied by modern researchers, not speaking historical subjects.
- Injured or impaired people are described through practitioners’ observations. Their self-understanding, accommodations, long-term life, and family care are absent.
- Lower-status, enslaved, mobile, rural, and nonliterate people are largely invisible, although they may appear indirectly in household lists or unnamed cases.

### Collection and colonial history

- Papyrus Ebers was bought in Luxor in 1873 and transferred to Leipzig; original archaeological context is limited (`SRC-003004`).
- The Berlin/Amherst *Dialogue* witnesses passed through nineteenth-/early twentieth-century collections; Morgan acquired Amherst material in 1912 (`SRC-003019`).
- BM EA498 was donated by an English aristocrat in 1805 and lacks modern excavation documentation (`SRC-003009`).
- OIM E17478 was purchased in 1943 and lacks provenance (`SRC-003008`, p. 189).
- The Heqanakht papyri are comparatively stronger contextually because they came from a documented Met excavation, but that excavation and removal are still products of twentieth-century foreign archaeology (`SRC-003020`).
- European museum mummy samples reflect collecting and preservation choices, not ancient population structure (`SRC-003012`).

### Translation and retrospective diagnosis

- Translation choices can turn a body/interior term into “heart,” a `ba` into “soul,” or a poetic death image into suicidality.
- Terms should not be treated as stable modern clinical constructs across medical, literary, theological, and mortuary genres.
- Similarity to modern symptoms is not diagnostic identity. No modern label should appear as the historical subject’s diagnosis without an explicit “modern comparison” frame and evidence review.

## Public-use red lines for the eventual outline

- Never call a literary speaker, mummy, or unnamed case a “patient voice” without explaining the mediation.
- Never diagnose a historical person from a phrase, poem, object, or corpse.
- Never use “superstition,” “primitive,” “crazy,” “barbaric,” or comic mummy imagery.
- Never infer a civilization’s cognitive theory from organ removal alone.
- Never reproduce human-remains images for decoration or traffic.
- Never say “Egyptians believed” when the evidence is a single object, text, author, place, period, or selected sample.
- When discussing harm, preserve agency where visible and name whose voice is missing.
