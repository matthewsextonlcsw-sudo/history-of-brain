# R15-EARLY-MODERN-ENGLISH-CASEBOOKS lived experience and archive audit

## Bottom line

These records can recover **traces of encounters and concerns**, not transparent private minds. Their unusual value lies in how often they name subjects, querents, ages, relationships, status, places, consent formulae, requests and practitioner actions. Their central limit is equally important: Forman, Napier or an associate wrote the note; proxies often supplied information; the chart and practitioner-directed questioning shaped the judgment; and modern editors selected, normalized, translated and encoded the searchable layer.

“Direct” in this packet means direct access to the historical record or facsimile, not direct access to a person's experience.

## Family, household and community mediation

- Sixteen of 26 sample records have a proxy querent, and one combines self- and proxy questioning. Spouses, parents, mistresses, a daughter, a cousin and unnamed messengers appear.
- A proxy is not automatically supportive or coercive. CASE41111 records a daughter asking for a comforting letter and possible physic with her mother's consent; CASE48779 records a husband asking without his wife's consent and supplying hostile allegations. Both belong in the same archive without collapsing into one family-care story.
- CASE5155 names a father for an eight-year-old. It proves a father's recorded action, not the child's wishes, the quality of care, or a general family norm.
- CASE53754 says Ralph Winterton's father sent for him, inside a recorder-supplied study/employment explanation. That can show family involvement in one record, not verified causation or benevolent outcome.
- Servant cases place household hierarchy next to consultation: Dorothy Headlowe's and Harry Mauldon's mistresses ask without consent. This is evidence of recorded authority and proxy practice, not enough to reconstruct either servant's experience.

## Consent, refusal and coercion

The sample has four explicit yes, seven explicit no and fifteen unrecorded consent fields. These categories must not be modernized.

- `cum consensu` and `sine consensu` are historical formulae mediated by practitioner and editor.
- The editorial guide says consent/knowledge attributes are applied only with explicit evidence. Missing attributes are therefore Unknown, not tacit agreement.
- The guide also notes a working hypothesis that consent language for very young children may sometimes have tracked presence. This prevents universalizing the field as autonomous authorization.
- CASE671 is especially useful because knowledge and will split: Thomas Bridgman reportedly knew the urine was being taken but it went against his will. Knowledge is not consent.
- CASE8280 contains both with-consent and without-consent formulations while the XML encodes no. The contradiction is retained, not silently harmonized.
- CASE18910 adds a different contradiction: the transcription's brother layer conflicts with the XML's externally inferred female `#a2` querent. The packet keeps the sample classification at proxy but does not resolve the proxy's identity.
- No record in this sample supports a claim about modern capacity assessment, informed disclosure, privacy, legal consent, or ethics compliance.

## Gender, age, status and access

The 24 distinct encoded subjects comprise 14 female and 10 male people. This is only the composition of a deliberately selected sample.

- Recorded women appear as self-querents, proxy querents, subjects of proxies, relatives, a servant, a knight's daughter, married and unmarried people. “Women were passive” is not an adequate description of these cases.
- Men appear as servants, children, husbands, scholars, proxy relatives and absent subjects. Their records are also relational rather than uniformly autonomous.
- Ages are recorder/editor fields that the edition itself tells users to treat cautiously. The sample includes an eight-year-old, a fifteen-year-old and adults described from 18 to 50.
- Status appears when the record names maid, servant, child, knight's daughter, bachelor, MA/commoner or place/relationship. It is unevenly recorded and selected here on purpose.
- People who could not afford, reach, trust, know about or choose these practitioners are not visible through this sample. No equal-access, class-distribution or underserved-population claim is supportable.
- Sex in the XML is a modern controlled field derived from the record. The sample contains no evidence adequate for reconstructing gender identity beyond historically recorded names/roles and editorial sex categories.

## Distress, emotion and historical language

The packet retains exact historical terms but separates speaker layers:

- `troubled in mind` or `disquieted in mind` can be the brief question wording recorded by Napier.
- `melancholy`, `mopish`, `distracted`, `mad`, `lunatic`, `sottish`, `light-headed`, `despair`, `tempted` and `grief` do not form one stable category across cases.
- Some labels appear in a proxy's representation, some in recorder prose or chart notes, and some only as editorial topic facets inferred from untranscribed material.
- CASE32140 is the cleanest layer warning: the visible question says `disquieted in minde`, while haunted/witchcraft are additional topic metadata.
- CASE44800 shows explicit recorder hostility. The subject should not be redescribed using the insult; the wording is evidence about the record and practitioner stance.
- CASE44800 also contains a date-layer conflict: XML normalizes 30 April 1617 as Wednesday, while the transcription preserves the Venus symbol and glosses it Friday. The calendar date is retained, but the weekday is unresolved.
- CASE13315's `animo` / `the mother` translation belongs to early modern body-mind language and copying history, not a modern diagnostic checklist.
- Spiritual attributions are reported historically without endorsing them as causes or mocking them as superstition.

## Self-harm and trauma-aware gate

The sample includes several records selected to test how the archive handles reported self-harm risk. They require minimum necessary detail, no cliffhanger framing, and no claims about motive or diagnosis.

1. CASE8280, CASE18910 and CASE19524 are one Matthew Altham event chain, not three incidents. Variation in consent, explanatory language, and CASE18910's transcribed-versus-encoded proxy identity is the main finding.
2. CASE12907 uses an attribution marker and describes fear near water; fear is not proof of an attempt.
3. CASE31759 contains despair/harm wording and a recently born child in the same entry. Temporal proximity does not authorize a postpartum diagnosis or cause.
4. CASE18216 concerns a fifteen-year-old and uses the editorial self-harm cluster. No fuller voice or outcome is available in the transcribed question.
5. Public drafting should omit operational detail, avoid spectacle, and pair any discussion with archive mediation and uncertainty.

## Treatment, payment, comfort and outcome

- Eight selected records carry treatment information, two financial information and three subsequent-event information.
- Those are edition presence markers in 24 question-only records. The packet did not paleographically transcribe the lower-page text and cannot state what happened merely from the facet.
- CASE41111 is transcribed in full apart from chart/astrological notes and records a request for a comforting letter and possible physic. It does not record that Napier replied or provided either.
- CASE46520 records a requested purge and treatment/sigil facets. It supplies no efficacy or safety evidence and is held from how-to treatment use.
- The project reports a lost separate Forman treatment/payment notebook. Missing treatment notation can therefore be recordkeeping loss, not absence of advice.
- No sample record supports a general effectiveness, adherence, payment burden, recovery or harm rate.

## Material and digital archive

- All 27 selected page downloads were visually opened. Entries appear among neighboring cases, charts, calculations, deletions, cramped columns, faint writing and blank spaces.
- Twenty-four records encode at least an active or deleted astrological figure; CASE46520 and CASE48779 do not. This is a material-record fact, not a belief statistic.
- Only the editorially defined question is fully transcribed in 24 records. CASE41111 and CASE48779 are marked whole-entry with the edition's chart/astrological-note exception.
- Digital topics are retrieval aids. They may represent normalized historical wording or editor inference from material outside the displayed question.
- Forman's pre-1596 and post-1603 records are lost. The project describes a likely missing Napier volume from autumn 1613 to spring 1614. Wider silence cannot be inferred from either gap.
- The XML, website, transcription and image are not independent sources. They are different access layers for the same records.
- Image rights remain route-specific: the Casebooks permissions page states CC BY-NC 3.0 for manuscript downloads; CUDL manifests state CC BY-NC 4.0 for downloadable images/metadata and separately reserve Bodleian zoom-image rights. All images remain on a commercial-publication hold. Public drafts should link to case pages and shelfmarks unless reuse is separately cleared.

## Who remains missing

- subjects whose case was asked by another person and whose own words were not marked;
- people who declined or never knew about a proxy consultation;
- people who sought help elsewhere or nowhere;
- poor, mobile, institutionalized, linguistically marginalized or otherwise unrecorded people not identifiable through this sample;
- details in untranscribed judgments, treatments, payments and subsequent-event notes;
- harmed or helped outcomes not later recorded;
- practitioner assistants and messengers whose roles the edition cannot securely identify;
- alternate understandings that the practitioner did not write down;
- a defensible account of how representative any selected distress term was.

## Ethical publication rules

1. Attribute every distress word to subject, proxy, recorder, translator or editor when knowable.
2. Prefer “the record says/represents” to “she was/he believed.”
3. Do not repeat degrading or intimate allegations for color.
4. Never infer consent, treatment, payment, outcome or belief from silence.
5. Do not show self-harm or treatment pages as decorative artifacts.
6. Keep modern diagnoses and treatment advice out of historical case summaries.
7. Preserve one-event and repeat-person dependencies in any count.
8. Link to the edition; obtain separate permission before commercial image reuse.
