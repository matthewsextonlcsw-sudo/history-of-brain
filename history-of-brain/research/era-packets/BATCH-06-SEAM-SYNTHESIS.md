## Assembly decisions

### 1. Enslaved letters preserve authored agency and heavy mediation at once

The St. Thomas microcorpus documents named enslaved people writing, dictating, translating,
petitioning, organizing, and addressing distant political and religious authorities. These
records support bounded history of distress, kinship, bodily and emotional language, linguistic
agency, resistance, and religious repair. They do not establish a comprehensive Caribbean
healing system, diagnosis, treatment efficacy, unanimous petition consent, or transparent
autograph voice.

Every item must retain author, writer, dictator, translator, language, copy, recipient,
missionary note, archive locator, and modern diplomatic-edition layer. Damma's African-language
text remains unidentified; the original facsimiles, translation adjudication, Herrnhut and DBNL
rights, and USVI-rooted/descendant review remain public gates.

### 2. Utica demonstrates why a promising archive can still stop

Phebe B. Davis's complete 1855 publication and Utica's Eleventh Annual Report passed source and
image replay. They preserve an outside former-patient argument and an official institutional
account. The packet's third frozen voice, the complete 1855 volume of *The Opal*, did not pass:
the lawful Google witness is physically discontinuous and the cataloged complete Hathi witness
was inaccessible through ordinary routes.

Excerpts cannot impersonate the frozen complete volume. The packet therefore records a useful
acquisition failure, exact witness gaps, derivative dependencies, and what may later be tested;
it does not become an outline node by quietly shrinking its evidentiary promise.

### 3. Dullunda's reports expose administration, labor, and family routes—not patient experience

The Dullunda packet separates the 1858 legal design from 1867, 1869, and 1872 administrative
evidence. It can support bounded claims about certification and custody routes, visitor action,
institutional labor and revenue, expenditure, mortality, release, and relatives' or friends'
maintenance obligations. It cannot establish implementation from statute alone, voluntary or
coerced labor, treatment benefit, family motive, prevalence, or direct affected-person voice.

The source audit rejected a similarly titled but nonmatching report instead of absorbing it.
Mediated Cuttack speech found in the broader parent run is recorded as an out-of-scope trace, not
converted into Dullunda testimony. The absence of direct Dullunda quotation remains visible.

### 4. Jamaica's official counter-dossier is an argument about credibility

The 1860 Ann Pratt official dossier is a constructed colonial source object, not an impartial
investigation and not Pratt's original pamphlet. It preserves elicited examinations, reported
patient experiences, worker evidence, administrative defense, hostile credibility framing,
mediated family traces, and bounded procedural agency. These layers can support a history of how
evidence, coercion, care, gender, race, class, labor, reputation, and authority were contested.

The packet does not adjudicate intimate allegations, infer witness race, generalize one scandal
to Jamaica or the Caribbean, or claim the inquiry's later conclusions. Pratt's original
*Seven Months* and the 1861 commission folios remain unopened and held.

### 5. Nicolat's publication is direct authorship without becoming a complete case history

Alberto Nicolat's deliberately published book is a rare affected-person source for what he chose
to argue publicly. It can be compared, at bounded levels, with Ríos Molina's source map and
current AHSSA service and policy records. It does not independently verify every biographical,
criminal, clinical, family, ancestry, or institutional event.

The complete Oneto thesis, consequential La Castañeda case-file folios, item-specific AHSSA
access/publication determinations, and independent Spanish and specialist review remain absent.
Generic archival language about Indigenous ancestry does not identify a Nation, community, or
language and cannot support cultural interpretation. Raw patient data and case images remain
excluded.

## Cross-packet findings

### “Patient voice” is not one evidence category

Self-publication, self-writing, dictation, translation, petition, elicited examination,
institution-supported periodical writing, clinical quotation, administrative paraphrase, and
reported family or worker speech require different evidentiary treatment. Direct authorship does
not make every factual assertion independently verified; institutional capture does not erase
all agency.

### Work records do not reveal consent or therapeutic value

Sewing, workshops, printing, farming, outside labor, tailoring, production totals, and revenue
may document activity and institutional economy. Without evidence about choice, refusal,
coercion, pay, injury, meaning, and outcome, they do not establish occupational therapy,
rehabilitation, exploitation in every instance, or therapeutic success.

### Law, report, inquiry, and policy are different acts of authority

An enacted statute establishes legal design. An annual report represents an institution. An
official dossier organizes a defense and investigation. A current archive policy describes
general service conditions. None alone proves implementation, truth of every reported event,
item-specific access, historical consent, or reform outcome.

### Print can create agency and control simultaneously

Enslaved letters, Davis's book, Nicolat's book, *The Opal*, and *New Moon* belong to different
print and manuscript economies. Authorship, literacy, audience, editing, institutional support,
reputation management, commercial printing, archive survival, and later digitization all shape
whose words become recoverable.

### Source stops are part of the historical method

An inaccessible complete volume, unopened thesis, unavailable case folio, missing facsimile, or
unfinished issue census is not permission to substitute snippets or later summaries. The
project records the stop, preserves the opened lanes, and waits.
