# R14 East Africa / Swahili Coast — Lived Experience and Archive Audit

**Status:** internal research control; no public prose

## Bottom line

The opened corpus contains **no direct pre-1800 affected-person account** of distress, healing, coercion, treatment or outcome. It contains material traces; later oral historiology; language reconstruction; later descriptions of illness/misfortune and specialist associations; official living-heritage protocol; and manuscript/collector histories. Empathy here requires refusing to manufacture a wearer's belief, a patient's voice, or a community consensus from those traces.

## People at greatest risk of being invented or flattened

### The unknown object wearer

An object labeled an amulet is not a biography. No opened full source establishes who made it, who wore or carried it, whether it was worn, what the inscription meant to that person, whether protection was intended, or whether illness was involved. Public narrative must not turn the object into a patient story.

### People categorized in later vitio accounts

The opened synthesis reports bodily symptoms and socially/morally framed danger. It does not preserve a direct person's description of their experience, choice, fear, relief, stigma or result. Modern psychiatric language would add a diagnosis the archive does not contain. Sexual and gender context makes sensational framing especially harmful.

### People subject to accusation, ordeal or restricted associations

The broader later archive includes accusation, oaths/ordeals, guarded knowledge, fees and unequal entry. That is a warning against romanticizing community care as automatically voluntary, equal or safe. This packet does not have the direct testimony needed to describe coercion rates or individual outcomes.

### Women, young people and lower-status members

Later sources describe gendered associations and elite/fee distinctions, but the strongest pre-1800 reconstruction is not a balanced testimony archive. Women, young people, outsiders, nonmembers and people unable to pay do not reliably assess the institutions in their own words here.

### Poets, scribes, interpreters and private holders

Collectors did not create the Swahili manuscript archive alone. Named coastal knowledge workers copied, interpreted, selected and adapted texts, while private collections were scattered, hidden or lost. Archive prose must credit those contributors and treat European repository possession as one custody layer, not cultural ownership.

## Translation and source-position controls

- Govi's available words are mediated through Spear's English translation; the original ChiMijikenda audio was not opened.
- Bresnahan's historical claims move among reconstructed lexical forms, later oral traditions, archaeology, colonial-era writing and modern interviews. Each layer must remain dated.
- Uganga should remain in the text with a source-specific explanation. Translating it simply as “medicine” removes political, productive, artistic, protective and ancestral senses.
- Samsom's author/commission/date account is a later scholarly transmission, not a contemporaneous Pate record.
- The claimed 1728 witness is reported through later scholarship, challenged, and missing; it cannot be silently treated as inspected.
- The SOAS viewer did not expose item metadata through available access. Search snippets are not a manuscript description.

## Colonial and collection mediation

1. European missionaries, colonial officials and scholars shaped which Mijikenda oral traditions were elicited, translated, excerpted and archived.
2. Spear's synthetic publication made traditions visible while reducing individual variants; the original language survives in audio that requires repository and community replay.
3. European manuscript collecting generated commissioned copies adapted to client preferences and dispersed local intellectual work into Berlin, Hamburg, London and other repositories.
4. Political danger, non-return by researchers, weak storage and private-catalog gaps shaped what survives. The accessible corpus is not neutral survival.
5. UNESCO and State Party representation is valuable for protocol but does not replace the authority of individual kaya councils or current knowledge holders.

## Sacred, restricted and custodial gates

The following are **link-only / do not disclose** without direct authority review:

- exact kaya entry routes, restricted site locations, ritual instructions, recipes or objects;
- archived ChiMijikenda audio or extended translated testimony before repository rights and community/descendant authority are checked;
- archaeological object images or inscriptions before repository accession, custody and image rights are verified;
- private/local manuscript images, extended text or provenance before holder/custodian permission;
- SOAS manuscript images or metadata not exposed by the authoritative catalog layer.

## Empathy and wording rules

- Lead with what a source records and who controls the narration.
- Describe bodily symptoms, actions and categories before offering interpretation.
- Say “later-recorded tradition,” “reconstructed,” “the author proposes,” or “traditionally attributed” at the first relevant sentence, not in a distant caveat.
- Avoid “superstition,” “primitive,” “crazy,” “mental patient,” “witch doctor,” “tribe,” or a mocking contrast with modern medicine.
- Do not romanticize every community practice as care; preserve fees, hierarchy, guarded access, accusation and possible coercion when directly sourced.
- Do not make silence sound serene. The archive's failure to preserve someone's voice is a research limit, not evidence that they accepted, benefited from or resisted a practice.
- Do not use literary battle, grief or religious adversity as therapy evidence without a critical edition, language review and genre analysis.

5. **Across all settings:** direct affected-person testimony where it lawfully exists, without assuming the absence can be repaired by imaginative narration.
