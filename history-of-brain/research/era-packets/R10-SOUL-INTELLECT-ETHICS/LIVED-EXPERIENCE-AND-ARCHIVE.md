# R10-SOUL-INTELLECT-ETHICS lived experience and archive

Status: internal archive/ethics audit; not public prose.

## Archive verdict

This packet does **not** recover patient experience. It recovers elite arguments about modeled souls, bodies, intellects, rulers, prophets, and ethical aspirants. Even where a text describes sensation, emotion, sleep, habit, or self-awareness, the represented figure is constructed for an argument. No one here is speaking in a clinical encounter, petitioning an institution, describing a treatment received, or evaluating whether care helped.

That limit must remain visible. A lively public history cannot compensate for missing voices by inventing what a reader, student, patient, woman, servant, or religious minority “must have felt.”

## What survives between author and reader

### Manuscripts and editions

The al-Kindī corpus is the clearest warning. Most of the philosophical materials used here descend through a single Istanbul manuscript (`EVD-014001`). A modern reader may encounter a confident English taxonomy after multiple acts of copying, selection, editing, and translation. That does not invalidate the text; it changes the kind of certainty available.

The ACO scans preserve twentieth-century Cairo editions, not ancient autographs. They permit page and work verification but do not replace critical editions, manuscript comparison, or accountable translation. In the al-Ghazālī chain, the scan confirms the printed page ranges but does not independently corroborate an English paraphrase (`EVD-014022`).

### Translators and disciplinary framing

Walzer, Rahman, McGinnis/Reisman, Adamson/Pormann, and modern SEP authors make the arguments legible in English. Their translations and categories also mediate them. English “soul,” “intellect,” “imagination,” “estimation,” “heart,” and “spirit” can sound more stable than the source vocabulary warrants. Translation authority must appear at the point of use, not only in an end bibliography.

### Copyists, patrons, students, and communities

The opened texts imply social worlds of manuscript copying, court/patronage, teaching, debate, and religious interpretation, but this packet did not source those actors' own perspectives. A copyist's labor, a patron's selection, a student's confusion, a woman's reading, a Christian or Jewish Arabic reader's adaptation, and an ordinary worshipper's response remain largely outside the retained archive. They must not be silently absorbed into an author's voice.

## Care, control, and empathy controls

1. **Do not call ethical prescription care without evidence.** Al-Ghazālī's training of character can be described as a moral program. It is not a therapy session, clinical protocol, or outcome study.
2. **Do not pathologize revelation.** Al-Fārābī and Ibn Sīnā philosophize about prophecy. Terms such as psychosis, hallucination, delusion, seizure, or dissociation are not supported by this packet.
3. **Do not turn organ theories into ridicule or triumph.** Al-Fārābī's directly opened heart hierarchy, López-Farjeat's specialist-mediated brain account, and Ibn Sīnā's brain-cavity model deserve clear historical explanation. Neither should become a “they knew nothing” joke or a “they discovered neuroscience” victory story.
4. **Do not invent consent or satisfaction.** Repetition, discipline, governing anger, or governing desire may imply authority over a person, but the selected texts do not disclose who submitted voluntarily, who resisted, or what consequences followed.
5. **Name religious and philosophical plurality.** “Arabic-writing Muslim author” is sometimes relevant; “Islamic view” is not an adequate substitute for geography, work, genre, school, and disagreement.
6. **Do not retrofit diagnoses.** Similarity in a word or symptom-like description does not establish continuity with a DSM/ICD category or current neuroscience.
7. **Do not use the absent person as scene decoration.** A fabricated medieval “patient” fearing treatment would be empathetic fiction, not history. If public storytelling uses a composite or dramatization later, it must be explicitly labeled and separated from evidence.

## Missing voices and evidence classes

No direct, source-verified testimony was retained from:

- a person seeking help for fear, sadness, agitation, unusual perception, sleep disturbance, memory difficulty, or cognitive disability;
- a recipient of moral or spiritual discipline describing coercion, relief, failure, belonging, or change;
- women, children, enslaved people, servants, artisans, soldiers, rural residents, or poor people responding to these ideas;
- Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, or other Arabic-reading communities engaging the four authors;
- scribes, translators, physicians, teachers, jurists, household caregivers, shrine workers, or hospital staff describing application;
- students or critics outside elite later philosophical reports;
- material objects, marginalia, reading notes, petitions, letters, court files, case notes, institutional accounts, or treatment registers linked to these specific ideas.

The absence is produced by the packet boundary, preservation, access, editions, literacy, language competence, and source selection. It is not evidence that such people were passive or silent in their own lives.

## Vignette status

No patient-centered vignette is approved. The only possible public scene anchors after review are textual/material scenes:

- the single manuscript and modern editor behind al-Kindī's compact taxonomy;
- al-Fārābī's body/city analogy, clearly labeled as argument;
- Ibn Sīnā asking a reader to imagine a floating person, clearly labeled as a thought experiment;
- al-Ghazālī's prescriptive account of habit, clearly labeled as ethical formation.

Each scene must immediately disclose mediation and must not supply invented feelings, dialogue, treatment, or prevalence.
