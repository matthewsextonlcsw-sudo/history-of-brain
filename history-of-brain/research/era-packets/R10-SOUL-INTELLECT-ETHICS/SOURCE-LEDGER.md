## Scope and search record

This is a deliberately small comparison, not a survey of “Islamic psychology.” It follows four named Arabic-writing authors and four different textual projects: al-Kindī (ninth-century Baghdad/Iraq intellectual and career context; no birthplace claim is made here), al-Fārābī (probably born around 870 in Farab or Farayb, with reliable early biographical information scarce; later active in Baghdad and Syria), Ibn Sīnā (Afshana/Bukhara and later eastern Iranian/Central Asian court networks), and al-Ghazālī (Tus, Nishapur, Baghdad, and later travel contexts). Their Muslim identities, Arabic prose, and overlapping philosophical vocabulary do not make their claims interchangeable. Birthplace, career location, language, ethnicity, political geography, and anthology classification are separate facts. Christian, Jewish, Andalusi, North African, Egyptian, later Sufi, Persian-language, and hospital/practice histories require other packets.

- Ratings follow the project protocol: L1 direct/authoritative evidence; L2 specialist scholarship or accountable critical translation; L3 authoritative reference/scholarly synthesis; L4 accountable general synthesis; L5 discovery lead; LX unusable. Claim fit is A direct, B strong indirect, C context, D mismatch.
- Agent Reach health check found Jina Reader available and Exa unavailable. Jina opened the full reachable Stanford Encyclopedia entries and direct PDFs/pages; built-in primary-source web search was the discovery fallback. Snippets, abstracts, publisher blurbs, and restricted metadata were leads only.
- Arabic image scans were opened page-by-page. No independent Arabic translation is claimed. English arguments are grounded in named translators or specialist descriptions, with the Arabic edition used to verify work, edition, title, and printed pagination.
- `opened_by` = R10 packet researcher and `opened_on`/`accessed_on` = 2026-08-18 for all records. `archive_url` = none unless the lawful research cache is named. Sacred claims require attribution as the author's philosophical/theological account, not endorsement, diagnosis, or evidence of supernatural causation.
