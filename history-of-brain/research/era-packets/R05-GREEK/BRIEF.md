# R05-GREEK — Brain, Soul, Regimen, Sanctuary, and Plural Healing

**State:** Authorized; research only

**Owner:** One research session; this folder only

**Identifier range:** `SRC/EVD/CLM-006000–006999`

## Research question

How did selected Greek-speaking communities, medical writers, philosophers, sanctuaries,
households, laws, and sufferers describe mind, brain, soul, emotion, distress, difference, and
care—and why did naturalizing arguments not replace ritual or plural healing?

## Boundary

Greek-speaking Mediterranean contexts, approximately 800 BCE–300 CE, with each source tied to
its date, place, genre, school, and transmission history. Roman imperial synthesis and Galen
belong to `R05-ROMAN`, a later packet; use only necessary handoffs.

## In scope

- *On the Sacred Disease* in reliable Greek/translation and its rhetorical context;
- Hippocratic plurality rather than a single authorial system;
- *psychē*, *thymos*, brain, heart, humors, regimen, emotion, and conduct as changing terms;
- Asclepian sanctuary evidence, inscriptions, dreams, offerings, and healing claims;
- philosophical traditions only where they materially shape mind/emotion/care questions;
- household, gender, slavery, citizenship, cost, pilgrimage, and healer choice where evidenced;
- patient speech or reported experience with mediation visible; and
- the later invention of a clean reason-over-superstition origin story.

## Out of scope

- all Greek philosophy;
- Rome, Galen, or late antiquity except explicit handoffs;
- treating “the Greeks” as one view;
- equating ancient categories with epilepsy, psychosis, depression, or anxiety without a
  carefully bounded comparison; and
- public prose.

## Required evidence targets

- critical editions/translations and exact passages for core textual claims;
- archaeological, epigraphic, or catalog evidence for sanctuary practice;
- specialist history of medicine, classics, philosophy, and religion scholarship;
- competing interpretations of *On the Sacred Disease* and naturalization;
- evidence concerning sufferers, households, enslaved people, women, and non-elite access where
  the archive permits; and
- upstream checks for “birth of psychiatry,” “first brain theory,” and superstition-to-science
  narratives.
