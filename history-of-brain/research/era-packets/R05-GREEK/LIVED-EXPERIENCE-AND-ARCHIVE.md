# R05-GREEK — Lived experience and archive

**Purpose:** identify what the surviving record lets the project say about sufferers, families,
caregivers, healers, and institutions—and where empathetic narration must stop.

## Archive map: whose words survive?

| archive stream | surviving form | proximity to experience | institutional filter | safe use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Epidaurian *iamata* | sanctuary-curated third-person inscriptions, including named people and represented dialogue | May preserve visitor-originating details, but the earlier-inscription/object/oral source-layer reconstruction is attributed and not independently verified (`EVD-006015`); the surviving form is not a transcript. | Sanctuary personnel selected, adapted, and publicly inscribed narratives; miraculous success and skeptical conversion are recurring forms (`EVD-006012–EVD-006013`, `EVD-006017`, `EVD-006027`). | Say that a person “is represented as” suffering, dreaming, speaking, offering, or recovering. Treat efficacy as a sanctuary claim. |
| Isolated votives and dedications | objects, body-part images, short inscriptions | Materially close to an act of dedication; motive, symptom, sequence, and feeling often absent. | Archaeological survival, collecting, excavation, and museum cataloging determine what is visible (`EVD-006019–EVD-006020`). | Describe object, date, provenance, inscription, and catalog inference separately. Do not turn an ear or leg into a diagnosis. |
| Hippocratic cases | compressed medical observation and prognosis in a recorder's voice | Often close to severe illness and day-by-day change, but rarely preserves the sufferer's own account. | Recorder selects signs, household identifiers, treatment, crisis, and outcome; ancient compilation and nineteenth-century translation add layers (`EVD-006021–EVD-006023`). | Describe signs, chronology, and labels used by the source. Do not call these interviews or infer private feelings. |
| Medical/philosophical treatises | authorial argument, classification, polemic, dialogue, or prescription | Evidence for elite conceptual models and professional rhetoric, not ordinary belief or treatment uptake. | Genre, pedagogy, competition, copying, attribution, and translation shape the record (`EVD-006000–EVD-006011`, `EVD-006025–EVD-006026`). Regimen lead `EVD-006024` is translation-held. | Attribute every model to a work/authorial voice and resist population-wide claims. |

No opened core source is an unmediated diary by a distressed person. That absence is itself a
feature of the archive, but it does **not** mean ancient sufferers had no voice, agency, private
explanations, or criticism.

## Care relationships and coping visible—carefully

- **Seeking help and traveling:** sanctuary narratives presuppose journeys, incubation, sleep,
  companions, carrying, and offerings. These are represented care practices, not proof that every
  visitor could travel, enter, sleep safely, or pay.
- **Family and proxy care:** the Arata story represents a mother dreaming for an absent daughter;
  other cases identify people through households. It is evidence that relational care mattered in
  the narrative archive, not a measure of caregiver burden or authority.
- **Daily regimen is held:** Humanitext exposes Littré-derived Greek data, but the packet's English
  list of food, drink, exercise, baths, clothing, and seasonal adjustment came through machine
  rendering/summary (`EVD-006024`). Do not use that list in the outline until a named human
  translation or independently reviewed project translation is documented.
- **Patient participation:** *Epidemics* says the patient should combat disease with the physician
  (`EVD-006021`). Treat this as a normative sentence, not documentation of consent, equality, or
  a universal therapeutic alliance.
- **Dreams and meaning:** sanctuary dreams are integral to the cure narratives. They show how an
  institution represented divine contact and bodily change; they are not direct access to a
  dreamer's phenomenology.

## Power, selection, and archive loss

1. **Sanctuary authority:** public cure collections favor successful, legible stories and can
   transform skepticism into proof. Failed cures, disappointment, disagreement, exclusion, and
   ordinary improvement are much harder to see (`EVD-006013`, `EVD-006017`, `EVD-006027`).
2. **Medical authority:** *On the Sacred Disease* defines competitors in hostile terms
   (`EVD-006001`); *Epidemics* determines whose symptoms and outcomes count as recordable
   (`EVD-006022–EVD-006023`). Rival healers and sufferers rarely answer in their own words.
3. **Household and status:** enslaved and dependent people can appear through owners, employers,
   fathers, husbands, or masters. Their visibility in elite texts does not establish free choice,
   equal treatment, or legal agency.
4. **Material survival:** stone and durable objects privilege institutions and dedications that
   were carved, deposited, excavated, collected, and cataloged. Perishable household practices,
   spoken complaint, informal care, and unsuccessful treatment are systematically undercounted.
5. **Translation:** “madness,” “servant,” “slave,” “soul,” “mind,” and “understanding” can hide
   contested Greek categories. A modern critical translation and Greek check are required before
   these terms become public quotations.

## Missing or weakly represented voices

- People who sought no formal care, could not travel, or were excluded from a sanctuary or
  physician.
- People whose cure failed, whose symptoms returned, or who criticized care without being turned
  into a cautionary skeptic story.
- Enslaved people speaking in the first person; low-income visitors; noncitizens and migrants;
  rural households; children outside institutionally authored dialogue.
- Family caregivers, birth attendants, nurses/assistants, temple laborers, and the rival ritual
  practitioners attacked by medical authors.
- People now described as disabled or neurodivergent whose ancient self-understandings cannot be
  reconstructed from body-part votives or acute fever cases.
- Local languages and communities interacting with Greek-speaking institutions, especially in
  Caria, southern Italy, Egypt, the Levant, and wider exchange routes.
- Everyday emotional life, coping, friendship, grief, violence, confinement, and care outside the
  narrow philosophical, medical, and sanctuary record opened for this packet.

## Empathetic public-language guardrails

- Begin with the person and documented predicament, not spectacle: “The inscription represents
  Ambrosia as arriving with impaired sight and skepticism,” not “a bizarre miracle cured a blind
  woman.”
- Name mediation every time it matters: “the sanctuary says,” “the medical recorder observed,”
  “the catalog infers,” or “a later author attributes.”
- Describe documented pain, sleeplessness, fear, dependence, or bodily limitation without
  inventing how “thrilled,” terrified, grateful, coerced, or satisfied someone felt.
- Do not use “crazy,” “insane Greeks,” “superstitious patient,” or diagnosis-as-metaphor. When an
  old translation says “madness,” pair it with the Greek term and source context.
- Give unsuccessful treatment, professional competition, institutional self-promotion, and
  structural silence space alongside celebrated cures.
- Do not imply that ancient suffering was less real because categories differ, or that modern
  treatment is the uncomplicated endpoint of a progress story.

## Archive verdict for outline use

The packet can support a section on **represented lived experience and institutional mediation**.
It cannot yet support a section claiming to reconstruct “what Greek patients felt” in general.
The strongest scene-level anchors are Kleo/Ambrosia/Euphanes as sanctuary representations and the
socially marked *Epidemics* cases as medical representations. Every one should be framed with its
narrator, genre, transmission, and missing voice.
