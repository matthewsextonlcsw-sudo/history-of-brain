# R13-KOREA-JAPAN-MEDIEVAL lived experience and archive audit

State: internal ethics/archive review; no public prose

## Governing finding

This packet does **not** recover medieval Korean or Japanese patient experience in a direct sense. It recovers how one Buddhist narrative and one learned prescriptive compilation represent illness, distress, authority, and intervention. Patient experience must not be invented to make the history feel empathetic. The honest empathy move is to name who is acted upon, what the record says happened, who controls the account, what harm is prescribed, and what the archive does not let us know.

## Korean lane — `惠通降龍`

### People represented

| Person/group | What the selected text says | Who controls the account | What remains absent | Editorial consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tang princess | She is ill; male authorities refer the matter; Hyetong performs an incantatory bean ritual; a dragon flees; the story says illness ends (`EVD-021003`) | Iryeon's late-thirteenth-century compilation; emperor and male religious figures direct the plot | Name, age, symptoms, words, fear, wishes, refusal, diagnosis, duration, cost, safety, follow-up, independent outcome | Introduce as “the story's unnamed princess,” not a clinical case; do not write satisfaction or cure as fact |
| Silla royal daughter | She suddenly becomes ill; a royal order directs Hyetong to treat; the story reports recovery (`EVD-021005`) | Compiler, king, officials, monk | Name, symptoms, assent, method, duration, adverse effects, follow-up | State royal command and textual silence; do not infer voluntary care |
| Jeong Gong's wife and children | Their punishment is lifted after Hyetong's intervention in the political sequence | King/compiler | Their speech, conditions of punishment, losses, response to pardon | Do not turn their release into a physician-centred success beat |
| People harmed by the dragon/bear spirit | The narrative says lives are harmed and “the people” suffer | Compiler and monk-centred plot | Names, bodies, coping, household response, complaint, care access | No prevalence or community-experience claim; plural suffering is a narrative device without a denominator |
| Hyetong | Detailed agency, travel, ordeal, ritual action, moral authority, confrontation with state violence, and office | Hagiographic/Buddhist narrative favourable to his authority | Independent witnesses, ordinary practice record, payment/access information | Treat as represented religious authority, not a licensed modern therapist or verified clinician |

## Japanese lane — *Ishinpō* scroll 3, chapters 21–23

### Persons in the prescriptive text

The chapters do not present named Japanese patients. They assemble generic person-types from quoted works. Behaviours and experiences are visible only through authoritative categories: confused speech, unusual hearing/seeing, agitation, collapse, running, wakefulness, hunger change, self-exaltation, anger, joy, grief, crying, abuse, and spirit-language (`EVD-021008`–`EVD-021012`).

This material can show what learned authors/compilers classified and prescribed. It cannot tell us how a recipient described an episode, what it meant within a family, whether a procedure occurred, whether the person resisted, or whether it helped.

## Contact passage — medicine-taking chant

The `服藥頌` passage addresses a person taking medicine and gives a bodily/religious formula plus a direction to face east and recite once before ingestion (`EVD-021016`). It offers no named user, setting, provider, reason for taking medicine, literacy mode, oral performer, consent, cost, or result. Even “self-care” is too strong: the passage does not say whether the user recited it personally, was instructed, or heard it from another person.

The phrase `新羅法師方` is a transmitted attribution, not a recoverable Silla patient's voice. Korean scholarly and popular retellings that turn the citation into a complete national medical book or broad practice are reception evidence, not lived-experience evidence (`EVD-021017`–`EVD-021019`, `EVD-021028`).

## Archive-production audit

### Who made or preserved the records

- A male Buddhist compiler and later male-oriented religious/court authorities dominate the Korean narrative.
- A male court compiler and earlier named learned works dominate the Japanese chapters.
- Later manuscript owners, scribes, print editors, national institutions, and modern scholars determine what is accessible.
- The consulted Korean translation on individual NIKH records does not name a translator, which prevents attribution of its interpretive choices at the record level.
- No accountable modern translation was opened for the selected *Ishinpō* chapters; the packet therefore does not authorize polished English quotations or term equivalences.

### Who is visible

- Named male monk/compiler/official figures are biographically and politically legible.
- Royal women are visible as bodies acted upon but not as narrators.
- Generic people in *Ishinpō* are visible as symptom/behaviour bundles and intervention targets.
- Communities appear as aggregate sufferers without names or records.

### Who is missing

- Patient-authored letters, diaries, petitions, complaints, refusals, songs, or oral testimony.
- Ordinary caregivers, women healers, nurses/attendants, household members, pharmacists, itinerant specialists, ritual practitioners, and disabled people's accounts.
- Poor, rural, migrant, enslaved, stigmatized, criminalized, or institutionally confined people.
- Material practice records: prescriptions actually filled, payment, inventories, injuries, follow-up, or outcomes.
- Korean vernacular and Japanese vernacular recipient language; the surviving learned texts use Classical Chinese/kanbun.

These are gaps in the selected archive, not proof that the people or practices did not exist.

## National-canon and colonial-reception audit

The medieval materials precede modern Korean/Japanese nation-states and modern colonial categories. “Korea,” “Japan,” “China,” “Silla,” and “Tang” are necessary locating terms, but modern national ownership must not be projected backward without qualification.

Risks detected:

- Shin 1995 and popular descendants infer a complete, indigenous, advanced Silla medical book and active international exchange from fragments preserved in a Japanese compilation. Those conclusions are historically meaningful examples of national medical canon-building, but the fragment does not by itself prove them (`EVD-021017`–`EVD-021019`, `EVD-021028`).
- A mirror-image Japanese exceptionalist story could call *Ishinpō* a purely national invention or the “first Japanese psychiatry.” The chapters visibly depend on continental works, and the mental-health priority definition/global survey is absent (`CLM-021011`, `CLM-021016`, `CLM-021026`–`CLM-021028`).
- Calling the Japanese material timeless “Kampo” can retroactively clean away ritual, hemerological, Buddhist, and spirit-language elements (`CLM-021014`).
- Calling the Korean lane “Buddhist psychotherapy” can turn hagiographic authority into modern professional lineage and obscure women, court power, and unverified outcomes (`CLM-021005`, `CLM-021015`, `CLM-021021`, `CLM-021025`–`CLM-021027`).
- The packet found no colonial-period source in scope. It therefore cannot make claims about Japanese colonial medicine, Korean resistance, or later nationalist professionalization. Those require a separate packet.

## Empathy and language controls

- Say “the narrative represents,” “the chapter classifies,” or “the text prescribes” before describing an event or procedure.
- Keep `癲` and `狂` in characters at first use and explain the historical body/text context. Do not silently translate them as modern diagnoses.
- Do not call incantation “placebo,” “primitive,” “superstition,” “psychotherapy,” or “mindfulness.” Each imports an unsupported hierarchy or clinical mechanism.
- Do not describe cold-water exposure, binding, burning, bloodletting, or intimate-body treatment as benign traditional wellness.
- Do not presume recipients were passive; state that their response is not preserved.
- Do not presume recipients were satisfied, terrified, cured, or harmed in a delivered encounter. Distinguish what is prescribed/narrated from what is unknown.
- Do not use dragon or ghost language for spectacle without explaining genre and the coexistence of bodily explanations.
- Do not turn “the earliest extant medical book in Japan” into “the first mental-health system.”
- Do not merge the Korean and Japanese lanes into a civilizational relay. The only contact check is the `新羅法師方` attribution, and its route is unresolved.

## Archive-ready conclusion

The packet can support an outline about representation, textual classification, transmission, and authority. It cannot support a general account of how medieval Korean or Japanese people with distress felt, coped, consented, accessed care, or fared. That limitation belongs in the eventual narrative rather than being repaired with invented patient colour.
