# R13-SOUTH-ASIA-MEDIEVAL — findings

**Purpose:** internal outline foundations, not public prose
**Status:** `PASS WITH GAPS` after independent closure; internal reconciliation only; public gate closed
**Boundary:** three named settings only; “medieval” is not a universal South Asian period label

## Packet answer in one sentence

The sources do not reveal one medieval South Asian theory of mind: a Sanskrit diagnostic chapter classifies *unmāda* within heterogeneous medical and being-affliction categories, a Tamil inscription records tightly endowed institutional care without patient voices or mental-health indications, and a Tamil devotional poem represents embodied suffering/rapture/social mockery through a lyric voice whose date, authorship, and translation require qualification.

## Setting 1 — the *Mādhavanidāna* chapter 20 diagnostic corpus

### Evidence anchors

- `EVD-019100`, *Mādhavanidāna* 20.1, printed p. 59 (continuing into the p. 60 sequence): the received Sanskrit text calls *unmāda* a `mānasa vyādhi` within a *doṣa*-based explanation. This anchors a history of classification, not a modern diagnosis (`CLM-019201`).
- `EVD-019101`, 20.2–5, p. 60: *doṣa*-related classifications, `mānasa duḥkha`, and poison sit within the same diagnostic sequence.
- `EVD-019102`, 20.15–34, pp. 62–64: *graha*/being-affliction categories and modeled conduct appear later in the chapter. The text does not fit a simple medicine-versus-religion ladder (`CLM-019202`).
- `EVD-019103`, *A Literary History of Medicine* 12.1 n.9: modern editors propose c. 700 CE and identify later Arabic reception. The proposal does not locate composition precisely or prove continuous practice (`CLM-019204`).

### Responsible outline topics

1. **A chapter, not a civilization.** Introduce the text, received edition, Sanskrit terminology, approximate date, unresolved place, and diagnostic genre before paraphrasing any category.
2. **Classification without forced equivalence.** Show how one chapter orders several kinds of explanation, while leaving *unmāda/mānasa/duḥkha/graha* untranslated or multiply glossed until specialist review.
3. **Learned architecture versus encounter evidence.** Make explicit that chapter 20 supplies typology and signs, not named patients, family accounts, bedside decisions, refusal, coercion, or outcomes (`CLM-019203`).
4. **Transmission as a separate story.** The c. 700 proposal and Arabic bibliographic reception can form a later transition, but not an “India invented and exported psychiatry” claim.

### Transition out

Use a question, not a continuity assertion: “What changes when the archive moves from a prescriptive Sanskrit chapter to a dated Tamil institution's expenditure record?” The answer is a change in source position—from elite diagnostic taxonomy to sponsor administration—not proof that the latter implemented the former.

## Setting 2 — the Tirumukkūḍal Tamil inscription of Vīrarājendra's sixth regnal year

### Evidence anchors

- `EVD-019104`, Ayyar pp. 220–221: a 55-line Tamil wall inscription with Grantha characters, copied at the temple and dated palaeographically/regnally to the late eleventh century.
- `EVD-019105`, Ayyar p. 223 and translated ll. 42–45: care is explicitly connected with inpatients and the temple/college community—servants, teachers, and students. Wider admission remains unknown (`CLM-019206`).
- `EVD-019106`, Ayyar pp. 223–225, 249–250: fifteen inpatient rations, a named prescribing physician, surgical and medicine-preparation roles, two attendants, barber, lamp, water, medicines, and differentiated compensation (`CLM-019205`).
- `EVD-019107–108`, Wujastyk pp. 21–22 and printed p. 28 (PDF p. 30): this is the sponsor's financial view; it gives patronage/funding, not the patient-experience density available in other archives (`CLM-019208`).
- `EVD-019114`, Somasundaram et al. 2014: the authors admit the epigraph lacks indications, then infer mental treatment from other texts. The inference is retained as modern reception, not medieval fact (`CLM-019207`; `DIS-019401`).

### Responsible outline topics

1. **What was endowed.** Present the specific recorded allocations, exact site, date, and roles. Use “records,” “funds,” and “provides for,” because implementation is not independently observed.
2. **Who appears and who does not.** A named physician and differentiated workers appear; patients remain unnamed. Role/pay differences are visible, while caste, gender, household resources, and consent are not.
3. **Access was not demonstrably universal.** The beneficiary language is institutionally bounded. Do not market the site as a free public hospital for all classes.
4. **Mental-health inference audit.** Explain the difference between a medicine listed in an inscription, an indication in another text, and evidence that a named institution used it for that indication. Only the first is directly documented here.
5. **Care and power together.** Patronage made beds, food, staff, light, water, and stocks possible; the same administrative source leaves patients without speech. This is an empathy point, not a reason to invent their feelings.

### Transition out

Move from recorded care to represented inwardness with a genre warning: “The inscription tells us what a sponsor allocated. A devotional poem can sound more intimate, but its ‘I’ is a crafted voice, not automatically a clinical case or transparent autobiography.”

## Setting 3 — received *Tiruvācakam* poem 4 and its transmission

### Evidence anchors

- `EVD-019109`, Pope 1900, Hymn IV, pp. 32–34: the English-facing edition represents the speaker through desire, poverty, weeping, movement, bodily quivering, mockery, rapture, and longing.
- `EVD-019110`, Project Madurai/GRETIL poem 4 lines 60–73: the romanized Tamil includes `uḷam`, body-quivering language, `cakam pēy eṉṟu tammaic cirippa`, and `maṉam`; these cannot be replaced silently with modern diagnostic labels (`CLM-019209–210`).
- `EVD-019111`, Nasti pp. 7 and 23–26: p. 26 says the ninth-century view is now widely held, while individual evidence does not firmly date the figure; much wider dates and single-authorship questions remain (`CLM-019211`).
- `EVD-019112`, Nasti p. 44: sustained suffering and timing of exuberance complicate the standard “spontaneous ecstatic outpouring” frame (`CLM-019213`).
- `EVD-019113`, EAP1217/1/480: an eighteenth-century palm-leaf witness remains at Tamil University, Thanjavur; it evidences later transmission/custody, not original composition (`CLM-019212`).

### Responsible outline topics

1. **The lyric speaker is not a chart.** Describe the poem's first-person representation and bodily/emotional vocabulary; do not diagnose the poet.
2. **Translation is part of the history.** Pope's poem-4 “demon” rendering belongs to a 1900 missionary scholarly edition. Put the Tamil term, translator, date, and caution next to any quoted English. “Madman” and “maniac” occur in Hymn III and are outside the selected poem-4 evidence.
3. **Suffering and exuberance co-exist.** A literary-affect section can resist both sentimental “pure bliss” and pathologizing “madness.” Attribute Nasti's tragic-focus argument as one specialist reading.
4. **Social response appears, but prevalence does not.** The poem represents mockery by “the world/people.” It does not establish a universal policy, persecution regime, or tolerance level.
5. **Received corpus versus original moment.** Preserve ninth-century working placement, authorship/date disputes, later canonization/reception, and eighteenth-century manuscript witness as separate stages.

## Exchanges and transmission without a civilizational relay race

| Chain | What is supported | What is not supported |
|---|---|---|
| Sanskrit *Mādhavanidāna* → later Arabic bibliographic/translation network | modern critical editors identify *Nidāna* and report quotation/probable translation; c. 700 is proposed | exact translator route, complete Arabic text survival, geographic origin, clinical uptake, or linear “East to West” progress |
| Tamil inscription → modern hospital histories | Ayyar published the epigraphic edition; Wujastyk and Somasundaram use the same chain | multiple independent ancient confirmations or proof of mental-health indication |
| Received Tamil poem → 1900 Pope edition → Project Madurai/GRETIL and modern scholarship | exact modern edition/transcription layers and later scholarly readings are visible | authorial autograph, unmediated ancient meaning, or uninterrupted unchanged reception |
| Palm-leaf witness → Tamil University → BL EAP digitization | eighteenth-century material witness, local original custody, digital access route | British ownership of original, ninth-century dating, or representative performance history |

## Regional, linguistic, and period cautions

- Do not use “medieval” as if 600–1500 were a single emic period across the subcontinent. Give dates and local/corpus labels.
- The *Mādhavanidāna* setting is named by corpus, not assigned to a region without evidence.
- Tirumukkūḍal is one Tamil temple-college-hospital site near Kanchi; it is not “the Chola healthcare system” or “South Indian public medicine.”
- *Tiruvācakam* is a Tamil Śaiva received corpus with living sacred significance; it is not evidence for all Tamil speakers, Śaivas, women, lower-status groups, or households.
- English “mind,” “mental,” “emotion,” “disease,” “demon,” “mad,” “care,” “nurse,” “surgeon,” and “hospital” all mediate older terms and roles. Public use requires passage-specific translation notes.
- Sanskrit, Tamil, Grantha-script forms, English colonial translation, Arabic reception, and modern digitization are distinct layers; no layer counts as independent corroboration merely because it uses another language.

## Candidate outline transitions (internal only)

1. **From categories to budgets:** a diagnostic chapter orders types; an inscription orders grain, cash, work, light, water, and beds.
2. **From intended care to missing experience:** administrative precision can coexist with silence about consent and outcome.
3. **From patient-shaped absence to lyric-shaped presence:** the devotional “I” offers affective vocabulary, but genre blocks its conversion into a case history.
4. **From old text to later meanings:** Arabic reception, colonial translation, digital transcription, modern psychiatry, and current scholarship each make new claims; their dates and dependence must stay visible.
