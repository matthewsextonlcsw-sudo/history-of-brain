# R13-SOUTH-ASIA-MEDIEVAL — lived experience and archive audit

**Status:** internal ethical/archive review; no public reconstruction approved
**Core finding:** the packet contains a prescriptive medical voice, a sponsor-administrative voice, and a lyric devotional voice. None is direct patient testimony.

## Required social-axis audit

### Caste and status

- Tirumukkūḍal explicitly names an institutional ecology tied to a temple, Sanskrit college, teachers, students, servants, and specialized workers. That supports a status/access boundary, not a full caste map.
- Differentiated paddy, cash, and land allocations show an occupational hierarchy. They do not identify workers' caste, freedom/dependence, hereditary status, or bargaining power.
- The physician's name and land benefit are preserved while patients and most workers are not. This asymmetry is evidence about the administrative archive's priorities, not proof that patients were socially insignificant.
- *Mādhavanidāna* offers no recoverable caste/status distribution for the modeled afflicted people in the selected chapter.
- The poem's self-lowering and social-mockery vocabulary is devotional rhetoric; it cannot be converted into legal/caste status without separate evidence.

### Gender and women

- The Ayyar translation used for the primary inscription says two attendants/nurses; it does not establish their sex. The later 2014 article labels medicine-preparation workers male and administering nurses female, but the packet did not verify that gender assignment against the Tamil epigraphic terms. Public gender claims are held.
- No woman patient, family caregiver, birth-related care, or woman's first-person experience is recovered in these three settings.
- Poem 4 includes conventional erotic/gendered imagery in Pope's translation, but the first-person speaker is not a population sample and the packet did not perform a gender/sexuality study of the corpus.
- The absence is archive-bounded: “not recovered here,” never “women were absent from care/devotion/medicine.”

### Labor, dependence, and material conditions

- Tirumukkūḍal is unusually concrete about grain/cash/land, beds, food, herb gathering, fuel, medicine preparation, water, laundry/barber work, light, and attendance. This can ground a material history of care work.
- Provision does not equal delivery. The record does not document working hours, injuries, coercion, caste obligations, compensation receipt, worker voice, or household consequences.
- The fifteen-bed/ration figure is a funded capacity, not an occupancy, admission, recovery, or mortality count.
- *Tiruvācakam* poem 4 represents poverty, wealth, desire, and social response poetically; it is not a household budget or labor history.

### Poverty, cost, and access

- The inscription records endowment and institutional rations, but it does not say whether patients paid, who qualified, whether poor outsiders entered, or what opportunity costs families bore.
- “Free,” “charitable for all,” “public,” “universal,” and “equal access” are prohibited.
- The beneficiary list makes institutional membership visible. It cannot prove complete exclusion either; outsider access remains Unknown.

### Disability and dependence

- No disability category or long-term dependence record is securely translated in the selected sources.
- Inpatients imply temporary or sustained need for care, but severity, impairment, accommodation, and support after discharge are absent.
- Do not transform described conduct in *Mādhavanidāna* or bodily states in the poem into modern disability identity.

### Coercion, consent, and refusal

- No source records consent to diagnosis, medicine, ritual, admission, or nursing.
- No source records refusal, confinement, restraint, punishment, involuntary treatment, or escape.
- Absence cannot establish either humane consent or coercion. Public prose must state “not recorded in this source” when those questions matter.
- The poem's social mockery can support represented stigma-like response only at the level of this lyric; it does not establish institutional control.

### Pain, distress, hope, and coping

- *Mādhavanidāna* supplies learned terms/signs rather than a person's account of what distress felt like.
- Tirumukkūḍal supplies material provision without feelings.
- *Tiruvācakam* poem 4 gives a rich represented voice of melting/weeping/quivering/longing and mockery. It can be discussed empathetically as lyric representation, but not relabeled a clinical symptom inventory or “coping strategy.”
- Nasti's interpretation usefully keeps suffering and exuberance together; it does not prove how historical listeners felt or whether recitation improved wellbeing.

### Outcomes and efficacy

- No recovered setting provides treatment response, recurrence, adverse events, mortality, satisfaction, or comparative outcomes.
- Medicine stock, nursing allocation, diagnostic classification, devotion, and textual survival are not efficacy evidence.
- No historical practice should be recommended for current care from this packet.

## Archive mechanics and power

### *Mādhavanidāna*

- Core Sanskrit work → later commentaries → 1943 printed edition → public-domain scan → modern OCR/search.
- Each layer privileges learned textual transmission. Manuscript variants, regional use, oral practice, and patient speech are outside this packet.

### Tirumukkūḍal

- Eleventh-century wall inscription → 1915–16 epigraphist's copy → 1931–32 ASI edition/translation → modern hospital histories → digitized scan/OCR.
- The inscription was made to administer/endow and memorialize. Its precision reflects sponsor priorities.
- Wujastyk explicitly identifies the sponsor view and the patient-experience gap (`EVD-019107–108`).
- Somasundaram et al. show how later psychiatric history can move from medicine names to inferred mental treatment despite the epigraph's missing indications (`EVD-019114`). The packet keeps that inference visible and quarantined.

### *Tiruvācakam*

- Received Tamil corpus → later manuscript/canonical transmission → 1900 Pope bilingual edition → Project Madurai/GRETIL transcription → modern literary scholarship and EAP digitization.
- Pope's Christian/missionary comparisons and diagnostic-sounding English are part of the reception archive.
- EAP1217/1/480 is an eighteenth-century witness at Tamil University, not an ancient autograph. Local custody must remain prominent.
- The corpus is living sacred literature. Legal public-domain status of a 1900 edition does not remove ethical duties around translation, quotation, and community framing.

## Empathy rules for later writing

1. Describe what the source records before imagining how it felt.
2. When patient voice is absent, say so plainly; do not add a generic suffering patient for narrative effect.
3. Do not ridicule *graha* categories or devotional intensity, and do not validate supernatural/clinical equivalence as fact.
4. Do not call historical people “lunatics,” “psychotic,” “possessed,” or “manic” because a colonial translation or modern article does.
5. Name care labor and material provision without romanticizing the institution.
6. Put access, caste/status, gender, cost, consent, and outcome questions beside any impressive bed/staff list.
7. Attribute poetic experience to the speaker/text; preserve genre and sacred context.
8. Credit Tamil University and local/site heritage stakeholders, not only metropolitan digitizers/translators.
