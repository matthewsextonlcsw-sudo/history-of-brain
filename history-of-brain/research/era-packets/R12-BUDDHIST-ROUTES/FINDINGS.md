# R12-BUDDHIST-ROUTES — findings

**State:** independent closure complete; PASS WITH GAPS for internal reconciliation; no public prose
**Question:** what can three located manuscript/practice chains establish about mind, distress, medicine, and care across South/Central Asian routes without converting “Buddhism” into a timeless psychology?

## Bounded answer

The packet establishes three materially different carrier, witness, or transmission-source
problems. It does not establish three complete routes:

1. a Gāndhārī/Kharoṣṭhī verse collection reportedly recovered near Khotan, whose revised colophon names an owner, teacher, scribe, and monastery but whose place of manufacture and local use remain unresolved;
2. a Sanskrit birch-bark bundle purchased at Kucha, including prescriptive medical language whose modern English renderings must be separated from the source terms and from actual patient experience; and
3. a Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya medicine chapter that survives unevenly in Sanskrit, Chinese, and Tibetan witnesses and preserves normative stories about illness, supplies, labor, and exceptions rather than institutional case records.

Together they support a history of mobile manuscripts, editorial reconstruction, translation, monastic norms, medical recipes, and modern custody. They do **not** establish a single Buddhist discipline called psychology, a continuous clinical therapy, a straight India-to-East-Asia relay, or unchanged “mindfulness.”

### Mind/emotion term anchor

- CKM 77 §15 contains Gāndhārī `maṇasa priaṇi` and `maṇo nivartadi` in a verse about turning or restraining the mind from desired things (`SRC-018001`).
- This is passage-local evidence for `maṇo/manas` and cultivation language in this Gāndhārī witness. It is not evidence that the text was a “psychology manual,” that the practice was performed at Khotan, or that it is identical to modern clinical mindfulness.
- Because the full verse history shares a single editorial chain, later sources cannot be counted as independent confirmation simply by repeating the reading.

### Transition/topic proposed for the master outline

**From northwestern literary production to Tarim manuscript presence:** narrate a portable scroll, named scribal labor, split modern custody, and the difference between where an object was reportedly found and where it was made or used. This is an object-history transition, not a civilizational “Buddhism arrives in China” marker.

### Medicine, distress, and translated categories

- The *Navanītaka* opening says its knowledge benefits men, women, and children, addresses physicians, and restricts transmission to a son, brother, or disciple (`SRC-018006`, printed p. 77). This is normative access rhetoric; it does not demonstrate who actually learned or received care.
- Sahacara-oil verses 329–336 contain Sanskrit `unmāda`, `bhūtopahatacetasam`, and `apasmāra` (`SRC-018007`, vv. 329–336). Hoernle's 1909 English renders these through terms including “insanity,” demoniacal possession, and epilepsy (`SRC-018006`, p. 110).
- Source categories, Hoernle's dated English, and current diagnoses must remain three separate layers. The recipe's claim that an oil addresses these conditions is prescriptive textual evidence, not proof of actual preparation, access, consent, safety, or efficacy.
- The represented patient population is grammatical and prescriptive. No named patient describes pain, fear, satisfaction, resistance, side effects, recovery, or coercion.

### Transition/topic proposed for the master outline

**From portable formulas to a colonial manuscript archive:** use the bundle to show how medicine, monastic affiliation, scribal plurality, Silk Road purchase, changing paleographic dates, dated English diagnostic vocabulary, and Bodleian custody intersect. The historical topic is transmission and archive-making, not “Buddhist medicine cured mental illness.”

### What textual comparison establishes

- Yao's examples show the additional Sanskrit manuscript sometimes preserves material omitted from Gilgit and is often closer to Tibetan or Chinese at particular loci, but it also differs from them (`SRC-018011`, pp. 93–99). The pattern does not form a single linear stemma or prove that either translation copied the extant fragment.
- Differences among surviving Sanskrit, Chinese, and Tibetan witnesses could have existed in Sanskrit transmission before translation. “Translation changed Buddhist psychology” is too broad without passage-by-passage comparison.

### Care passage: represented distress and divided labor

- In 84000's **v1.1.5 Tibetan-based English translation** at 1.70–1.81, Degé fols. 282a–b,
  Pilindavatsa is represented as describing multiple illnesses and pains that he cannot bear. The
  rule narrative authorizes a medicine bag, addresses spoilage/storage, and orders medicine
  movement through roles **rendered by this translation** as “layman,” “novice,” and “monk”
  (`EVD-018115`, `EVD-018116`).
- The note to 1.80 compares Sanskrit `śrāmaṇeraka`, Chinese `求寂`, and Tibetan `dge sbyong` for
  the role rendered “novice.” This is a documented translation choice, not proof that all three
  witness terms have identical social boundaries or that a male lay-worker demographic has been
  independently established.
- The passage ends by separating permissions for illness from ordinary circumstances and attaching an offense to misuse. It therefore documents normative boundary-making around sickness, supplies, status, and labor.
- It does not establish a hospital, a professional social-work role, routine implementation, prevalence, or outcomes. Pilindavatsa's quoted distress is a represented voice within a normative text, not a recoverable first-person diary.
- The sequence makes the translation's ordered roles visible inside a male-monastic narrative.
  It should not be generalized to nuns' communities, all monasteries, a staffing model, or a
  historical labor demographic. No Sanskrit–Chinese–Tibetan collation of this care locus was
  completed (`GAP-018509`).

### Transition/topic proposed for the master outline

**From fragmentary Sanskrit to complete Tibetan survival:** use the chapter to show translation teams, missing folios, variant recensions, normative illness exceptions, and work allocation. Handoff Chinese and Tibetan regional reception to the appropriate East/Central Asian packets rather than treating a translated witness as proof of uniform local implementation.

## Chronological transitions/topics proposed for outline reconciliation

1. **Probably second century CE in Baums's 2014 proposal — portable Gāndhārī cultivation verse:** named manuscript labor; reported recovery near Khotan; uncertain production/use; possible movement separately attributed to Neelis.
2. **Approximately fifth/sixth century — Sanskrit medical compilation at the Kucha archive node:** plural scribes/texts; prescriptions for bodily and disturbing conditions; unstable modern dating and diagnostic translation.
3. **Approximately sixth–ninth century witnesses/translations — medicine chapter as mobile Vinaya:** missing Sanskrit folios, Yijing's partial Chinese translation, and a Tibetan translation that Yao calls ninth-century and 84000 says “seems” to date then; completeness remains bounded to Yao's four-witness comparison.
4. **Nineteenth–twenty-first century — recovery, purchase, split custody, digitization, translation, and psychologization:** colonial collecting and modern disciplinary labels are part of the history of what can now be known.

These transitions are packet proposals only. They require cross-review and reconciliation with R06-SOUTH-ASIA, Central Asian manuscript/archaeology work, R07-EARLY-CHINA, later East/Southeast Asia packets, and modern mindfulness/clinical/neuroscience packets before any public outline or prose.
