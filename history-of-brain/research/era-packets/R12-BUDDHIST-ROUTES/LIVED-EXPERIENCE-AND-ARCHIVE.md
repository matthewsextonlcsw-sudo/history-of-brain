# R12-BUDDHIST-ROUTES — lived experience and archive audit

**State:** independent closure complete; PASS WITH GAPS for internal reconciliation; no public prose

## Bottom line

This packet contains **no securely identified ordinary patient's first-person testimony**. It contains one sufferer's represented speech inside a Vinaya narrative, prescriptive beneficiary categories in a medical compilation, and many elite/monastic/editorial/custodial voices. The safe empathetic stance is to make pain, dependence, hierarchy, and missing agency visible without inventing either benevolent care or patient hostility.

## Status, labor, gender, and power

### Monastic rank and delegated labor

- 84000's Tibetan-based English 1.80 gives an ordered sequence rendered “layman,” then “novice,”
  then “monk” (`EVD-018116`). The claim is about the translation's roles, not an independently
  reconstructed labor demographic.
- The note identifies Sanskrit `śrāmaṇeraka`, Chinese `求寂`, and Tibetan `dge sbyong` for the
  role rendered “novice.” The term choice is editorial and comparative, not proof that every
  witness has identical social boundaries or that the “layman” was necessarily a male lay worker
  in every historical implementation.
- Nothing in the selected passage records how those workers perceived the task, whether they could refuse, or how care labor was distributed in a particular institution.

### Gender

- The Bower medical opening names men, women, and children as intended beneficiaries but presents transmission through a son, brother, or disciple (`SRC-018006`, p. 77). This juxtaposition can support a question about who was treated versus who was authorized to inherit knowledge; it cannot answer the question.
- The selected *Bhaiṣajyavastu* passage is about monks. It provides no basis for generalization to nuns' communities, women caregivers, childbirth, household medicine, or gendered access.
- No named woman speaks in the selected sources. This packet must not turn a grammatical beneficiary (“women”) into lived testimony.

### Physician, monastic, and family authority

- The Bower bundle's association with Yaśomitra does not establish that he authored, copied, dispensed, or tested its recipes. “Owner,” “monk,” and “physician” are not interchangeable roles.
- The medical opening's restriction to son, brother, or disciple is a prescription about authorized knowledge transfer. It may encode professional/familial gatekeeping, but actual circulation needs documentary comparison.
- In *Bhaiṣajyavastu*, rule-authority frames when illness suspends ordinary restrictions. That makes institutional power visible; it does not prove either compassionate flexibility or punitive rigidity in practice.

## Custody, provenance, and access

### CKM 77

- Reported 1892 recovery near Khotan is uncertain in the modern catalog (`SRC-018000`).
- Physical portions are divided between Paris and St Petersburg; Baums's later realignment changed the recoverable colophon (`SRC-018002`). Split custody and editorial access are therefore part of the evidence, not an afterthought.
- The packet has not established the full acquisition trail, current Paris shelfmark, export conditions, excavation authorization, local Uyghur/Khotanese stakeholder views, or image reuse permissions.

### Bower bundle

- The bundle entered scholarship through a British officer's 1890 purchase at Kucha, intermediaries' accounts, Hoernle's inquiries, and later Bodleian acquisition (`SRC-018005`, `SRC-018008`). This is colonial-era collecting, not controlled archaeological excavation.
- The seller's identity, payment conditions, legal/export regime, and local community perspectives remain incomplete in this packet.
- Current Bodleian digitization improves access but does not erase the purchase history or supply cultural permission for image reuse.

### *Bhaiṣajyavastu*

- The additional Sanskrit manuscript is divided between a private Virginia collection and the Schøyen Collection in Oslo, and Yao says discovery details are unclear (`SRC-018011`). Private custody affects provenance, scholarly access, and the ability to reconstruct an archaeological bundle.
- The modern English comes from a nonprofit translation project. Its **undated draft** terms,
  opened 2026-08-18, impose noncommercial/no-derivatives and attribution restrictions; its
  principles name teachers, scholars, and practitioners as stakeholders (`EVD-018117`,
  `EVD-018124`).
- No text-specific restriction was identified for this Vinaya chapter, but its status as a living sacred/legal text requires attribution, careful contextualization, and community-aware review. One organization's protocol cannot stand for all Buddhist communities.

## Archive silences and missing voices

1. **Patients:** no identified recipient of the Bower oil speaks; Pilindavatsa is represented inside a normative text.
2. **Families:** “son/brother/disciple” appears as a transmission rule, not household testimony.
3. **Women and children:** named as beneficiaries, not speakers or documented practitioners.
4. **Lay and novice caregivers:** assigned work, but their motives, burdens, consent, and competence are not recorded.
5. **Local readers:** no direct Khotanese or Kuchean reception evidence was opened for CKM 77 or the Bower bundle.
6. **Scribes and artisans:** Dharmaśrava is unusually named; most hands, bark processors, binders, and repairers remain anonymous.
7. **Translators' collaborators:** some are named, but copyists, oral consultants, patrons, and readers remain incomplete.
8. **Communities near find/acquisition sites:** no Uyghur, Khotanese, Kuchean, Gilgit, Bamiyan, Tibetan monastic, or diaspora community consultation was conducted in this packet.
9. **Poor, enslaved, disabled, rural, and nonliterate people:** no direct voice securely identified.
10. **Treatment outcomes:** no patient-level evidence for benefit, harm, abandonment, or coercion.

## Protocol gates for later writing

- Use an empathetic archive statement whenever illness or disturbing experience appears: who is speaking, who is being described, and who cannot answer back?
- Keep Sanskrit/Gāndhārī/Tibetan/Chinese terms and named translator/date visible when an English word resembles a current diagnosis.
- Do not reproduce manuscript images or extended 84000 translations without a fresh rights and cultural-protocol review.
- Do not identify a “first,” “origin,” “invention,” or continuous practice from these three carriers.
- Request specialist/community review before public discussion of living sacred-text use, colonial custody, or culturally specific possession/illness categories.
- Pair any public treatment claim with an explicit evidence state: prescriptive text, normative rule, represented narrative, or documented practice/outcome.
