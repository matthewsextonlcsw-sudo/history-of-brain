# R07-EARLY-CHINA — Findings

**Use:** source-backed outline foundations; not public prose
**Core:** selected Chu/Warring States manuscripts and received philosophical works, followed by selected Qin–Han medical and legal witnesses, approximately 350 BCE–200 CE
**Gate:** usable for an internal chronological outline with the holds stated below; not ready for public drafting

## Why this is a bounded core

The packet does not attempt “the Chinese view of mind” or a continuous history of “TCM.” It follows several named witnesses whose differences can be dated and explained: the excavated Guodian *Xing zi ming chu*; the Baoshan illness divinations; selected received *Mengzi*, *Xunzi*, and *Guanzi/Neiye* passages; the received *Huangdi neijing lingshu*; the excavated Wuwei medical corpus; and selected Zhangjiashan legal manuscripts. These are separated by witness type, genre, region, institutional purpose, and date (`CLM-010000–CLM-010001`; [source ledger](SOURCE-LEDGER.md)).

## Proposed chronological transitions and topics

### 1. Warring States heartmind, disposition, affect, and formation

The excavated Guodian manuscript *Xing zi ming chu* provides a physical late Warring States manuscript context rather than a received text alone. In Lisa Raphals's edition-based translation, slips 323–25 / XZMC 1–3 describe heartmind as acquiring determinate direction through encounters, pleasure, and habituation, while naming the *qi* of joy, anger, grief, and sorrow as part of *xing*. Slips 324–26 say a way begins in *qing* and *qing* arises from *xing*; later slips describe sound/music entering or stirring heartmind and desire ([Raphals, pp. 6–7, 13–14](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~raphals/pubs/2019%20Body%20mind%20Guodian.pdf)). This supports a story about relational and cultivated formation, not a discovery of stimulus-response psychology (`CLM-010002`).

The Guodian corpus itself should not be presented as doctrinally unified. Raphals reads its texts as ranging across more dualist metaphors and more holistic *qi*-based accounts ([pp. 15–16](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~raphals/pubs/2019%20Body%20mind%20Guodian.pdf)). The physical context is supported by the [Hubei Provincial Museum](https://www.hbww.org.cn/bambooslips/p/11903.html) and [CUHK manuscript project](https://www.history.cuhk.edu.hk/en/elkt/a-database-of-the-chu-bamboo-manuscripts-of-guodian-2001/), but an individual museum record for *Xing zi ming chu* and the full critical plates remain gaps (`CLM-010001`).

The received *Mengzi* offers different normative examples. In 2A6, the sudden sight of a child approaching a well prompts alarm/compassion and becomes evidence in an argument about the beginnings of humane conduct; in 6A15, the text contrasts sense organs overwhelmed by objects with the heartmind's capacity to think ([*Mengzi* 2A6 and 6A15](https://ctext.org/mengzi/gong-sun-chou-i/ens)). These are ethical and political arguments, not controlled observations of universal behavior or modern claims about brain localization (`CLM-010004`).

The received *Xunzi* “Zhengming” passage groups liking, disliking, joy, anger, sorrow, and delight as *qing* and connects heartmind selection to deliberation; a related passage links *xing*, *qing*, and desire ([Chinese witness, anchors `n12625`, `n12638`](https://ctext.org/xunzi/zheng-ming/ens)). Because CText's English is generated, the public English wording is held until a named current human translation is opened (`CLM-010005`). More broadly, *qing* has a disputed semantic chronology and should not be silently standardized as “emotion” ([Seok, SEP §§1–3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotions-chinese/); `CLM-010003`).

### 2. Cultivation is bodily, affective, social, and political—not modern wellness by another name

The received *Guanzi* chapter *Neiye* links calm and disturbance, posture, blood-*qi*, food, thought, music/ritual, and government. The Chinese passages beginning `凡心之刑` and `四體既正，血氣既靜` are available in the [Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/guanzi/nei-ye/ens); R. Eno's named human translation discusses these at pp. 2–6 and 9–12 of [*The Inner Enterprise*](https://dyhr.com/qigong/Neiye.pdf). The source can anchor a segment on embodied self-cultivation and political order. It cannot be relabeled without qualification as mindfulness, self-care, lifestyle medicine, or mental-health treatment (`CLM-010006`).

This also creates a methodological transition: apparently “internal” practices can serve moral and governing projects. A history of coping and wellness must therefore ask who is being trained, for what social purpose, and what the received text prescribes—not only whether a practice resembles something popular today.

### 3. A named sick person appears, but through an elite divination archive

The Baoshan Tomb 2 slips preserve repeated illness divinations concerning the high Chu official Shao Tuo in 318–316 BCE. The records describe an ailment around abdomen or heart, shortness of breath, inability to eat or bad taste, and a rising vapor. Five diviners offer differing causal readings, sacrifices, remedies, and forecasts ([Raphals, pp. 1–7, slips 207, 221, 223, 236–248](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~raphals/pubs/2005baoshan.pdf)). This is unusually concrete evidence of a named person's represented bodily distress and a sequence of care. It also demonstrates uncertainty and disagreement rather than one settled explanatory system (`CLM-010007`).

The empathy rule is to stay close to what survives. Shao Tuo may have been frightened, frustrated, hopeful, or dissatisfied, but the archive does not tell us. It is not his diary or a consultation transcript. The records entered an elite tomb, and even their prior administrative context is uncertain. A modern cardiac diagnosis reported in the scholarship is excluded because the signs do not justify it.

### 4. Medicine and ritual do not form a simple before-and-after story

Miranda Brown traces the modern construction of Physician He as a first secular medical “naturalist” and argues that physicians and ritual experts shared vocabulary, patrons, and social fields rather than following a clean superstition-to-science replacement ([Brown, *Medical History* 56.3](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/medical-history/article/who-was-he-reflections-on-chinas-first-medical-naturalist/094C2D612F16B682A28C0A2435E2C8DE)). Baoshan supplies a concrete reason for caution: bodily signs, divinatory causation, sacrifice, and practical concern coexist in the same archive (`CLM-010014`).

The early Eastern Han Wuwei manuscripts then show real change without validating a total replacement story. Yang and Brown describe 78 documents, largely drug formulae with some acupuncture, found in an early Eastern Han tomb. They find no exorcism or divine mitigation in this corpus and contrast it with selected earlier manuscript material, while also noting restrictions on transmitting remedies ([Yang and Brown, *Early China* 40](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-china/article/wuwei-medical-manuscripts-a-brief-introduction-and-translation/428F79323358B94C190AE4BD7A030D67)). The defensible conclusion is “different manuscript corpora preserve different therapeutic repertoires,” not “religion vanished from Han medicine” (`CLM-010010`).

### 5. Received early imperial medicine clusters affect, body, conduct, and technique

The received *Huangdi neijing lingshu* is composite and layered, not a single author's datable clinical notebook ([Karchmer and Herman, SEP §§1–2](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2025/entries/chinese-phil-medicine/)). In chapter 22, the received Chinese text clusters *dian* and *kuang* with heterogeneous signs: unhappiness, sorrow, fear, anger, forgetting, reduced sleep or hunger, grandiose or abusive speech, uncontrolled laughter/song/wandering, and false seeing/hearing. It prescribes observation and varied needle, bleeding, or moxibustion responses ([Wikisource fascicle 5, chapter 22](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/黃帝內經/靈樞第五卷); comparison [Kanripo `[009-004b]–[009-005b]`](https://krx.kanripo.org/text/KR5d0042/009)).

This can support an outline topic on how received medical texts organized distressing conduct and bodily signs. It cannot support the sentence “ancient Chinese doctors diagnosed bipolar disorder” or “*dian/kuang* means schizophrenia.” The terms must remain visible, the signs must be described, and the received textual layer must be stated (`CLM-010008`, `CLM-010016`).

Jen-der Lee's textual history shows related discourse linking heart, vessels, *shen*, sorrow, joy/laughter, and bodily signs, while emphasizing textual layering and later change ([Lee, pp. 1–10](https://www1.ihp.sinica.edu.tw/storage/people/upload/10_Jender_LEE-Laughing_Disorders-revised_and_updated_Helen-%E5%AE%8C%E7%A8%BF%E7%84%A1%E6%A8%99%E8%A8%98.pdf)). This is evidence about medical writing, not proof that a psychiatric profession existed or that all sufferers received these interventions (`CLM-010009`).

### 6. Emotion enters law and household power through institutional voices

One *Zouyanshu* case records officials weighing a young widow's represented failure to grieve and sexual conduct during mourning. A preliminary judgment treated the conduct as unfilial, but the final decision released her ([Goldin, pp. 16–17, slips 180–196](https://ealc.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/bio/%5Buser-raw%5D/papers/Han%20Law%20and%20the%20Regulation%20of%20Interpersonal%20Relations.pdf)). The turn matters: the source documents contested official reasoning, not a settled general rule. It also does not give the widow's feelings in her own words (`CLM-010011`).

A separate *Ernian lüling* succession provision gives a conditional order. If a deceased household head has no male descendant, succession passes to his father or mother; if neither survives, to the widow; if there is no widow, to a daughter; later named kin follow. Moonsil Lee Kim's dissertation gives the Chinese and translation on [printed pp. 125–126](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8x89q4t1/qt8x89q4t1.pdf). Wu Dongming gives an independent modern translation check of the same Zhangjiashan witness on [printed p. 69](https://brill.com/view/journals/bsms/8/1/article-p57_3.pdf) and cautions against turning such evidence into a universal story of women's equal or “high” status. The two translations are not independent ancient corroboration and do not establish implementation. Jingrong Li further argues from the manuscript's date, selection, errors, size, and production that it was probably made for the tomb owner's funeral rather than official practice ([full dissertation, printed pp. 70–82](https://ediss.sub.uni-hamburg.de/bitstream/ediss/5557/1/Dissertation.pdf)). Formal capacity under constrained conditions is not equality, implementation, or access to healing (`CLM-010012`).

The same article translates two letters excavated from Shuihudi Tomb M4, dated to about 223 BCE. Qin soldiers Heifu and Jing ask their mother for cash and clothing, making material strain and family reliance unusually direct ([Wu, pp. 69–70](https://brill.com/view/journals/bsms/8/1/article-p57_3.pdf)). The letters preserve the men's urgent requests, not their mother's reply. One emphatic statement that without the requested resources the writer will “die” must not be converted into a suicide disclosure or modern diagnosis (`CLM-010017`).

### 7. Phlegm-fire and modern diagnostic mappings belong in later reception, not the core

Natalie Köhle's NYU/ISAW summary states that *tan* 痰 does not occur in the *Neijing*, first appears in the second century CE, and develops into a fuller discourse much later ([“Fluid Fire”](https://isaw.nyu.edu/events/archive/2017/fluid-fire)). This is a strong warning against back-projection but only a lecture summary; the exact chronology remains held pending publication-level verification (`CLM-010013`).

A modern clinical textbook chapter that maps *dian/kuang* and phlegm-fire to bipolar/manic-depression is evidence that such retrospective equations circulate, not evidence that they are historically valid ([Elsevier sample chapter](https://booksite.elsevier.com/samplechapters/9780702029882/Chapter%2019.pdf)). An outline may later examine this translation history as modern reception, but it must not use the mapping to narrate the Warring States or Han past (`CLM-010016`).

## Change over time that the current evidence can support

1. **Late Warring States manuscripts and received arguments:** named terms for heartmind, disposition, affective response, desire, ritual/music, and cultivation appear in different normative configurations; they are not one model.
2. **Warring States elite care archive:** Baoshan shows repeated divination, embodied signs, sacrifice, and disagreement around one named high official.
3. **Early imperial received medical synthesis:** layered *Neijing/Lingshu* discourse clusters body, affect, conduct, *shen/qi*, and intervention, without matching modern diagnostic boundaries.
4. **Early Eastern Han manuscript contrast:** Wuwei's surviving formula/needle corpus differs from selected earlier ritual-medical manuscripts, while transmission restrictions reveal knowledge and access as power.
5. **Law and household:** emotion and conduct could be institutionally interpreted in legal disputes; gendered household capacity existed under bounded statutory conditions; two conscripted men's letters reveal material coping through family requests, while women's replies remain missing.
6. **Later handoff:** phlegm and phlegm-fire require a post-core history; modern diagnostic equations require a reception history.

## Disputes and contrary readings that must stay visible

- **What does *qing* mean, and when?** Scholarly chronologies differ. Do not flatten *qing* into a stable universal category called emotion.
- **How unified are the Guodian texts?** The corpus preserves varying body–heartmind metaphors. Do not manufacture a Guodian school doctrine.
- **Did medicine secularize?** Wuwei's contents show a corpus difference; Brown's work and Baoshan block a simple religious-to-rational succession.
- **What are *dian* and *kuang*?** The received text presents heterogeneous signs. Modern psychiatric labels are interpretive reception, not transparent translations.
- **Did Han law prescribe emotion?** The widow case includes a preliminary moralized judgment and a final release. Neither half may be omitted.
- **When does phlegm become explanatory?** The opened event summary points later than the *Neijing*, but the detailed specialist publication is still required.

## Internal outline gate

The following are ready for a cautious chronological outline: plurality/source layers; Guodian/XZMC relational formation; *qing* semantic caution; *Mengzi* moral examples; *Neiye* embodied-political cultivation; Baoshan as mediated named experience; medicine/ritual interaction; *Lingshu* signs and interventions without diagnosis; Wuwei corpus contrast; the widow's contested legal episode; Qin soldiers' direct household requests; archive silences; modern diagnostic reception.

The following remain held: English quotation of *Xunzi*; strong public quotation of Guodian or *Lingshu* without fuller critical editions; general claims about gender equality or household practice; clinical interpretation of the soldiers' urgent death formula; exact first-appearance chronology for phlegm; claims about prevalence, efficacy, affordability, consent, coercion, satisfaction, or a discrete psychiatric profession.
