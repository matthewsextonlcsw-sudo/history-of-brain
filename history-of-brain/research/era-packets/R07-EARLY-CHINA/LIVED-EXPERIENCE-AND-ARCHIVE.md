# R07-EARLY-CHINA — Lived experience and archive

**Use:** internal empathy, power, and archive control; not public prose
**Rule:** distinguish a person's preserved words from institutional representation, and distinguish a prescription from an experience of care.

## What kind of “voice” survives?

| case / corpus | what survives | whose institution or mediation? | what can be said | what remains unknown |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shao Tuo, Baoshan illness divinations (`EVD-010010–EVD-010012`) | Named elite, repeated dates/consultations, bodily signs, diviners' causes, sacrifices, forecasts, remedies | Divinatory/administrative records copied or deposited in an elite Chu tomb; modern slip reconstruction/translation | A named high official was represented as undergoing prolonged/repeated care amid uncertainty and disagreement | His own words, fear, pain, wishes, consent, satisfaction, trust, adherence, outcome, cost, and the experience of nonelite people |
| Young widow, *Zouyanshu* (`EVD-010019–EVD-010020`) | Officials' account of sexual conduct, mourning/grief, preliminary judgment, and final release | Legal casebook preserved in Zhangjiashan Tomb 247; official and later editorial voices | Her behavior and represented grief became objects of contested official judgment; the final ruling rejected the preliminary disposition | Her private feelings, speech, coercion, economic circumstances, relationship history, and experience of the proceeding |
| Household succession rule, *Ernian lüling* (`EVD-010021–EVD-010022`, `EVD-010026`) | A formal succession sequence in a manuscript that Li argues was probably produced for the tomb owner's funeral | Legal/administrative text copied by scribes; two modern translations of the same witness | Under specified conditions, a mother, widow, or daughter could formally become household head after earlier candidates were unavailable | Implementation, frequency, local variation, social equality, and whether household status changed access to care |
| Heifu and Jing, Shuihudi letters (`EVD-010027`) | Two Qin soldiers' direct requests to their mother for money and clothing | Letters excavated from Shuihudi Tomb M4; modern edition/translation | Material scarcity, conscription, urgency, and reliance on household support appear in petitioning male voices | Their mother's words or response, full household conditions, whether the urgent death phrase was literal, and the emotional experience of other soldiers |
| Wuwei medical manuscripts (`EVD-010017–EVD-010018`) | Formulae, some needle procedures, transmission restrictions, tomb context | Specialist/knowledge corpus in an early Eastern Han burial | Some medical knowledge circulated under access restrictions; this corpus differs from selected earlier ritual-medical manuscripts | Who prepared or received remedies, literacy, price, consent, adherence, outcomes, and the experience of women, servants, enslaved people, children, or poor households |
| *Mengzi*, *Xunzi*, *Neiye* | Normative arguments about heartmind, affect, desire, cultivation, and conduct | Received philosophical/political textual traditions | Elite textual projects conceptualized and attempted to shape affect/conduct in differing ways | Ordinary emotional vocabulary, resistant practice, family negotiation, and whether prescriptions were followed |
| *Lingshu* chapters 8 and 22 | Received medical classifications, signs, observation instructions, and interventions | Layered transmitted medical corpus and later editions | Medical writers organized certain embodied and behavioral signs together and proposed interventions | Named patients, consent, pain, caregiver response, implementation, efficacy, stigma, confinement, affordability, and specialist institutions |

## Case file: Shao Tuo without invented interiority

Raphals's edition-based study identifies slips 197–250 as illness divinations concerning Shao Tuo, a high Chu official, during 318–316 BCE. The records describe repeated bodily difficulties and multiple consultants. Slip 207 includes an ailment around the abdomen and shortness of breath; slips 221 and 223 place difficulty near the heart alongside shortness of breath and inability to eat; slips 236–248 preserve several diviners and differing forecasts and responses ([Raphals, pp. 1–7](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~raphals/pubs/2005baoshan.pdf)).

This is the packet's strongest named-person evidence, but it is mediated. The safest empathetic frame is uncertainty: the person was represented as repeatedly unwell, the specialists did not agree, and care involved both bodily observation and ritual action. It would be tempting to narrate frustration or fear, but no such feeling is preserved. It would also be tempting to call his signs cardiac disease; that retrospective diagnosis is specifically excluded (`CLM-010007`).

## Case file: a widow judged through grief

Goldin translates a *Zouyanshu* case in which preliminary officials treated a young widow's represented lack of grief and sex during mourning as grounds for an unfilial finding. The final decision released her ([Goldin, pp. 16–17](https://ealc.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/bio/%5Buser-raw%5D/papers/Han%20Law%20and%20the%20Regulation%20of%20Interpersonal%20Relations.pdf)).

Both legal turns matter. Quoting only the moralized preliminary judgment would misrepresent the case as settled law; quoting only the release would conceal that officials could interpret a woman's affect and sexuality as evidence. The record gives institutional readings of her conduct, not a transcript of her grief. Public empathy must not invent either callousness or secret devotion (`CLM-010011`).

## Gender, household, status, and access

Moonsil Lee Kim translates an *Ernian lüling* succession provision in a conditional order: if a deceased household head has no male descendant, his father or mother succeeds; if neither survives, the widow; if there is no widow, a daughter; later named kin follow ([Kim, printed pp. 125–126](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8x89q4t1/qt8x89q4t1.pdf)). Wu Dongming provides an independent modern translation check of the same Zhangjiashan witness on [printed p. 69](https://brill.com/view/journals/bsms/8/1/article-p57_3.pdf) while cautioning against a universal high-status story. These are two translations, not two ancient witnesses or evidence of implementation. From the manuscript's likely 186 BCE production, selection, scribal errors, impractical format, and the tomb owner's chronology, Jingrong Li argues that three scribes probably produced it hastily for the funeral rather than official legal work ([Li, printed pp. 70–82](https://ediss.sub.uni-hamburg.de/bitstream/ediss/5557/1/Dissertation.pdf)).

This evidence corrects a simplistic picture of female legal nonexistence, but it does not support equality. It supplies a constrained legal capacity within a gendered succession hierarchy. No opened source connects this provision directly to control of remedies, physician access, legal autonomy in treatment, or ordinary household decision-making. Those are research questions, not inferences (`CLM-010012`).

Status is equally consequential at Baoshan: the best named illness sequence belongs to a high official whose ability to consult several diviners cannot stand for ordinary access. At Wuwei, prohibitions on transmitting remedies suggest knowledge control, but the identities and bargaining power of recipients are largely absent. The archive makes elites and experts visible while leaving most users of care difficult to hear (`CLM-010015`).

Two Shuihudi letters partly break that silence. Heifu and Jing, conscripted Qin soldiers from a lower-status household, directly request cash and clothing from their mother. Their urgency and attention to prices/resources show material coping through family dependence rather than an abstract doctrine of wellness ([Wu, pp. 69–70](https://brill.com/view/journals/bsms/8/1/article-p57_3.pdf)). Yet their mother remains the person spoken to, not a speaker whose reply survives. The phrase translated “If you really don't send it, I will just die” is emphatic epistolary language; without semantic and genre study it cannot support suicidality or diagnosis (`CLM-010017`).

## Care, coping, and power

- **Ritual consultation:** Baoshan shows repeated consultation and sacrifice as part of care represented around one elite. It does not show that the client accepted every explanation or that ritual care excluded other bodily practices.
- **Self-cultivation:** *Neiye* practices of calm, posture, moderation, music, and ritual are embedded in normative and political order. They should not be marketed backward as individualized consumer wellness.
- **Medical intervention:** *Lingshu* chapter 22 instructs practitioners to observe and perform needle, bleeding, or moxa interventions. This is prescriptive professional voice; it does not document consent, pain, or outcome.
- **Knowledge restriction:** Wuwei transmission prohibitions make remedies a question of authority and access, not merely neutral technique.
- **Legal emotion:** The widow's case shows that authorities could interpret emotional display and intimate conduct together. Emotion history must include who has power to name a response adequate or deviant.

## Missing-voice register

| missing or weakly represented group / question | present evidence | next archive needed |
|---|---|---|
| Nonelite sufferers and families | Two conscripted men's direct household requests survive, but no comparable first-person illness account | More letters, contracts, complaints, household texts, local administrative slips, mortuary bioarchaeology with careful limits |
| Women beyond official legal representation | Widow is represented by officials; succession statute is formal | Full Qin/Han case corpora, women's letters/epitaphs where available, gender-focused legal and medical social history |
| Enslaved, penal, servant, and migrant people | Not adequately identified in retained core | Status terminology in legal manuscripts, labor/penal records, excavated household archives |
| Children, older adults, disabled people | Philosophical child-at-well is hypothetical, not a child voice | Age-specific medical/legal records and material evidence |
| Caregivers and household negotiation | Almost absent | Household and kinship records; case narratives with named companions |
| Cost, access, and geography | Multiple diviners for one elite; restricted formula transmission | Economic history of practitioners/remedies, travel and local institutional records |
| Coercion, confinement, consent, pain | No direct evidence sufficient | Legal statutes/cases, institutional histories, treatment records with explicit procedural context |
| Outcomes and satisfaction | Divinatory forecasts and medical prescriptions, not reliable outcome datasets | Repeated case sequences, follow-up records, patient-authored material if extant |
| Ordinary words for distress | Elite normative and medical terminology | Vernacular letters, administrative complaints, excavated noncanonical texts |

## Empathy and terminology rules

1. Do not use “crazy,” “lunatic,” “psychotic,” “bipolar,” “schizophrenic,” or diagnostic person-labels for ancient subjects.
2. Keep *dian* and *kuang* visible and describe the signs in the checked passage. A modern diagnostic comparison belongs only in a clearly labeled reception section.
3. Use “represented as,” “the record describes,” or “the text prescribes” when the institutional source controls the voice.
4. Do not equate ritual explanation with lack of observation, or medical intervention with humane/effective care. Ask what action followed, who authorized it, and whose response is missing.
5. Do not romanticize cultivation as universally accessible self-care. Elite literacy, discipline, political ends, labor, gender, and status require investigation.
6. Do not turn absence into a claim that a group received no care. State that the opened archive does not establish access or experience.

## Outline-ready lived-experience beats

- A high Chu official appears repeatedly in illness divinations; several experts disagree while his body remains the object of observation and ritual action.
- A widow's grief becomes an institutional problem, but officials disagree and the final ruling releases her; her own feelings remain outside the record.
- A medical manuscript cache preserves recipes and restrictions on who may receive them, making healing knowledge a power relationship.
- Two Qin soldiers ask their mother for clothing and cash; the household coping is visible, but her answer and interior experience are not.
- A received medical chapter gives detailed signs and interventions but no named sufferer, reminding readers that classification can become more visible than the classified person.

Each beat must carry its archive caveat in the same paragraph, not in a distant disclaimer.
