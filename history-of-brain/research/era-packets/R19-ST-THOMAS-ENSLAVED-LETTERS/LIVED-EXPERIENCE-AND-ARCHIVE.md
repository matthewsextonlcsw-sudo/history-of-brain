# R19 St. Thomas enslaved letters — lived experience and archive audit

**Status:** internal research audit; not public prose
**Corpus:** six selected documents dated 1738–1753, consulted through the 1996 *Die Creol taal* edition and later specialist work
**Core caution:** a letter may preserve enslaved people's agency without being an unmediated transcript of inner life.

## What kind of lived evidence survives

The packet preserves words attributed to named Black writers and petitioners, but it does so through unequal and different record chains. “Voice” therefore has to be described item by item.

| Item | Attributed speaker/author | Material writer or hand reported by the edition | Other mediation | What can be said | What cannot be said |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peter/Pitrus, 1738 | Peter/Pitrus | Peter; Friedrich Martin's note says he wrote it himself | historical spelling; editorial transcription, gloss and free English translation; DBNL display | self-writing is attested in the consulted edition; the letter records communal prayer, eating and heart/emotion language | the hand is not independently verified; it is not an unfiltered diary or population evidence |
| Petition to the Danish king, 1739 | Pieter, Mingo and others; a claimed larger constituency | Domingo Gesoe | collective composition unknown; manuscript/1742-print variation; editorial and translation layers | the petition records allegations of violence, coercion and religious obstruction, plus emissaries, flight and appeal | every line is not each signer's verbatim speech; the claimed 650-plus is not a verified roster or unanimous assent |
| Damma/Marotta/Madlena, 1739 | Damma/Marotta/Madlena | not securely identified in the consulted chain | three African-text versions; a separate Creole layer; modern translation; disputed language and interpretation | a multilingual, multiply mediated woman's petition survives; the 1996 Creole-English chain records sadness and obstruction of worship | the Creole/English wording is not a transparent equivalent of the African-language text; language, theology, ethnicity and the claimed 250-women constituency remain held |
| Lenathge/Milka, 1740 [probably 1741] | Lenathge | missionary Valentin Löhans | dictation/composition circumstances unknown; editorial translation; uncertain year | a mission-written letter represents crying, forgiveness, strength of heart and mercy | exact spoken wording, private belief, voluntariness, diagnosis, cure or durable outcome cannot be recovered |
| Domingo Gesoe, 1752 | Domingo | Domingo | editorial transcription/translation and a correspondence route to Bethlehem | self-writing is attributed; the letter records gratitude, happiness, remembrance, greeting and hoped-for reunion | the original hand is unreplayed; recipients' response or shared understanding is not established |

## Experience without retrospective diagnosis

- Heart, sadness, crying, happiness, anger, forgiveness, mercy, bodily illness and remembered relation are historically recorded expressions in particular religious and petitionary genres.
- These expressions matter as evidence of how people communicated suffering, hope, repair, obligation and relationship. They do not by themselves reveal a stable private interior state.
- No item establishes depression, trauma disorder, anxiety disorder, psychosis, psychosomatic illness or any other modern diagnosis.
- No item establishes duration, severity, functional impairment, population prevalence, clinical treatment, adherence, safety, recovery or cure.
- Prayer and Moravian belonging may be discussed as practices and meanings represented in the documents. They may not be presented as therapies proven to have caused recovery.
- Violence in the king petition is necessary to understanding coercion and resistance, but details should remain no more graphic than the historical argument requires.

## Agency and coercion must remain together

The documents show Black writers and petitioners using correspondence, religious language and collective address to sustain relationships, ask for protection, contest obstruction, remember distant people and express hope. This is evidence of agency. It does not cancel the conditions of slavery, threats, punishment, ownership, missionary authority or archival selection.

The Zinzendorf comparator is essential because it records a Moravian leadership position that spiritual freedom did not end bodily enslavement and that obedience remained required. It prevents a rescue narrative in which mission Christianity is automatically equated with emancipation or humane mental-health care. The comparator also does not erase the uses Black writers made of the religious vocabulary and network. Public synthesis must retain both parts of that tension.

## Collective speech, representation and consent

- Petition numbers are claims made in petitionary texts, not census counts.
- No opened roster shows who authorized the documents, how wording was negotiated, whether everyone knew of the petition or whether all assented.
- A named writer can be a scribe for others without being the sole author of their position.
- Phrases such as “we” and statements made for hundreds of people establish a claim to representation, not unanimity.
- The absence of an assent record is not proof of dissent. Consent is **Unknown**, not absent.
- Historical participation in Moravian correspondence cannot be converted into contemporary consent to digital reuse, commercial publication, translation, illustration or search optimization.

## Gender, status, age and disability limits

- Women appear as attributed authors/speakers and as a claimed collective constituency, but the record chain is especially mediated in the Damma/Marotta/Madlena and Lenathge items.
- Male writers appear both writing for themselves and as scribes for collective documents. The selected pattern cannot support a literacy or gender-rate claim.
- Owner labels and enslaved status belong to the historical record chain and should be stated only where the edition supports them. A newer project supplies a potentially different status biography for Damma, but that biography was not independently replayed here; public status wording for her remains a review gap.
- The six selected documents do not provide a usable age distribution.
- No defensible inference about disability prevalence, cognitive difference or access to care can be made from the corpus.
- Named people described by someone else are not converted into independent first-person witnesses.

## Language and translation audit

1. **Source language is not English experience.** The 1996 English renderings are explicitly free translations. Any quotation must identify the language and edition layer.
2. **Damma's item is disputed.** The 1996 edition labels the African language unidentified. A named 2025 collaboration identifies it as Gbe, reports divergent Creole content and acknowledges incomplete and contested interpretation. That later work is important but does not erase the frozen publication hold.
3. **No ethnicity shortcut.** A historical place/origin expression, “Poppo,” “Aja,” or a modern language identification cannot be converted automatically into a contemporary ethnic identity.
4. **No theology shortcut.** Terms translated as God, Jesus, spirit, mercy or Saviour may carry histories not captured by an English equivalent. Damma's text especially requires Gbe and Creole specialist review.
5. **Named-language care.** A candidate contemporary label is “Carriols (Virgin Islands Dutch Creole).” Historical labels containing slurs should appear only when indispensable to identify a source and with explanation; final naming requires USVI-rooted review.
6. **Uncertainty stays visible.** `[probably 1741]`, unknown writer, copy status, disputed interpretation and free-translation status must survive every outline and prose handoff.

## Archive formation and custody

The path opened for this packet is not the original encounter. It is:

`St. Thomas document → Moravian collection/copying and custody → Herrnhut manuscript grouping → microfilm → photocopy → 1991–1993 diplomatic transcription project → 1996 anthology/interlinear gloss/free translation → DBNL diplomatic rendering with listed adaptations and omissions → current researcher`

Each arrow can preserve material and also select, reorder, normalize or obscure it. The 1996 editors describe about 150 letters from a broader 1737–1768 corpus; this packet uses six. The selection cannot stand for the full archive, all enslaved residents, non-Moravians, people who did not write, or people whose records were not preserved.

The current archival hierarchy and signatures are supplied by Unity Archives and the edition, but public digital facsimiles of the six original items were not established in the bounded search. That is an **unresolved access gap**, not proof that images do not exist. Until manuscript images are obtained and replayed, the packet cannot make visual, handwriting, paper, ink, strikeout, insertion or paleographic claims.

## People and experiences absent from the packet

- people who never joined or addressed the Moravian network;
- people who could not, did not wish to, or were not permitted to write or dictate;
- people whose words were discarded, untranslated, unselected, destroyed or remain uncatalogued;
- non-Christian, dissenting and privately ambivalent people;
- children, elders and disabled people whose experiences are not securely identifiable here;
- people who fled, resisted or sought healing in practices excluded from this microcorpus;
- the many people invoked by collective petitions whose individual assent and wording do not survive;
- replies from Danish, Moravian, Bethlehem or other recipients unless separately opened;
- enslaved workers involved in carrying, translating, copying or explaining documents but not named;
- outcomes after complaint, prayer, correspondence or punishment;
- descendant and present-day USVI interpretations of these exact documents.

Absence from this selection must never be rewritten as historical absence.

## Community and descendant authority

Online access and scholarly review are not community authorization. The Virgin Islands Studies Collective, Caribbean Genealogy Library, University of the Virgin Islands Special Collections and relevant territory/archive partnerships are feasible contact and review routes. This packet did not obtain their approval, did not establish that any one organization speaks for all descendants, and treats nonresponse as nonconsent.

Before public use, seek a bounded review of:

- the project's current language names and treatment of historical slurs;
- whether and how named enslaved people should be quoted, summarized or visually represented;
- the handling of Christian mission, African-language religious concepts, resistance and distress;
- the risks of turning intimate words, violence or prayer into SEO hooks;
- contextual resources created by USVI-rooted scholars, educators, archivists and descendant communities;
- whether commercial reuse requires benefits, attribution or restrictions beyond formal copyright.

Community review complements rather than replaces language expertise, archive permission and source criticism.

## Rights, privacy and dignity

- DBNL permits linking and limited supporting quotation under its stated conditions, but the title remains copyright-protected and other use requires permission. Commercial text/data mining is prohibited.
- Unity Archives requires archive/signature citation and generally separate consent for publication, duplication, sharing and digital projects. The ability to consult an edition does not clear original-manuscript reproduction.
- The cached Bøegh chapter is CC BY 4.0. Gerbner's monograph is CC BY-NC 4.0; embedded Herrnhut images have their own rights layer. Other opened scholarship is link-only unless a license is documented.
- Named historical people are not treated as anonymous content stock. Legal age alone does not eliminate dignity, descendant, cultural or commercial concerns.
- Do not reproduce original images, extended translations or culturally sensitive language material until all relevant permissions and reviews are recorded.

## Publication-safety rules

1. Name the document, date, source layer, writer/scribe and translation layer whenever a person's words are discussed.
2. Say “the letter records,” “the petition alleges,” or “the translation represents” where inner experience is not directly recoverable.
3. Preserve agency without romanticizing suffering or mission institutions.
4. Use minimum necessary violence detail and no sensational headlines, thumbnails or reenactments.
5. Do not diagnose, prescribe, claim efficacy or turn religious language into a modern treatment analogy.
6. Do not publish disputed African-language translation or identity claims before specialist and USVI-rooted review.
7. Do not publish petition counts as verified populations or unanimous consent.
8. Do not use historical slurs for SEO. If indispensable in a title/catalog note, contextualize once and prefer the approved current label elsewhere.
9. Do not reproduce manuscript images or copyrighted edition material merely because it is online.
10. Keep the six-document boundary visible and state who the archive cannot show.

## Internal lived-experience verdict

**Usable with strict attribution and public holds.** The corpus contributes unusually important named Black-authored, attributed and collective records of distress, hope, relationship, protest and religious meaning under slavery. Its value depends on retaining mediation, translation dispute, coercion, archival custody and missing voices. It cannot carry diagnostic, prevalence, efficacy, generalized-healing, firstness or representative-population claims.
