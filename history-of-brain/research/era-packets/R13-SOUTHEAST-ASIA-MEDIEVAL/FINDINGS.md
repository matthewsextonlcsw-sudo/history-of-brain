**Purpose:** internal evidence synthesis, not public prose. **Core:** three declared settings in two

## 1. Angkorian `ārogyaśālā` inscription formula

### What the opened witnesses document

K.209 is the strongest opened wording control. Its 1108 Śaka text uses `ārogyaśālā` and represents
a foundation with Bhaiṣajya Sugata imagery (B5–10), two `bhiṣajau` and lodging allocations
(B11–12), male and female service roles (B13–26), ritual rice residue to be given to `rogin` sick
people (B27–28), enumerated materials/remedies (C1–18), ritual officers and a `gaṇaka` (C19–20),
and administrative protections (D9–12). K.368 supplies a fuller inherited French translation, but
its parallel formula is not independent corroboration.

The “four `varṇa`” sentence at K.209 B11/K.368 stanza 19 is a royal normative formula. It supports
an intended eligibility statement in this textual program, not proof of universal access, absence of
fees, equal treatment in practice, or representation of people outside the formula's social categories.
Likewise, staff counts and provisions document a prescribed organization, not payroll, attendance,
survival, or treatment outcomes.

### `manoroga` translation seam

K.209 A25–26 reads `dehinān deharogo yan manorogo rujattarām ...`; K.368 is damaged at the
corresponding place and Siddham records Honda's `yanmanorogo` variant. Honda translates this as
the king's “disease of mind” being more painful when people have bodily disease; Finot's French
translation makes the stanza a statement about the king suffering subjects' diseases/public pain.
In either reading, the speaker is royal eulogy and the contrast attributes distress to the king. It does
not identify a patient diagnosis, a mental-health ward, a mind–body taxonomy for clinical use, or a
treatment for psychological distress.

### Mixed ritual, material, and administrative stack

This exact inscriptional corpus does not fit a “spirit belief only” description: divine figures and
merit coexist in the formula with physicians, gendered staff assignments, food/water, enumerated
substances, institutional management, and tax/labor rules. The evidence also does not license the
opposite simplification that the institution was secular medicine with religion added decoratively.
The categories are co-constitutive in the royal text, and implementation is unknown.

Honda's 1965 inference that food and other necessities show “relief of the poor” is not stated in the
inscription. Poverty is not named in his cited passage. Preserve his reading as historiography only;
do not convert it to access, welfare, or free-care fact.

## 2. Tonle Snguot archaeological control

The complete preliminary report provides a valuable but narrow material check. Six test units
totaled 61.5 m², which the authors estimated as at most 0.1% of the site (printed p. 51). The report
describes architecture/chapel context, floors/post bases, ceramics, sculpture and other finds, with
the deposits provisionally concentrated in the twelfth–thirteenth centuries (pp. 51–52, 60–74).
This documents material activity within a compound that the excavators identify as an Angkorian
hospital site. The material sample does not independently establish that function, the formula's
staffing, any diagnosis or treatment encounter, patient identity, fees, outcomes, or typical operation
across the hospital network.

The report's pp. 16–17 statements about physical, spiritual, and mental health and access for the
general population occur in a broad interpretive section before excavation results. The report itself
calls the work preliminary/cursory and later notes that iron tools could be ordinary household/work
objects; the identification of two iron objects as medical tools remains speculative pending cleaning
and radiography (printed pp. 99–100). Those broad mental-health
and access statements therefore rate D for this project's intended claims.

Present-day custody and protocol are evidence in their own right: removal of statuary followed a
ceremony for blessing/protection, and finds/samples were stored with APSARA Authority (pp. 51,
56, 74). These current actions are not medieval ritual evidence. They do require that any image,
object, or display decision be handled through Cambodian custodial and community consultation.

## 3. *Dharma Pātañjala* textual/manuscript corpus

### Date, language, and custody

Schoemann I 21 is a codex unicus of 89 gĕbang palm leaves, written in black ink, with West
Javanese script/linguistic features. Its colophon gives Śaka 1389/1467 CE. That is the copy date,
not a proven composition date. The text is principally Old Javanese prose interspersed with
Sanskrit verses. Its nineteenth-century route is reported—rather than fully demonstrated—as
Merapi–Merbabu/Windu Sono collection to Friederich, Schoemann, and the Staatsbibliothek zu
Berlin. Current Berlin custody and colonial collecting history must remain visible.

### Exchange is not copying

Acri's full-source analysis describes a roughly one-third `yogapāda` that follows/reworks parts of
the first three chapters of the Sanskrit Pātañjalayogaśāstra while diverging, rearranging, adding
Śaiva material, and presenting Old Javanese exegesis that is not a direct translation. Exact passages
at DhP 194.11–13, 196.8–14, 290.10–13, 294.14–17 and 298.2–4 frame `samādhi`, `citta`,
`ātman`, release, and yoga within a Śaiva soteriology. This is evidence of local learned agency and
reconfiguration, not passive “Indianization” or exact copying.

The source chain is not diverse: Acri edited/translates the codex and authored or co-authored all
three opened interpretive works used here. CrossAsia institutional metadata is also authored by
Acri. Claims should therefore remain medium-confidence even when exact locators are excellent.

### Not modern therapy or wellness

DhP 196.8–14 calls the goal `sukha viśeṣa`, but Acri explains this “supreme pleasure” as release in
a Śaiva soteriological argument. DhP 294.14–17 critiques treating stillness of `citta` as yoga's
final goal; 298.2–4 concerns consciousness/Soul, mind, union with the Lord, and supernatural
prowess. These are not clinical instructions, psychotherapeutic encounters, stress-reduction
outcomes, or evidence that a population used yoga for modern mental wellness.

The text may preserve practical techniques, but a manuscript prescription cannot establish who
performed them, how often, under what institutional access conditions, or with what effects.
The packet has no contemporaneous practitioner diary, learner testimony, patient narrative, or
material implementation evidence for this corpus.

## 4. Controlled cross-corpus conclusions

- The Angkorian formula witnesses are repeated administrative texts within one dependency group;
  copying is precisely what makes them poor independent corroborators of practice.
- The West Javanese work engages transregional Sanskritic materials through selective Old Javanese
  reconfiguration; similarity establishes neither direct copying from a single model nor a line from
  Angkor to Java.
- Neither corpus demonstrates a continuous “traditional medicine,” “mindfulness,” or therapy line
  to the present. The chronological and genre seams must remain explicit.
- The packet can reject only exclusive descriptions of its **selected evidence** (“spirit only,”
  “mere Indian copy”). It cannot quantify belief, medicine, yoga, or institutional care across
  Southeast Asia.
- Patient/practitioner experience is structurally absent: inscriptions speak for a king, archaeology
  preserves things and deposits, and the codex preserves learned doctrine. Empathy here means
  refusing to invent satisfaction, fear, consent, efficacy, or neglect.
