# R05-ROMAN — lived experience and archive

**State:** internal archive/voice audit; not public prose.

## Voice-proximity register

| Evidence | Voice proximity | What the person reports or is shown doing | Mediation and empathy rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seneca, Letter 78 (`EVD-008003`) | First-person retrospective self-writing | Serious illness, desire to die, concern for his father, friends' conversation and study as help | Wealthy male senator writing a moral letter. Preserve the reported relational anchors; do not diagnose or reuse Stoic claims as suicide advice. |
| Aelius Aristides, *Sacred Tales* I.1–28 (`EVD-008015`) | Extensive first-person literary patient narrative | Sleep disruption, stomach symptoms, bathing/fasting/vomiting, dreams, decisions and multiple helpers | Wealthy Greek-speaking orator composing praise/religious literature from notes/dictation. | Galen cases (`EVD-008006`) | Physician-reported examples | Source-language passages concern fear, sadness, cognition and bodily routes; the packet's English summary is wording-unverified | Greek/Latin old edition with no named project translation; individual social lives largely absent. | Celsus/Caelius patients (`EVD-008000`–`008001`, `008007`–`008008`) | Prescriptive imagined/observed patient types | Fear, anger, quietness, sleep, danger, response to environment, post-episode pain; Caelius wording remains translation-held | The patient's consent, cost, outcome and own words are absent. ## Care networks made visible

- **Kin and obligation:** Seneca's father; a son's legal duty toward his mother; relatives expected to supervise; curators controlling affairs.
- **Friends and peers:** Seneca's visitors; Aristides's friends and foster father; friends named as possible household supervisors in the imperial rescript.
- **Servants, enslaved attendants and paid helpers:** Aristides's servants/attendants and medical texts' *ministri* are visible as labor but rarely speak. Status and coercion within care work remain underdescribed.
- **Physicians and medical authors:** distinct named texts argue over cause, environment, restraint, food and drugs. The unopened Polito article cannot support a broader jurisdiction claim.
- **Priests, sanctuary staff and divine agency:** Aristides's priest/temple network and Asclepius coexist with doctors and regimen.
- **Officials and jurists:** governor, prefect, praetor and curator become involved through capacity, property, public burden or acute risk.

This is a network map, not evidence that every person could access every resource. Fees, travel, household wealth, citizenship and enslavement likely structured access, but this packet lacks adequate price/account evidence to quantify them.

## Archive-production filters

### Genre

- Philosophical letters/dialogues select morally useful episodes and arguments.
- Medical works classify, prescribe and argue with rivals; they rarely record consent, cost or outcomes.
- Legal compilations preserve disputes/categories useful to administration and property.
- Praise/religious self-writing organizes illness around divine relationship and rhetorical identity.
- Archaeology establishes place/building history, not inner experience by itself.

### Status and power

- Property-bearing, educated male citizens dominate named self-writing.
- Enslaved people enter through sale, service, escape, punishment and owner liability.
- Women appear mainly as mothers/wives/dependents in legal arrangements or as generic bodies in medical claims; no adult Roman woman's first-person illness account was opened for this packet.
- Children appear as legal analogies or generalized medical categories, not sustained voices.
- Poor/propertyless and rural people are mostly inferred or absent because curator and property law has little reason to preserve them.
- Carers/attendants are often present but unnamed; their training, payment, coercion, affection and burden are usually unrecoverable.

### Translation and transmission

- Cicero's Latin terminology is an argument, not a word-for-word dictionary of Greek mental categories.
- Spencer, Gummere, Yonge, Adams, Scott and Moyle are older English translations with their own period vocabulary.
- Kühn's Galen and Amman's Caelius are old editions; translation-sensitive public wording needs a current critical edition and specialist.
- Caelius's Soranic/Methodist inheritance is layered: a Latin author/adaptor, a lost Greek source, rival views quoted within the work, later editors, OCR and modern translation.

## Missing voices register

| Missing/weak voice | Consequence | Target for next research |
|---|---|---|
| Enslaved people speaking for themselves | Sale-law categories can masquerade as lived description | Manumission inscriptions, petitions, legal testimony, household letters and epigraphy with explicit provenance |
| Women as sufferers/caregivers in first person | Male authors and legal kinship roles dominate | Papyri/letters from Roman Egypt and other provinces; funerary/epigraphic corpora; gender-focused classical scholarship |
| Children and adolescents | Medical age generalizations lack lives | Letters, school texts, epitaphs, injury/disability archaeology with ethical review |
| Poor, propertyless, rural and mobile people | Curator/property law and elite self-writing skew the archive | Provincial papyri, petitions, work records, settlement archaeology, charitable/religious archives in later packets |
| Attendants and family carers | Prescriptions show tasks without worker voice | Household contracts/accounts, inscriptions, papyri, legal disputes and social-history studies |
| Treatment outcomes and refusal | Recommendations can be mistaken for effective care | Case collections with follow-up, letters/petitions, complaints, iatrogenic injury evidence |
| Cost and access | Elite sanctuary travel can look universal | Fees, travel accounts, temple finances, physician contracts, patronage records |
| Provincial diversity | Rome/Pergamum can stand for an empire | Separate evidence from Africa, Iberia, Gaul, Britain, Balkans, Syria and Roman Egypt; coordinate with regional packets |

## Public-use red lines

- Do not use “crazy,” “lunatic,” “madman,” “psychopath,” or equivalent as narrator language; retain historical terms only when needed and explained.
- Do not diagnose Seneca, Aristides, Caligula, an Aretaean case, or a legal subject.
- Do not romanticize self-harm or quote it for dramatic search traffic.
- Do not call restraint “treatment” without naming safety/control/coercion and missing consent/outcome.
- Do not call Pergamum a psychiatric asylum or hospital from Aristides's account.
- Do not turn enslaved people into colorful legal examples. State the commodifying archive and missing testimony.
- Do not say “Romans believed,” “Romans treated,” or “Roman psychiatry” when the evidence is one author, rescript, sanctuary, class or language region.
