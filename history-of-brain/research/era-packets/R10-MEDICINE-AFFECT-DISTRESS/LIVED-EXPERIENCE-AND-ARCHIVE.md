# R10-MEDICINE-AFFECT-DISTRESS lived experience and archive audit

State: internal sensitivity and archive control; no public prose

## Bottom line

This packet does not contain an unmediated patient-authored account. Its strongest evidence concerns what elite learned men prescribed, classified, claimed to observe, or narrated about other people. That absence must remain visible: a vivid case story is not a patient's voice, and an author's claimed cure is not the recipient's evaluation.

## Archive asymmetries

### Who produces the record

- Four learned male authors/compilers dominate the selected corpus.
- Named modern male translators/editors strongly shape the accessible English/French wording.
- Patrons, rulers, physicians, wise advisers, and elite readers occupy more textual space than recipients.
- The most vivid “cases” come from a genre designed partly to instruct, entertain, and enhance practitioner reputation (`SRC-015011`).

### Who is made visible

- Elite men are named and biographically developed.
- Patients are unnamed types, narrative devices, or evidence for a physician's skill.
- The laboring porter is socially identified but not individually named.
- Women appear as a quoted bereaved type, a silent beloved, or a devalued comparison.
- Children appear as objects of elite training rather than speaking subjects.

### Who is missing

- Patient-authored documents, letters, diaries, petitions, complaints, refusals, and household records.
- Women healers, midwives, nurses/attendants, pharmacists, musicians, non-elite counselors, religious practitioners, and disabled people's own accounts.
- Jewish, Christian, Zoroastrian, and other participants within Arabic-/Persian-speaking medical networks.
- Rural communities, enslaved people, migrants, poor recipients, and people outside court/book culture.
- Price, time, travel, language, literacy, and other access barriers.

These are gaps in this packet and often in the surviving/selected archive; they are not proof that the people, practices, or voices did not exist.

## Empathy and language controls

- Lead with what the person is represented as experiencing before the physician's theory, but attribute that representation to its narrator.
- Preserve *mālīkhūliyā* at first use; explain Isḥāq's own humoral/faculty model. Do not substitute a DSM/ICD term.
- Do not repeat degrading historical descriptions unless they are essential to analyze stigma, coercion, or archive power.
- Do not write that a person “believed he was…” as settled clinical fact when the only witness is a later anecdote; write that the story represents him that way.
- Do not treat marriage as a cure or happy outcome without noting that the beloved's voice and both parties' consent are absent.
- Do not turn “music” into a warm human-interest beat while omitting that the same corpus contains force, hierarchy, and absent consent.
- Do not praise authors as “humane,” “modern,” or “ahead of their time” from isolated passages. Mixed evidence must remain mixed.

## Archive-ready conclusion

The most responsible lived-experience statement is negative but important: these works show how authoritative writers organized distress and imagined responses, while the people experiencing distress rarely control the record. Public writing should treat that silence as part of the history rather than filling it with modern empathy theater or assumed satisfaction.
