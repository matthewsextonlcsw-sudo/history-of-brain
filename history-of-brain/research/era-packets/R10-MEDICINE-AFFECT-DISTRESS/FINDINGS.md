State: internal evidence records; no public prose

## Reading rule

The “evidence” below is evidence that a named text prescribes, classifies, reports, or narrates something. It is not automatically evidence that the prescription was implemented, a reported example occurred, a story's outcome was caused by the intervention, or a modern diagnosis applies. Exact historical English/French terms remain the translators' wording unless an Arabic term is explicitly visible.

## Canonical evidence-field key

Each row below is one evidence record. Column 1 is `evidence_id`; column 2 is `source_id` and
`locator`; column 3 contains `evidence_form` and `verbatim_or_description`; column 4 supplies
`context_before_after`, `speaker_or_subject`, and `collector_or_recorder`; column 5 supplies
`method`, `directness`, `representativeness`, `limitations`, and `researcher_note`.

Shared fields unless a row overrides them: `evidence_form=paraphrasable passage` for a named
text and `bounded corpus audit` for `EVD-015022`–`EVD-015024`; `translation=the named consulted
translation in SOURCE-LEDGER.md`; `original_text=not opened` for medieval Arabic/Persian
records; `sensitivity=standard`, except `EVD-015002`, `EVD-015006`, `EVD-015008`,
`EVD-015015`–`EVD-015016`, `EVD-015020`–`EVD-015022`, `EVD-015025`–`EVD-015026`, where
`sensitivity=trauma/violence/clinical/identity` as applicable; `extracted_by=R10 packet
researcher`; `extracted_on=2026-08-18`; `verification_status=checked against source`.
`Unknown` fields remain Unknown; no missing consent, result, practice, or motive is inferred.

## Selected topics and bounded findings

### Affect, self-regimen, and conversation

Al-Rāzī and al-Balkhī both preserve structured prescriptions for anger, worry/fear, grief/sadness, recollection, counter-thoughts, diversion, and advice from others (`EVD-015000`–`EVD-015007`). The similarity is evidence of comparable techniques at the level of text, not proof of one therapy, profession, lineage, or clinical outcome. Adamson places al-Rāzī's work in ethical advice; al-Balkhī's English text is heavily framed by Badri's modern psychology vocabulary.

### Brain/body, humors, and troubling states

Isḥāq's first book supplies an elaborate humoral, bodily, environmental, affective, and faculty-based causal scheme and a three-form taxonomy (`EVD-015011`–`EVD-015014`). It is evidence of an explanatory model, not of the model's biological truth. Terms such as brain, heart, stomach, imagination, memory, fear, and sadness cannot be silently recoded as neuroscience or present diagnostic criteria.

### Music

The al-Balkhī translation directly prescribes music/songs alongside encouraging talk for one translated category (`EVD-015007`). Isḥāq describes some sufferers preferring music/conversation (`EVD-015014`); that is not treatment. López González reports music within a broader Avicennian lovesickness regimen (`EVD-015019`), but this packet did not open the critical Arabic *Qānūn* passage. A 2017 article supplies a dated “earliest/first” al-Kindī reception claim, but its cited Shiloah chapter instead traces therapeutic music to earlier biblical/Pythagorean precedents and does not reproduce the child anecdote in the replayed segment (`EVD-015027`–`EVD-015028`). None documents a delivered session with consent, follow-up, or measured result.

### Case history and famous diagnosis

The love-diagnosis story is securely documented as a story in Niẓāmī (`EVD-015016`) and challenged as clinical documentation by Álvarez Millán (`EVD-015018`). Browne also records a passage-local chronology contradiction: Ibn Sīnā's own chronology places his Jurjān arrival after Qābūs's deposition (`EVD-015026`). A modern version's change in patient identity (`EVD-015020`) demonstrates transmission instability. The porter anecdote (`EVD-015021`) also belongs to *adab*, but its explicit violence and the family's inability to intervene are historically meaningful representations of medical/status power even though the episode is not verified practice.

## What is not established

- No selected source establishes “the first psychotherapy,” “the first cognitive therapy/CBT,” “the first music therapy,” or “the first modern diagnosis.”
- No selected source establishes that a prescription was routinely administered, affordable, voluntary, effective, or hospital-based.
- No selected source supports identifying *mālīkhūliyā*, *al-gham*, sadness, fear, inner speech, lovesickness, or any narrated person with depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, OCD, PTSD, anxiety disorder, or a psychosomatic diagnosis.
- No selected source supports a universal account of medicine among Muslims or in all Arabic-/Persian-speaking societies.
- The Isḥāq source opened here is the first book only. It postpones formal treatment while reporting one lead-cap intervention; consent, result, safety, duration, routine use, and efficacy are Unknown, and the unopened treatment book remains held.
