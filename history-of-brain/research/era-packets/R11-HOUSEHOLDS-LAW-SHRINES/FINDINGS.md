# R11-HOUSEHOLDS-LAW-SHRINES findings

Status: internal research synthesis; no sentence below is approved public prose.

## What the bounded corpus can establish

### 1. Household responses were relational, practical, and emotionally costly

T-S 6J4.20 preserves a woman's appeal after hearing that her nephew was ill. Her letter speaks
of crying, fasting, sitting in a corner, worry (`hamm`), and a “fire” in the heart; she asks her
nephew not to take worry upon himself and presses her brother, through their shared upbringing,
not to neglect him (`SRC-017000`, recto lines 4–17 and right margin). These are the writer's
communicative acts, not symptoms from a clinical interview. No reply or direct appraisal from the
nephew is linked or identified in the opened PGP record; the packet did not establish wider Geniza
survival.

AIU VII.E.149 preserves a father's response to news of his son Isḥāq's relapse. He orders the son
to return to the family immediately so they can take him to `al-hukama'`, glossed by the editor as
physicians, and says the household's own frailty prevents them from fetching him (`SRC-017001`,
recto lines 4–13). This is a direct example of family concern, travel constraints, and learned
practitioners in one plan. It does not show that every family could or would provide care, that the
son consented, that treatment occurred, or that it worked.

Together the letters do not support an absolute “family versus medicine” story in this two-letter
sample. They do not establish a general system of community care. Their direct writers are a worried
female relative and a father; no words from the ill men are linked or identified in the two opened
PGP records.

### 2. A legal category changed specific liabilities, not a person's whole identity

In al-Kasani's Hanafi theft discussion, reason and maturity are conditions for the
fixed corporal punishment. He distinguishes an act during `junun` from one during `ifaqa` and
states that the stolen property's value remains owed even when the fixed punishment does not
apply (`SRC-017004`, vol. 7, p. 67). This is a partition of consequences inside a named doctrine.
It is not a medical description and does not mean the person lacked every legal interest or duty.

Hamilton's *Hedaya* renders a related dispute among Abu Hanifa, Zufar, and Abu Yusuf about a
mixed group containing an “infant or lunatic.” The edition-accountable Arabic *al-Hidāya* witness
uses `ṣabī aw majnūn` and preserves the same principal/dependent-act structure (`SRC-017010`,
vol. 4, pp. 153–54; Hamilton `SRC-017003`, Book VIII, pp. 134–35). Hamilton remains a 1791
translation through Persian, not a critical translation. The collation establishes a doctrinal
disagreement, not a court applying it, a family confining anyone, or a ruler being tolerant.

Alaghbri's full legal-theory study (`SRC-017011`) further cautions that legal subjectivity and
`taklīf` are distinct analytic questions and that his Ashʿari sample cannot validate this Hanafi
penalty rule. Category, capacity, penalty, guardianship, status, and implementation remain separate.

The same al-Kasani passage says sex, freedom, and Islam are not conditions for that penalty and
explicitly lists enslaved statuses. This is evidence of a violent normative regime that classified
people across status groups; it is not evidence of equal social standing or implementation.

### 3. Shrine healing could create care obligations and durable dependency at once

The eighth-century P.KRU instruments connect narrated healing at Apa Phoibammon's shrine with
legal donation/service. P.KRU 80 says a father brought a healed boy to the `topos`, executed a
deed through the monastery's officials, and assigned sweeping, sprinkling, basin-water, lamp, and
other duties (`SRC-017005`, P.KRU 80 lines 27–30, 37–40). Later clauses could allow work outside
the shrine while wages went to its lamp. The record supplies a rule and arrangement, not the
boy's evaluation or proof of daily compliance.

P.KRU 79 is unusually important for consent: Schenke reports that the widowed Kalisthene's son
Merkourios agreed when he reached legal age (`SRC-017005`, p. 13 and appendix Text 2). That one
assent cannot be projected onto children in the other instruments. For P.KRU 80, the correct
bounded finding is only that Schenke's opened discussion does not report Abraham's assent; the
complete Till translation was not opened. P.KRU 104 is an adult
self-donation after reported healing; its surviving formula begins with a declaration of sound
wits and binds Petronios to lifelong service (`SRC-017005`, lines 18–33). Formulaic capacity and
gratitude do not settle whether his choice was socially unconstrained.

The deeds also prescribe reciprocal support: P.KRU 87/90 contextual parallels require shrine
officials to nourish and clothe servants, while servants obey orders. That context helps interpret
the retained sample but is not promoted into a separate primary corpus. “Care” and “control” are
therefore not exclusive readings. Shelter, food, ritual participation, labor, obedience, and
lifelong dependence can occupy the same legal arrangement.

## Category separation

| Category | What appears in the core | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Legal capacity/liability | Al-Kasani's theft rule limits a specific corporal penalty by reason/maturity and distinguishes `junun` from `ifaqa`; P.KRU deeds use capacity/assent formulae | Modern competence tests, diagnosis, comprehensive civil incapacity, or implementation |
| Moral/religious category | P.KRU donors frame illness/healing, vows, salvation, saintly intercession, and gratitude religiously | That illness was “really” punishment, that all Copts held one view, or that gratitude was uncoerced |
| Social conflict | Hanafi robbery doctrine concerns group wrongdoing; shrine deeds arrange institutional obedience; letters press kin to act | Prevalence of violence, neglect, conflict, confinement, or harmonious tolerance |
| Medical description | AIU VII.E.149 names recurrent `marad` and proposed consultation with `al-hukama'` | A diagnosis, therapeutic method, outcome, or equivalence with modern medicine |
| Rule | Juristic punishment doctrine and contractual shrine obligations | Routine practice |
| Implementation | The letters document appeals actually written; the deeds document instruments actually produced | That requested travel, physician visits, shrine duties, food, clothing, or cures occurred as promised |

## Myth-risk safeguards (not completed reception chains)

The packet did not retain reproducible popular exemplars or complete upstream reception chains.
The cautions below are internal tests of what this exact corpus cannot bear; they are not findings
about the prevalence or origin of a popular story.

### Universal family care or tolerance — unsupported

Two letters show concern and effort; they also show separation, bodily frailty, pressure, and a
missing ill person's voice. Three shrine deeds show families arranging represented healing and
support while also transferring service and obedience. The corpus supports neither “families
abandoned people” nor “families always cared tolerantly.” Poverty is visible only indirectly through
travel/frailty and work/payment alternatives; the exact selected corpus contains no household
budget or poor person's account.

### Demonology-only — unsupported by this selected corpus

The retained P.KRU material frames healing through a saint, water, altar, vow, and miracle, but
P.KRU 80 and 104 describe severe illness without making a demon the only category in the
retained passages. The Geniza father names an illness/relapse and proposes physicians. This is
enough to show that an exclusive description does not fit this selected corpus. It is not enough to
measure how common any explanation was or to claim a completed history of the popular story.

### Religion versus medicine — false binary for this corpus, but coexistence is not quantified

AIU VII.E.149 places family action and physicians in one plan. The P.KRU deeds place ritual,
water, contractual form, labor, and institutional provision in another. These are different
communities and centuries; combining them proves diversity in the packet, not a universal
integration of faith and medicine.

### Benevolent sanctuary — one-sided

Schenke argues that P.KRU service belonged to a healing shrine and included institutional duties
to feed and clothe. The same documents use lifelong service, obedience, and parental transfer;
the known donation/service dossier discussed here is all male. The affected children's reactions are mostly absent. A responsible account
must hold benefit, dependency, power, and missing consent together.

## Negative findings

- No retained source documents a psychiatric hospital, household confinement, shackling, or a
  specialized mental-health practitioner.
- A four-query heuristic search was run, but no candidate-by-candidate screened-result trail was
  preserved. It cannot establish absence; no implementation source is linked in the packet, and
  implementation remains **Unknown**.
- Schenke's opened P.KRU 80 discussion does not report Abraham's assent; the full Till translation
  remains unopened. P.KRU 79's later assent is exceptional, not a baseline.
- No prevalence, outcome rate, family-tolerance rate, treatment efficacy, or representative
  Islamicate/Coptic/Jewish practice can be calculated.
- No modern diagnosis can be assigned to the letter writers, the ill relatives, a `majnun`, or a
  shrine patient from this evidence.
