## Power and material-condition audit

### Gender and age

- The two household letters preserve one woman's direct voice and one father's voice; both ill
  addressees are male and silent.
- Schenke reports that the twenty-six known child-donation documents concern boys. P.KRU 79
  offers an older son's assent, while Schenke's opened P.KRU 80 discussion does not report one.
  The surviving donation/service dossier discussed here is therefore all male; it does not prove
  that girls were excluded from shrine access or healing.
- P.KRU duties and authority are age- and status-asymmetric. “Parents consented” is not a
  substitute for the child's consent.

### Status, enslavement, and dependency

- Al-Kasani's same doctrinal page names free and enslaved legal statuses when discussing who can
  incur the fixed penalty. This is not evidence that status made no social difference.
- P.KRU language compares service to that of a person acquired for money, while Schenke argues
  for religious/shrine service rather than simply monastic free labor. Monastic legal ownership,
  broad commands, lifelong obligation, and provision coexist. The packet leaves the exact status
  disputed (`DIS-017001`).
- Do not call P.KRU donors “patients who chose employment.” Do not call them enslaved without
  explaining the live terminological and legal dispute.

### Poverty, cost, and access

- AIU VII.E.149 documents frailty as an access constraint: relatives say they cannot travel to
  fetch the sick son. It supplies no treatment cost.
- P.KRU instruments can attach labor, annual payment, wages, lamp oil, food, clothing, and
  lodging to healing/service. This material exchange must accompany any description of shrine
  care.
- Schenke reports competing poverty/unwanted-child interpretations in prior scholarship but
  argues against treating them as established. Richter is now opened and maps poverty, exposure,
  sale, religious-vow, and monastic-economic interpretations while emphasizing the sparse direct
  evidence for acute economic pressure in the deeds. Other antecedent works mapped by Schenke and
  Richter were not independently opened. The motive remains disputed, and the packet cannot assign
  poverty as the cause of any individual donation.

### Consent and refusal

- Direct refusal survives nowhere in the core.
- P.KRU 79: later assent is explicitly noted and should be reported as exceptional.
- P.KRU 80: parental and institutional agreement is represented; Schenke's opened discussion does
  not report the child's assent. Do not make a full-document silence claim until Till is opened.
- P.KRU 104: adult self-donation and capacity formula survive, but social/religious pressure and
  future exit options are not independently documented.
- AIU VII.E.149: the father commands an immediate return; the son's choice is unknown.

### Violence and confinement

- The Hanafi passages concern severe corporal penalties; legal exemption from one penalty is not
  a comprehensive practice of mercy.
- No core source documents household or shrine confinement, chains, cells, restraint, or forced
  medical treatment. Lifelong service and obedience are dependency/control evidence, not proof
  of physical confinement.
- No violence by kin against an ill relative is documented; absence in seven selected items is not
  prevalence evidence.

### Community support and family labor

- Letters show information networks, kin pressure, travel, lodging/family return, and a plan to
  engage physicians.
- P.KRU deeds make families, shrine officials, scribes, witnesses, workers, and donors part of a
  support-and-obligation network. The labor of maintaining water basins, lamps, cleaning, and
  bread distribution is part of care infrastructure, but the workers' experience is mostly silent.

## Archive formation

- Geniza survival is accidental/selective. PGP warns that provenance can be uncertain and that
  editions/transcriptions follow varying conventions. Two letters cannot represent Jewish
  households generally.
- The P.KRU deeds are inferred from content to belong to the Apa Phoibammon institutional archive
  and were written to make transfers durable. They were not recovered from a securely documented
  excavation context. Their legal form privileges donors, witnesses, and monastery officials.
  Schenke notes insecure find-provenance for the group and destruction of the site's later Christian
  layers without adequate excavation documentation. The consulted Crum–Steindorff scan is a later
  reprint of the original 1912 edition, not evidence of find-provenance.
- Juristic manuals preserve arguments that teachers/jurists considered; they do not enumerate
  cases or recipient reactions. Hamilton's colonial-era translation adds a second mediation layer.

## Empathy controls for future writing

1. Name whose words survive before interpreting an experience.
2. Do not turn rhetorical distress into diagnosis.
3. Present provision and control together when discussing shrine service.
4. Never equate exemption from punishment with a person's entire social treatment.
5. Avoid comic or sensational language around `majnun`, vows, demons, corporal penalty, or
   child donation.
6. Say “the source narrates healing,” not “the child was cured,” unless a claim is explicitly
   limited to the document's representation.
7. Do not make medieval families morally exemplary or monstrous from this sample.
