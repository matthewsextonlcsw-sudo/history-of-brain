## What this corpus lets us hear

### A rare first-person interior account

Augustine's grief narrative is the closest retained evidence to a person reflecting on his own distress. It is unusually useful because the sequence includes bodily and social actions, self-judgment, and change over time. It is still not transparent access to an event: Augustine writes retrospectively, addresses God, frames grief morally and theologically, and selects details for a literary work. Public use should preserve both its intimacy and its construction.

### A woman's speech preserved through a son

Monica's voice is not absent, but it is not direct. Augustine supplies the scene, paraphrase or remembered wording, and the interpretation. Empathetic wording should not erase her agency, yet it must say who reports her. The exact safe formula is: “Augustine reports that Monica…”

### Modeled and narrated sufferers

Evagrius's monk is a pedagogical subject. Athanasius's sufferers are figures in a sacred life story. Gregory's people with leprosy are made to carry a bishop's argument about exemplary charity. Each can reveal a source-world: what authors noticed, feared, praised, stigmatized, or prescribed. None automatically reveals the sufferer's explanation or evaluation.

### Institutional recipients without institutional testimony

The rules and laws name sick, poor, weak, and dependent people, but authority speaks. Material support and restriction appear together: more food can accompany separation; attendance can accompany controlled access; an inheritance can be assigned to recipients while administrators retain discretion. This is why “care” and “control” must be written together without assuming either benevolence or cruelty beyond evidence.

## Voices not recovered in the narrowed pass

No direct, source-verified testimony was found from:

- a resident or recipient of Basil's charitable complex;
- a patient in a civic hospital or monastic infirmary;
- a poor person affected by the Justinianic inheritance rule;
- a person labeled possessed describing the experience in their own words;
- an institutional cook, nurse, attendant, servant, porter, or lower-status worker;
- an enslaved person receiving or providing care;
- a non-elite lay woman describing grief, unusual experience, or institutional care;
- a child, chronically ill person, or disabled person interpreting their own treatment;
- Jewish, pagan, Syriac Christian, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, or Coptic-language communities within the broader 200–800 brief.

This is a finding about the opened archive, not proof that such voices never existed. Search, survival, literacy, copying, language, modern editions, digitization, and access shape the silence.

## Empathy and harm controls for later writing

1. Name the power relationship: who writes, who is described, and who is silent.
2. Avoid using historical insults or dehumanizing descriptions as colorful scene-setting. When a source uses them, explain that the rhetoric documents stigma.
3. Do not invent how a recipient “must have felt.” Ask what the evidence permits: a restriction, separation, pain, appeal, or reported response.
4. Do not make people into props for a triumphant origin story about hospitals, psychiatry, Christianity, or the modern West.
5. Treat sacred explanations as historically real explanatory frameworks without endorsing supernatural causation or converting them into modern diagnoses.
6. Do not frame dependence as personal failure or charity as uncomplicated kindness. Rules and laws distribute resources and authority together.
7. Use present-day diagnostic labels only when discussing a clearly identified modern scholarly comparison, never as a retrospective fact.

## Future lived-experience searches

- Documentary papyri from Egyptian monasteries and households: complaints, requests for food or medicine, letters concerning illness, work rosters, and accounts.
- Epigraphy and donor/recipient inscriptions associated with Cappadocian and Constantinopolitan charitable sites.
- Coptic and Syriac lives, letters, rules, healing shrines, and legal documents in critical translation.
- Women's ascetic correspondence and lives, with genre controls and attention to male redaction.
- Archaeological reports on infirmary spaces, diet, mobility, burials, and disability—without treating skeletal pathology as a person's own voice.
- Legal petitions or dispute records that expose implementation rather than only legislation.
