Status: evidence records for internal outline construction; not public prose.

## Evidence-bearing synthesis

The checked corpus does not reveal a single “Christian view of mental health.” It contains at least five non-equivalent evidence streams: ascetic taxonomies and exercises; hagiographic representations of demons, suffering, care, and cure; autobiographical grief; monastic rules that combine material support with discipline; and imperial rules governing charitable property. The corpus is strongest for what authors and lawgivers prescribed or represented. It is weak for compliance, institutional outcomes, population prevalence, and recipients' own assessments.

### Ascetic classification and practice

#### `EVD-012000` — Evagrius's eight thoughts

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012000`, *Praktikos* ch. 6.
- **Evidence unit:** The chapter enumerates eight general tempting thoughts and places acedia sixth.
- **Genre / voice:** Ascetic manual; elite male monastic teacher addressing practitioners.
- **Level / fit:** Ancient work is a primary witness for its own taxonomy; Dysinger's consulted English is an accountable noncritical L3-B conduit. The displayed Greek is identified to Guillaumont and Guillaumont, SC 170–171 (1971), but no independent Greek collation is claimed.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012001`.
- **Limits:** It is neither a population diagnostic manual nor evidence that all Christians used the category.

#### `EVD-012001` — The “noonday demon” description

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012000`, *Praktikos* ch. 12.
- **Evidence unit:** The text locates the experience roughly from the fourth to eighth hours, depicts repeated looking from the window and at the sun, dissatisfaction with cell and life, perceived lack of charity or visitors, thoughts of family and former life, and pressure to abandon the cell; it says peace and joy follow the struggle.
- **Genre / voice:** Ascetic instruction using demonological and moral language.
- **Level / fit:** Ancient work is a primary witness for its own representation; Dysinger's consulted English is an accountable noncritical L3-B conduit.
- **Claim links:** supports `CLM-012001`; qualifies `CLM-012002`.
- **Disagreement record:** `DIS-012000`.
- **Limits:** Symptom resemblance does not establish depression, another modern disorder, or clinical prevalence.

#### `EVD-012002` — Prescribed responses to acedia

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012000`, *Praktikos* chs. 28–29.
- **Evidence unit:** The manual prescribes remaining in the cell, perseverance, and reflection on mortality and bodily duration.
- **Genre / voice:** Normative ascetic technique.
- **Level / fit:** Ancient work is a primary witness for its prescription; Dysinger's consulted English is an accountable noncritical L3-B conduit; fit C for what monks actually did.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012001`, `CLM-012015`.
- **Limits:** No outcome measurement or recipient testimony.

### Hagiographic suffering, care, and cure

#### `EVD-012003` — The vita states its imitative purpose

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012001`, *Life of Antony*, prologue; genre comparison `SRC-012002`, pp. 43–47, paras. 1–4.
- **Evidence unit:** Athanasius presents Antony as a sufficient pattern of discipline and invites imitation; Krueger analyzes the vita as both descriptive and prescriptive and Antony as a textual persona.
- **Genre / voice:** Hagiography plus specialist genre criticism.
- **Level / fit:** Ellershaw's consulted 1892 historical English is L3-A for the wording used here; the ancient vita has primary provenance but was not checked in a critical Greek edition. Krueger's scholarship is L2-B.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012005`, `CLM-012015`.
- **Limits:** The text's internal truth and eyewitness claims do not cancel literary construction.

#### `EVD-012004` — Human assistance around Antony

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012001`, *Life of Antony* chs. 8–9 and 91.
- **Evidence unit:** An acquaintance periodically brings bread, finds Antony speechless after an attributed demonic attack, carries him to a village church where kin and villagers gather; the closing chapter says two monks attended him for fifteen years because of age.
- **Genre / voice:** Hagiographic narration about an idealized holy man.
- **Level / fit:** Ellershaw's consulted 1892 historical English is L3-A for this representation; C for actual frequency, quality, or consent. Ancient primary provenance is separate from the English edition rating.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012006`.
- **Limits:** These are episodes about Antony, not representative care statistics.

#### `EVD-012005` — Possession and healing represented as sacred narrative

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012001`, *Life of Antony* chs. 38, 63–64, 83–84.
- **Evidence unit:** The vita warns against boasting of exorcism/healing and narrates people whose actions or suffering it attributes to demons and whose relief it attributes to prayer or Christ.
- **Genre / voice:** Hagiography and miracle narrative; sufferers' words/actions are author-mediated.
- **Level / fit:** Ellershaw's consulted 1892 historical English is L3-A for what this rendering asserts; D for retrospective psychiatric diagnosis or efficacy. Ancient primary provenance is separate from the English edition rating.
- **Claim links:** supports `CLM-012005`, `CLM-012013`.
- **Disagreement record:** `DIS-012002`.
- **Limits:** It cannot show that “mental illness was possession,” identify a modern diagnosis, or verify cure.

### Grief and reported family speech

#### `EVD-012006` — Monica's illness and words as reported by Augustine

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012003`, *Confessions* IX.11 §§27–28.
- **Evidence unit:** Augustine narrates Monica's fever, brief loss of visible awareness, reported final instructions about burial, and request for remembrance at the altar.
- **Genre / voice:** Retrospective theological autobiography; a son's report of his mother's speech.
- **Level / fit:** Pilkington's consulted 1887 historical English is L3-A for Augustine's narration; C for Monica's exact words or general practice. Ancient primary provenance is separate from the English edition rating.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012004`.
- **Limits:** Not a transcript, medical chart, or unmediated woman's testimony.

#### `EVD-012007` — Augustine's first-person grief sequence

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012003`, *Confessions* IX.12 §§29–33.
- **Evidence unit:** Augustine describes controlling tears, communal psalmody and company, hidden recurring anguish and a “twofold sadness” at grieving, an ineffective bath, reduced grief after sleep, recollection, and private weeping that brings rest.
- **Genre / voice:** Elite male retrospective first-person theological autobiography.
- **Level / fit:** Pilkington's consulted 1887 historical English is L3-A for the narrated sequence; C for generalized coping practice. Ancient primary provenance is separate from the English edition rating.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012003`, `CLM-012011`.
- **Limits:** The account is selective, literary, theological, and not a universal grief model.

### Charity, institutional language, and the “first hospital” problem

#### `EVD-012008` — Gregory's praised “new city”

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012004`, Oration 43.63–64.
- **Evidence unit:** Gregory praises Basil's “new city,” describes people with leprosy as socially expelled “living corpses,” and depicts Basil tending them and relieving wounds.
- **Genre / voice:** Eulogy/funeral oration; episcopal praise, not an administrative or patient record.
- **Level / fit:** Browne and Swallow's consulted 1894 historical English is L3-A for rhetoric and represented stigma; C for operations. Ancient primary provenance is separate from the English edition rating.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012007`.
- **Limits:** Do not infer facility size, staffing, admissions, outcomes, patient consent, or a psychiatric mission.

#### `EVD-012009` — Basilias classification is disputed

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012005`, pp. 25–48, esp. notes 1–14, 29, 39–40, 67–71; `SRC-012006`, pp. 45–49.
- **Evidence unit:** Caner re-reads Gregory, Basil, Sozomen, and Gregory of Nyssa and argues for classifying the facility as a leprosarium rather than a hospital; Horden shows that institutional labels, the presence of doctors, and modern definitions do not map cleanly onto one another.
- **Genre / voice:** Specialist historiography.
- **Level / fit:** L2-B.
- **Claim links:** supports `CLM-012008`.
- **Disagreement record:** `DIS-012001`.
- **Limits:** Caner's thesis is a position in a live historiographic dispute. Horden's numerical hospital survey spans later centuries and is not direct proof of fourth-century Basilias.

#### `EVD-012010` — A sixth-century legal allocation rule

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012007`, C. 1.3.48 (49).3–8, Blume PDF pp. 31–32, dated 23 Aug. 531; parallel `SRC-012008`, Scott §37.3–8.
- **Evidence unit:** The constitution assigns an indefinite inheritance for “the poor” to the city's hospital; directs the superintendent to distribute support to ill residents; allows bishop and clergy to select the neediest facility where several exist; provides church distribution where no hospital exists; and prohibits administrators' personal profit.
- **Genre / voice:** Normative imperial law in a sixth-century compilation.
- **Level / fit:** Blume is the controlling L1-A edited translation for the normative rule; C for implementation. Scott is only an L3-C same-law wording comparison and is not independent evidence.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012009`, `CLM-012013`.
- **Limits:** Translation vocabulary is not a transparent institutional taxonomy. Blume and Scott number the constitution differently; Scott's terminal date is anomalous. The law supplies no recipient voice or outcome evidence.

### Monastic infirmary as care and control

#### `EVD-012011` — Material accommodations and assigned ministers

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012009`, Preface 5 and Rules 40–46; transmission metadata `SRC-012010`.
- **Evidence unit:** The rule tradition assigns ministers and superiors, permits differentiated clothing and food, reserves wine/broth for the infirmary, and instructs ministers to supply an ill traveler abundantly so the brother is not distressed.
- **Genre / voice:** Prescriptive cenobitic rule transmitted through multiple languages.
- **Level / fit:** The transmitted rule tradition has primary provenance for its prescription; the consulted English adaptation is L3-B; C for implementation.
- **Supports:** `CLM-012010`, `CLM-012015`.
- **Limits:** A rule can reveal an ideal or problem administrators anticipated; it does not prove routine provision.

#### `EVD-012012` — Infirmary access, movement, and visitation controlled

- **Source / locator:** `SRC-012009`, Rules 40–47, especially 41–43 and 47.
- **Evidence unit:** Ill brothers may not self-serve from kitchen/pantry, enter or leave care spaces at will, remove even fruit, or receive visitors—including kin—without superior authorization.
- **Genre / voice:** Prescriptive rule written from institutional authority.
- **Level / fit:** The transmitted rule tradition has primary provenance for its prescription; the consulted English adaptation is L3-B; C for implementation.
- **Claim links:** supports `CLM-012010`, `CLM-012015`.
- **Disagreement record:** `DIS-012003`.
- **Limits:** The text does not record how ill monks evaluated these controls, whether exceptions occurred, or whether rules were enforced uniformly.

## Proposed outline transitions

1. **From multiple late-antique worlds to a bounded Christian corpus:** introduce named communities and genres instead of a “Christian takeover.”
2. **Training attention and conduct:** Evagrius's thought taxonomy and monastic techniques, with acedia left in its ascetic-demonological setting.
3. **Stories that made saints:** move from manual to hagiography and explain why literary purpose changes the evidentiary question.
4. **A human grief sequence:** Augustine offers rare first-person interior narration, while Monica demonstrates how even a woman's words arrive through a male author.
5. **Care becomes organized—and governed:** place Pachomian material accommodation beside permissions, separation, and authority.
6. **Charity acquires buildings and law:** Gregory's praise, Basilias historiography, and Justinianic allocation rules show institutions without granting a modern hospital identity.
7. **What the archive will not tell us:** end on missing recipient, worker, poor, enslaved, disabled, non-Christian, and female voices, then hand off to regional and medical packets.

## Current-evidence cautions

- Never translate *acedia* into “depression” as a factual equivalence. Explain the source category first; a modern comparison, if retained at all, must be labeled disputed and independently reviewed.
- “Demon,” “possession,” “vision,” and “healing” belong to the source's explanatory and literary worlds. Do not retro-diagnose named figures or treat a miracle narrative as a clinical outcome.
- “Hospital,” “infirmary,” “hospice,” “poorhouse,” and “leprosarium” are translation and historiographic choices, not interchangeable ancient labels.
- “Christian charity” can include nourishment, attendance, religious formation, segregation, surveillance, and restricted movement. Do not force care and control into an either/or story.
- Do not infer patient satisfaction, consent, or fear from authorial silence. The empathetic response is to name the absent voice and the power relationship.
