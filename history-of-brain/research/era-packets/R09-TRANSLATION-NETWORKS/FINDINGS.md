# R09-TRANSLATION-NETWORKS — findings

**State:** internal research findings; not public prose
**Scope:** two named chains only; no survey of the whole Graeco-Arabic translation movement

## Executive finding

The packet supports an outline transition from “texts crossing languages” to “translation as textual reconstruction, collaboration, and material labor.” In both selected chains, a modern reader encounters layered artifacts, not an intact idea passed hand-to-hand. The Galen chain preserves unusually explicit translator/workshop evidence but leaves its immediate Greek-or-Syriac source unresolved. The Aristotle chain permits exact comparison between two surviving Arabic recensions but does not identify whether several differences began in the Greek exemplars, the translators' choices, or later copying.

## Proposed transitions and topics

1. **From relay to reconstruction.** Begin with Ḥunayn's own record of searching for manuscripts, collating copies, revising earlier Syriac, translating or supervising Arabic, and sometimes starting again (`SRC-013000`, §§III.1–4). Transition to the physical risks of transmission: incomplete books, copying, a reported shipboard fire, later recensions, and lost source layers.
2. **From “one translator” to a workshop and patronage network.** Name Ḥunayn and the people attached to the exact record being discussed. The *Risālah* is addressed to its sponsor ʿAlī ibn Yaḥyā; Muḥammad ibn Mūsā commissions translations in other, nonselected Galenic entries. Neither is thereby established as commissioner of the selected *Epidemics VI* version or Isḥāq's *De anima*. Isḥāq, Ḥubayš, Ayyūb, ʿĪsā, secretaries, scribes, owners, and custodians likewise enter only with a chain-specific role. Do not make “Ḥunayn” a brand that erases collaborators, or make comparator actors patrons of the selected texts.
3. **A clinician-facing passage changes audience.** Use Galen, *Epidemics VI* commentary II 45, critical-edition pp. 392–397, to show how an Arabic medical text discusses conversation, perceived reason, disclosure, distress, attendants, and change from a person's prior behavior. The passage is prescriptive and paternalistic; it is not a patient record or outcome study.
4. **Words for mind are not modern diagnoses.** Keep the Arabic terms and the modern translator's choices visible: `ʿaql` is rendered contextually as mental state/capacity and rationality; `ḏihn` anchors a statement about the mind being affected; `ġamm wa-sūʾ al-ẓann` is rendered “despondent and depressed.” The final English word must not be back-projected as major depressive disorder.
5. **Two Arabic *De anima* witnesses, two lexical worlds.** Compare Isḥāq and pseudo-Isḥāq at Aristotle 402a22–402b7: `mabādiʾ` / `awāʾil`, `maqūlāt` / `nuʿūt`, `ḥadduhā` / `maʿnā`, `nafs al-insān` / `al-nafs al-nāṭiqa`, and `istikmāl` / transliterated `anṭalāshiyā` plus `tamām` (`SRC-013004`, pp. 150, 152–153).
6. **An omission can open another reading without revealing motive.** At 413b25–27, the Isḥāq recension contains the phrase that “this alone” may be separable; pseudo-Isḥāq lacks the equivalent of `τοῦτο μόνον ἐνδέχεσθαι χωρίζεσθαι`. Jabbour argues that this can enable a different reading of what is separable, while explicitly leaving the cause of the difference unresolved (`SRC-013004`, pp. 154–155 and p. 152 n. 39).
7. **The archive is the story.** End the unit by showing that the “Isḥāq translation” is reconstructed from lemmata embedded in a later Iranian manuscript of Avicennian annotations, while the older complete Arabic version survives in Ayasofya 2450 under a false attribution. The manuscript's composite form, missing colophon, unknown copyists, and later date are evidence, not housekeeping details.

## Chain 1 — Galen, *Commentary on Hippocrates' Epidemics VI*

### Reconstructed path

`Galen's Greek commentary (2nd–3rd c. CE)` → `Greek manuscript(s) and/or a Syriac source; immediate Vorlage unknown` → `Arabic version produced in Ḥunayn's ninth-century Baghdad workshop` → `Scorialensis arab. 805 / Ambrosianus B 135 sup. and other direct/indirect witnesses` → `Vagelpohl 2022 Arabic critical edition + English translation`

### What is demonstrated

- The *Risālah* says Ayyūb al-Ruhāwī had made a Syriac translation; Ḥunayn possessed a complete manuscript and translated the entire commentary into Arabic, but the entry does not specify the manuscript's language (`SRC-013003`, pp. 75–76).
- Neither principal Arabic manuscript directly credits Ḥunayn. David Colville's later note at Ambrosianus B 135 sup., fol. 185r says he copied from Ḥunayn's translation. Seventeen embedded notes introduced with `qāla Ḥunayn` and explicit reference to Greek strongly support workshop attribution, but do not eliminate every authorship question (`SRC-013003`, pp. 76–83; `SRC-013002`).
- The notes reveal omission, amplification, cultural explanation, gap-filling, and correction. One note says Greek grammatical examples from Homer and Plato were omitted because they would not work in Arabic; another explains Greek “breaths” and bodies for Arabic readers (`SRC-013003`, pp. 77–78, 82–83).
- The selected mental-life passage, Part II 45, addresses how a physician interprets conversation and departures from prior behavior and calibrates disclosure to a judgment of the patient's reason and fear. Family/attendants may receive information withheld or softened for the patient (`SRC-013003`, pp. 392–397).

### What is not demonstrated

- A Syriac intermediary for this specific Arabic version is **Unknown**. Ayyūb's Syriac version existed, and Ḥunayn often used Syriac, but the translator notes may indicate direct Greek translation or at least Greek checking.
- The packet has not independently aligned the Greek wording of all of Part II 45 with the Arabic. It cannot label `ʿaql`, `ḏihn`, or `ġamm wa-sūʾ al-ẓann` as translator-created additions.
- The passage does not establish routine practice, patient consent, clinical efficacy, or how patients experienced selective disclosure.

### Exact mental-life anchor

| locus | Arabic / modern edition signal | Safe research meaning | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Part II 45, pp. 392–395 | `ʿaql` rendered across the English as “mental state,” “mental (capacity),” and “rational” | Conversation is treated as evidence about a patient's capacities and as a reason to vary disclosure. | The English semantic range belongs to Vagelpohl's translation; no one-to-one timeless category. |
| Part II 45, pp. 394–395 | `ġamm wa-sūʾ al-ẓann`, rendered “despondent and depressed” | The text predicts increased distress when reassurance is disproved. | “Depressed” is ordinary English in this translation, not a modern diagnosis. |
| Part II 45, pp. 396–397 | `ḏihn`; speech/behavior compared with the person's earlier character | The text treats marked change in behavior, speech, alertness, and voice as signs for the physician. | Elite prescriptive gaze; the patient does not narrate the experience. |

## Chain 2 — Aristotle, *De anima* I.1–II.2

### Reconstructed path

`Aristotle's Greek De anima` → two distinct Arabic translations:

- `older anonymous/pseudo-Isḥāq Arabic version` → `Ayasofya 2450, complete Arabic witness`; and
- `Arabic version associated with Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn (d. 910)` → `lemmas embedded in an Avicennian commentary recension` → `Tehran, Ṭabāṭabāʾī 1367, T2 pp. 88–127, which Jabbour identifies as preserving a nearly continuous Isḥāq-recension witness for I.1 402a21–II.2 413b27`.

No Syriac intermediary is demonstrated for either selected *De anima* version in the opened evidence.

### Manuscript anchors and cautions

- Jabbour identifies the Ṭabāṭabāʾī 1367 lemmata as an Isḥāq-recension witness in a specialist edition announcement; the complete edition was still forthcoming in the consulted article. The object is a composite volume made from remnants of three manuscripts. Its T2 portion is probably later than the fourteenth century. It has no dated colophon and does not identify its copyists (`SRC-013004`, pp. 137–141).
- The author could not perform a physical autopsy because travel to Iran was not possible; Teymour Morel checked pagination and structure locally (`SRC-013004`, p. 139 n. 14).
- The only complete Arabic translation is the older version in Ayasofya 2450. Its manuscript title attributes it to Isḥāq, but specialist comparison refuted that attribution; call it pseudo-Isḥāq, not “Isḥāq's second translation” (`SRC-013004`, p. 139).
- Badawī's 1954 edition intervened heavily and sometimes reconstructed Arabic from modern Greek; the packet's comparisons therefore use Jabbour's manuscript transcription, not Badawī alone (`SRC-013004`, p. 149 n. 35).

### Exact comparison anchors

| Aristotle locus | Isḥāq recension | Pseudo-Isḥāq recension | What the difference establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 402a22, `ἀρχαί` | `mabādiʾ` | `awāʾil` | Different Arabic philosophical vocabulary. |
| 402a25, `κατηγοριῶν` | `maqūlāt` | `nuʿūt` | Different categorizing terminology. |
| 402b5–7, `ὁ λόγος αὐτῆς` | `ḥadduhā` | `maʿnā` | A definition/meaning contrast in the surviving renderings. |
| 402b4, `περὶ τῆς ἀνθρωπίνης μόνης` | `fī amr nafs al-insān` | `ʿan al-nafs al-nāṭiqa` | “Human soul” versus “rational soul” phrasing. |
| `ἐντελέχεια` | `istikmāl` | `anṭalāshiyā` plus `tamām` | One version uses a later-dominant technical term; the other exposes novelty through transliteration plus paraphrase. |
| 413b25–27 | includes `hāḏā waḥduhu qad yumkin an yufāriq` | lacks the “this alone may be separable” equivalent | The omission can permit a broader reading of separability; cause and intent remain Unknown. |

## Labor, confession/community, patronage, and material setting

- Ḥunayn's *Risālah* was addressed to the courtier and sponsor ʿAlī ibn Yaḥyā al-Munaǧǧim and explicitly records for whom particular translations were produced, translator competence and age, available manuscripts, previous translators, correction, and language (`SRC-013000`, §III.1). This addressee relationship does not establish that ʿAlī commissioned the selected *Epidemics VI* version.
- The selected Galen chain belongs to the work of an Arabic- and Syriac-using Christian physician-translator circle in Abbasid Baghdad. “Christian” is not a single institutional identity: sources distinguish individuals and communities, and workshop/religious influence claims remain debated (`SRC-013002`, especially introduction and conclusion).
- Across the broader *Risālah*/anthology comparator corpus, patrons included court officials, physicians, and the Banū Mūsā network. Muḥammad ibn Mūsā is attached to nonselected named Galen translations, not to either selected chain. These comparators demonstrate plural private/courtly networks but cannot supply the missing patron, place, or purpose of Isḥāq's *De anima* (`SRC-013001`, §§II.8–10, III.1–5).
- A later report says Ḥunayn could be paid in silver equal to a manuscript's weight and identifies his secretary al-Azraq, heavy paper, large script, and wide spacing (`SRC-013001`, §III.5). Treat this as Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿah's thirteenth-century report, not a ninth-century payroll record.

## Myth-chain audits

The following are **reception audits**, not sources for medieval practice. No origin is claimed
where the downstream exemplar lacks a traceable claim-specific citation.

### “The House of Wisdom translated Greek science”

**Dated downstream repetition:** Khan Academy's “Golden age of Islam” video was uploaded 17/18 February 2017 depending display timezone (`upload_date=20170218`; timestamp `1487384981` is 18 February UTC and 17 February in U.S. time zones). At 02:30–02:47 it says al-Maʾmūn created in Baghdad what is called the House of Wisdom and describes it as a center of learning across named disciplines. The subject then shifts: at 02:47–03:12, “over the next several hundred years” Abbasid caliphs sponsor scholars working with Sanskrit, Greek, Chinese, and Persian knowledge, translation into Arabic, and collection in libraries in cities like Baghdad and in “one place” (`SRC-013009`). These are adjacent statements, not one exact House-centered proposition. Neither segment has a claim-specific citation, so the upstream origins are **Unknown**.

**Upstream check and resolution:** `SRC-013001` identifies Salm as head of a caliphal library called the House of Wisdom in chain-specific reports about the *Almagest* and a book mission. Neither selected chain is placed there. The video is L4-A for its own 2017 proposition and D for either selected chain. Use named patrons, workshops, manuscripts, and libraries; do not make one caliphal library the universal engine.

### “Arabs preserved Greek knowledge for the West”

**Dated downstream repetition:** The same video makes two distinct bridge statements. At 04:31–04:38 it says the Hindu numeral system was brought to the Islamic empire and was a bridge to bringing it “to the West.” At 08:50–09:05 it calls the Islamic world/empires between the eighth and thirteenth centuries a “powerful bridge of human knowledge,” names Indian, Chinese, and ancient Greek knowledge, and places that bridge during Europe's “Dark Ages.” The later segment does **not** say all that knowledge was carried “to the West.” Neither segment carries a claim-specific citation, so both upstream origins are **Unknown**.

**Upstream check and resolution:** The selected *Risālah* material documents contemporary patrons and readers for particular Galenic translations; it does not make later Europe their demonstrated purpose. The original patron, place, precise date, and reader context of the selected *De anima* version remain Unknown. A 1380–1420 Hebrew manuscript is one later reception node, not evidence of original purpose. The video is L4-A for its own framing and D for either chain's purpose. Replace the teleological bridge with dated, named reception nodes; do not claim Latin/European reception in this packet without a direct retained record.

### “Translation was a frictionless relay”

**Dated downstream repetition:** In the fourteenth-century *Pouring Rain*, al-Ṣafadī reduces translation to two methods: sequential word substitution and whole-sentence meaning transfer. He calls Ḥunayn's second method superior and says his nonmathematical translations need no correction (`SRC-013001`, §III.7, *Kitāb al-ġayṯ al-musaǧǧam* 46.12–25). The 2022 editors quote it precisely because it became authoritative through repetition while offering a misleadingly simple account. The framework's origin before al-Ṣafadī is not established here.

**Upstream check and resolution:** The opened *Risālah* entries and both selected chains document corrupt/missing exemplars, collation, revision, retranslation, loss, amplification, omission, explanation, ambiguous attribution, and later recension. Al-Ṣafadī is L2-A through the named modern translation for his own later proposition and D for ninth-century method as a whole. Preserve it as a reception/history-of-method claim, not evidence of a frictionless process.

### “A single genius preserved the corpus”

**Dated downstream repetition:** Bink Hallum's British Library/Qatar Digital Library essay of 14 October 2014 says Ḥunayn “managed to translate” into Arabic and Syriac the Galen books available to him, says his terminology decision “helped establish” Arabic as an international language of science, and later calls him “instrumental” through his efforts and terminology decision (`SRC-013010`, `Overview` and opening `Tell It to Me Straight, Doc!`). These are Hallum's qualified verbs; they do not say Ḥunayn single-handedly translated every work or solely established scientific Arabic. The page lists a broad secondary bibliography but does not map these sentences to a source/locator, so their exact upstream origin is **Unknown**.

**Upstream check and resolution:** Ḥunayn is unusually visible because his participant record survives, but that same record and the critical scholarship name earlier translators, his son, nephew, pupils, patrons, correctors, scribes, secretaries, owners, and revisers. The QDL essay is L4-A for its 2014 public framing and C/D for personal total-corpus attribution. Credit must follow each chain; QDL does not independently corroborate the selected *Epidemics VI* authorship.
