# R09-TRANSLATION-NETWORKS — lived experience and archive audit

**State:** internal audit; not public prose

## Core finding

This is principally an archive of authors, translators, patrons, manuscripts, and scholars. It offers rare detail about intellectual labor and a physician's prescriptive stance toward patients, but almost no unmediated account from a patient, family member, ordinary reader, scribe, or student. The absence must travel with the future outline rather than appear only as a final disclaimer.

## Patient-experience anchor: Galen II 45

### What the passage makes visible

- A clinical conversation is imagined as both information-gathering and a test of the speaker's capacities.
- The physician compares present speech and behavior with a known baseline when possible.
- The physician may soften information for someone judged fearful or lacking reason, while telling attendants the situation more directly.
- The text recognizes that excessive reassurance can deepen later distress when events contradict it.
- The patient's adherence and attendants' actions are treated as causal in outcome, while the physician retains interpretive authority.

### Ethical and empathetic treatment in future writing

- Describe the physician's proposed conduct without endorsing insulting historical labels translated as “fool” or “coward.” Paraphrase them as the text's judgments and preserve enough wording to expose the hierarchy.
- Center the conflict: the text is concerned with avoiding distress and securing cooperation, yet it permits deception or selective disclosure based on a physician's judgment. Both sides belong in the story.
- Do not infer that the patient lacked capacity, that family disclosure was welcome, or that this was a routine standard.
- Do not convert “despondent and depressed” into major depressive disorder, a mood-disorder category, or evidence of psychiatric treatment.
- Where modern comparison is later added, label it as comparison: present-day informed consent, capacity assessment, confidentiality, supported decision-making, and family involvement are not proven continuities.

## Labor and status audit

### Visible labor

- translator/editor: Ḥunayn;
- earlier translators: Ayyūb, Sergius, Ibn Šahdā;
- collaborators/revisers: Isḥāq, Ḥubayš, ʿĪsā ibn Yaḥyā;
- patron/addressee: ʿAlī ibn Yaḥyā al-Munaǧǧim for the *Risālah* itself; this does not prove he commissioned the selected *Epidemics VI* version;
- comparator patrons/commissioners: Muḥammad ibn Mūsā, Salmawayh, Buḫtīšūʿ, and others for specifically named nonselected entries in the bounded *Risālah*/anthology corpus; none supplies the missing patron of Isḥāq's selected *De anima* version;
- secretary/copyist in a later report: al-Azraq;
- later copyists, owners, librarians, catalogers, conservators, and modern editors.

### Structurally hidden labor

- unnamed scribes and paper makers;
- people transporting manuscripts and the labor affected by loss/fire;
- household carers serving patients;
- students/readers who tested terminology;
- women, enslaved people, poor people, rural communities, and nonliterate speakers whose knowledge or care work rarely enters these records;
- custodial labor in Iranian, Ottoman/Turkish, Italian, Spanish, Israeli, German, and digital institutions.

### Payment evidence caution

The often-repeated silver-by-weight story comes through Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿah, writing centuries later, who says he saw heavy-paper manuscripts in al-Azraq's hand and inferred deliberate profit maximization (`SRC-013001`, §III.5). It may illuminate the later memory and materiality of translation, but it is not an opened ninth-century account book and should not be narrated as settled fact.

## Confession/community audit

- Ḥunayn and several colleagues were Christians working in Syriac and Arabic under Abbasid political and patronage structures. “Christian translator” does not identify one denomination, one school, one ethnicity, or one relationship to Greek culture.
- Syriac versions were not merely disposable stepping-stones to Arabic; named Syriac-reading physicians commissioned and used them (`SRC-013002`; `SRC-013003`, p. 78 n. 1).
- Muslim patrons, court officials, scholars, and later authors are active nodes; “Christians preserved Greek science for Muslims” is as flattening as the opposite civilizational slogan.
- Religious and political narratives about translation include propaganda and polemic. Chain-specific material evidence outranks a dream story or triumphal cultural genealogy.

## Manuscript and archive cautions

1. **Survival is selective.** Much of Galen's Greek commentary is lost and survives through Arabic; that makes the Arabic witness important but not transparent.
2. **Titles can mislead.** Ayasofya 2450's title attributes its version to Isḥāq, but specialist comparison rejects that attribution.
3. **Commentary can preserve a lost translation.** Isḥāq's *De anima* survives in lemmata embedded in a later Avicennian commentary recension, not as a complete standalone copy.
4. **Composite manuscripts have histories.** Ṭabāṭabāʾī 1367 joins remnants from different periods and hands; the current volume is not the original translation context.
5. **Modern access shapes claims.** Jabbour could not autopsy the Tehran manuscript and relied on images plus a local pagination/structure check. The packet did not open Treiger 2017 because the repository served an anti-bot challenge.
6. **Modern editions are interventions.** Badawī's edition of pseudo-Isḥāq reconstructs from Greek in places. Jabbour's manuscript transcription is therefore the comparison anchor.
7. **A modern identification is not a colophon.** Jabbour identifies the Ṭabāṭabāʾī lemmata as an Isḥāq-recension witness in a specialist edition announcement. The complete edition was still forthcoming, the manuscript lacks a dated colophon/named copyist, and Jabbour did not physically inspect it.

## Missing voices to make explicit in the outline

- patients and relatives affected by disclosure decisions;
- ordinary Arabic and Syriac readers of medical works;
- women as patients, carers, translators, patrons, or readers;
- enslaved, poor, disabled, rural, and nonliterate people;
- unnamed workshop assistants and copyists;
- Iranian manuscript custodians' own account of the object and access history;
- users who encountered `nafs`, `ʿaql`, `ḏihn`, and related terms outside elite philosophical and medical writing.

## Publication-language gates

- Prefer “person/patient described or imagined by the text” to an unsupported diagnostic label.
- Prefer “surviving Arabic recension differs” to “translator changed Aristotle.”
- Prefer “Ḥunayn's workshop orbit” to unqualified personal authorship where the edition retains doubt.
- Prefer “immediate source language is unknown” to assuming Syriac or Greek.
- Prefer “this selected chain is not linked to the House of Wisdom” to denying the library's existence.
- Prefer the demonstrated “later Hebrew reception” node to “preserved for the West”; Latin/European reception is not retained in this packet.
