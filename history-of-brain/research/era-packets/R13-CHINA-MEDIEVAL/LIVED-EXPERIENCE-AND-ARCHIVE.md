## Status, class, gender, labor, and coercion

### Status and access

- Sun's ethics passage explicitly rejects discrimination by rank and wealth at the level of professional ideal (`EVD-020100`). It cannot establish equal access or actual treatment.
- Xu's cases emerge from a literate physician-official's self-authorizing book. Named patients and households appear because they help demonstrate medical virtuosity; readers should not treat the surviving sample as a population.
- Yuan Cai writes for property-holding male household heads. Servants, maidservants, concubines, wives, and hired/enslaved people are managed subjects. His occasional language of compassion coexists with accepting forced labor and corporal punishment.
- Song Ci writes for investigating officials. Bodies and witnesses enter the archive through administrative requirements, not because the affected people elected to narrate their lives.

### Gender

- The Tang/Song medical compilers are male; women are often organized as a separate therapeutic category and rarely speak. The packet does not infer women's beliefs from male-authored prescriptions.
- Yuan Cai explicitly stereotypes women and advises male heads to regulate women's punishment of female servants. This gives evidence of patriarchal household structure and authorial bias, not an empirical psychology of women.
- “Female servant” in Song Ci's scenario marks a legal/status category. It should not be generalized to all women, and the manual gives no direct account from the deceased.

### Coercion

- *Qianjin yifang* prescribes multi-person restraint and applied force. The packet calls it coercive even though the manual frames it as therapy and includes an injury warning.
- Yuan Cai normalizes controlled whipping even as he warns against anger and death. Reduced violence is not absence of coercion.
- Song Ci's procedural scrutiny occurs in a punitive legal system. Warnings about evidence do not establish trauma-informed or equitable practice.

### Self-harm and death language

The public project must avoid lurid scene-setting, retrospective motive certainty, and suicide-as-spectacle. Source categories should be named carefully: Yuan Cai's `輕生而自殘` and `自經`, and Song Ci's `自害自縊`. Modern “suicide” may be useful in explanation only after translation review, since the sources distinguish acts and circumstances without supplying a modern psychological construct.

## Family and community

Households are active care and pressure settings, not background decoration:

- Mr. Qiu's household challenges the physician and may shape treatment decisions (`EVD-020105`).
- Yuan Cai assigns owners, neighbors, guarantors, and officials different roles when a servant is ill, injured, or dead (`EVD-020111–EVD-020112`).
- Song Ci instructs investigators to question and compare neighbors and kin, while warning about bribery, conflict, illiteracy, and scribal substitution (`EVD-020115`).

None of these sources supports a simple modern opposition between “family care” and “professional care.” Household members could advocate, pressure, punish, rescue, conceal, report, or mediate.

## Missing voices

- No unmediated patient diary, petition, letter, or oral testimony appears in the bounded packet.
- No servant or enslaved person's first-person account appears.
- No woman author or healer speaks in the retained core.
- Poor people, children, disabled people, and people described with cognitive difference are not represented through direct accounts; Sun's nondiscrimination ideal names wealth, age, and `愚智`, but does not establish access or lived experience.
- Rural, frontier, non-Han, non-literate, and non-elite healing worlds are largely absent.
- Buddhist/Daoist ritual communities appear through elite textual synthesis, not community consultation.
- We do not know how often prescriptions were used, refused, adapted, or resisted.
- We do not know costs, material availability, waiting time, disability access, injury rates, survival, or satisfaction.

These are publication gaps, not invitations to invent composite voices. Later research should seek letters, legal case dossiers, tomb texts, local gazetteer cases, institutional registers, excavated manuscripts, and scholarship centered on women, servants, healers outside official medicine, and religious communities.

## Cultural and editorial protocol

- Preserve Chinese titles/terms beside cautious English; have a historian of Chinese medicine check translations.
- Credit Main Library, Kyoto University for any permitted use of RB00002954 images.
- Do not reproduce ritual instructions as actionable health advice.
- Use a content note for coercive restraint, beating, self-harm, and death investigation.
- Do not frame living Chinese medicine, Buddhist, or Daoist communities as direct survivals of one medieval passage.
- Seek specialist and, where public use concerns living religious heritage, community review before publication.
- Place affected people and power relations in the narrative without fabricating their interior states.
