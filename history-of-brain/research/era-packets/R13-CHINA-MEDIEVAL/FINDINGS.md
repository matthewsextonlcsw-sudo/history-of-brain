## Setting 1 — Tang compilations through later witnesses

### Physician conduct is a normative ideal, not a patient survey

In the received *Beiji qianjin yaofang*, juan 1, “論大醫精誠第二,” the displayed witness reads:

> 凡大醫治病必當安神定志無欲無求先發大慈惻隱之心……若有疾厄來求救者不得問其貴賤貧富長幼妍媸怨親善友華夷愚智普同一等……見彼苦惱若己有之……

The packet gloss is that the ideal physician should settle attention and intention, put aside desire, arouse compassion, and not discriminate among people seeking care by rank, wealth, age, appearance, relationship, cultural identity, or intelligence. This is authorial professional ethics. It does not establish how healers behaved or how patients experienced care. Steavu locates the same passage at *Beiji qianjin yaofang* 1.16b and argues that Buddhist concepts and vocabulary inform it (pp. 40–41 and n. 75). That supports a specific exchange argument, not a claim that the text is “the Chinese Hippocratic Oath” or a timeless national ethic.

### A later witness preserves ritual restraint and interrogation

The 1770 Kyoto witness of *Qianjin yifang*, juan 30, “禁邪病第十五,” p1209 begins:

> 凡鬼邪著人，或啼或哭，或嗔或笑，或歌或詠，稱先亡姓字，令人癲狂，有此狀者，名曰鬼邪……

This is a diplomatic character-level transcription of the visible 1770 witness forms (`著`,
`詠`, `稱`, `癲狂`, `狀`); the commas and ellipsis are packet punctuation, not a claim about
the witness's original punctuation.

The following instructions span Kyoto canvases p1209–p1210. They direct helpers to hold the affected person's hands/wrists and apply pressure or piercing at the shoulder-well area, scaling force to the person's strength; the operator interrogates the alleged entity through the person and records names, places, and demands before offering substitute goods and sending it away. The manual warns against injury from the sharp implement, but still prescribes non-consensual restraint. The text is evidence for a received ritual-medical category and a prescriptive coercive technique, not evidence that a named person was treated, consented, improved, or described the episode in their own words.

Fang's full two-page dissertation notice says that her complete annotated translation of the *Jinjing* used the oldest extant version then identified, dated 1307, and analyzed ritual etiologies, agents, healers, and therapy (pp. 543–544). That makes the seventh-century attribution a transmission question: the 1770 page is direct evidence for that witness, while Tang wording and use remain conditional.

## Setting 2 — Southern Song Zhenzhou medical print and case writing

Chen's full chapter 3 provides the packet's control. *Puji benshifang* is securely within Xu Shuwei's oeuvre because he names it in his writings; Chen dates its completion and printing after 1143 (pp. 79–80). By contrast, *Shanghan jiushi lun* is not mentioned by Xu or Southern Song writers, first appears in fragments in the 1408 *Yongle dadian*, contains an anachronistic substitution of Chen Baxian for Liang Wudi, uses different language, and contradicts Xu's secure works in some prescriptions. Chen proposes a later compilation using drafts or inherited case material and excludes its cases from her Song analysis (pp. 80–82). The packet follows that boundary.

### Household pressure around Mr. Qiu

Chen translates and analyzes a securely attributed *Puji benshifang* case in which Xu declined to sweat Mr. Qiu because of the pulse pattern, prescribed a different decoction, and represented household members as repeatedly urging a diaphoretic and becoming nearly rude. The physician-narrator says he endured the pressure, kept his plan, and the patient recovered after about five days (pp. 96–97). Chen notes that the written caution portrays household impatience as a danger. The household has mediated speech; Mr. Qiu does not speak. The cure is the author's literary claim.

### Clouded mind and raving are source descriptions, not a modern diagnosis

In another securely attributed case, Chen reports a person with dysentery whose body felt hot, mind was clouded, who raved and could not sleep. Xu justified *Xiao Chengqi Tang* by citing received classics and represented observers as astonished (pp. 97–98). Chen's n. 65 explicitly says this English translation is based on Goldschmidt, *Medical Practice*, pp. 287–289; those upstream pages were not opened, so Chen and Goldschmidt are one translation-dependence chain rather than independent confirmation. Chen immediately asks whether these debates happened as narrated or were constructed to advertise erudition and says the lack of other evidence prevents a concrete answer (p. 98). The packet therefore retains the symptom wording and physician's reasoning but assigns no psychiatric diagnosis, efficacy, or independent outcome.

### The famous ninety-case corpus remains a transmission warning

The received *Shanghan jiushi lun* contains vivid scenes—such as a woman reported at night to speak incoherently, see ghosts, and become agitated while her husband consults the physician—but the uncertain compilation date and authorship make it unsuitable as direct Southern Song event evidence. It may be used later to explain how case stories were transmitted and attributed, with Chen's counter-reading attached.

## Setting 3 — Southern Song households and death investigation

### Anger, punishment, and servants in Yuan Cai

The lower juan of *Yuan shi shifan* gives elite household heads advice about servants. One passage says owners can become increasingly angry at servants' mistakes and beat them, sometimes accidentally causing death; it urges more instruction and less anger (`奴僕小人…棰楚加之，或失手而至於死亡者有矣…多其教誨，省其嗔怒可也`). The passage also stereotypes servants and women as foolish or harsh. It is neither emancipatory nor a servant's account.

The same juan advises that after whipping, an owner should return to ordinary speech because a servant may remain resentful and, if the owner's anger continues, may “take life lightly and injure themself” (`恐有輕生而自殘者`). This is the author's causal expectation, not a first-person motive statement.

The immediately following procedure addresses a maidservant or male servant found hanging (`婢僕有無故而自經者`). If the body is warm, it tells people to support the body, loosen the ligature gradually, and attempt resuscitation before untying; if cold or unrevived, it says not to move the body, to call neighbors, report to the authorities, and guard the corpse. Another instruction tells owners to send ill servants without kin to a neighbor for treatment, record their statement through neighborhood guarantors, and notify officials (`婢僕無親屬而病者…`). These are prescriptions aimed partly at owner protection. They do not establish frequency, access, voluntariness, effectiveness, or the affected person's feelings.

The middle juan's advice about anger (`親戚故舊，因言語而失歡者…`) says harsh expression and tone can provoke anger and urges self-watch when enraged. This is moral/relational cultivation, not a clinical emotion theory.

### Song Ci treats servant self-hanging as an investigative problem

In *Xiyuan jilu* juan 1, “疑難雜說上,” Song Ci warns that death scenes can be misleading and includes:

> 人力女使因被捶撻，在主家自害自縊之類。理有萬端，並為疑難。臨時審察，切勿輕易。

The packet gloss: male laborers or female servants may harm or hang themselves in an owner's household after beating; circumstances have many possibilities and must be examined carefully. This does not diagnose the deceased or preserve their motive in their own words. It does document that violence by household superiors and servant self-harm were within the manual's field of official suspicion.

The same juan repeatedly warns examiners not to rely on one or two statements, notes that scribes may write for illiterate witnesses, and instructs officials to compare neighbors' accounts. These rules show awareness of testimony problems while also displaying the state's coercive and hierarchical process.

### Bounded priority audit: legal-medicine reception is not psychiatric priority

Asen's full article documents how twentieth-century forensic professionals, historians, commemorations, and popular media constructed Song Ci as “father of world legal medicine.” He emphasizes that the modern image and world-first story took shape under modern national/scientific conditions, while Song himself had much less prominence among earlier forensic practitioners (pp. 185–188). A bounded audit searched the full Asen article for `psychiatr`, `forensic psychiatry`, and `psychiatrist` (zero hits), read the selected *Xiyuan jilu* juan 1 loci, and compared the current LOC object description. The LOC record repeats a legal-medicine priority formula—“world's first-ever systematically written book of forensic science”—but none of those opened materials establishes a “first forensic psychiatrist/psychiatry” proposition. This is a declared corpus result, not proof that no such claim exists anywhere; the broader priority question remains Unknown and quarantined.
