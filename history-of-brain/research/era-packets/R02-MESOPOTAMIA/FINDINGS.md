# R02 Mesopotamia — Findings

**Use:** source-backed outline foundations, not publishable copy
**Evidence rule:** every interpretive statement below points to a source and locator in
`SOURCE-LEDGER.md`. Ancient text wording is described rather than quoted at length.

## Executive findings

1. The most defensible story is not “Mesopotamia invented psychiatry.” It is that surviving
   learned texts organize troubling bodily, behavioral, cognitive, social, and divine signs
   in ways that do not reproduce a modern mind/body or natural/supernatural division
   (SRC-002000, pp. 365–370; SRC-002003, pp. 140–154, conclusion pp. 176–177,
   and p. 185 n. 96).
2. Diagnostic-prognostic texts are structured scholarly artifacts. They can preserve acute
   observations, but they are not clinical charts and do not automatically transmit patient
   speech (SRC-002003, pp. 141–144; SRC-002004, pp. 261–267).
3. The archive supports close discussion of recurring terms and sign clusters, provided the
   English gloss stays visibly provisional: `ašuštu`, `ḫīp libbi`, and
   `ṭēmu šanû/nakāru` cannot be equated with Major Depressive Disorder, panic disorder,
   or a modern “mind” changing (SRC-002000, pp. 122–124, 169–225).
4. Surviving texts can prescribe or represent material remedies, spoken incantations,
   offerings, burial practices, plant preparations, and specialist consultation within the
   same healing world. “Medicine versus magic” would misdescribe that textual repertoire;
   these passages do not establish archaeological frequency, delivery, acceptance, or
   efficacy (SRC-002003, pp. 140–154; SRC-002004, pp. 267–270; GAP-002012).
5. *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* supplies unusually rich literary first-person representations of
   isolation, bodily suffering, failed expertise, dreams, care, and bodily restoration in
   I 43–95, II 49–120, III 1–48, and III 49–106. III 46 mentions people taking care of
   the speaker; III 107 only fragmentarily begins to mention possessions/companions, while
   most of III 108–120 is lacunose. Its value is literary and historical, not biographical:
   it is not a
   transparent patient memoir (SRC-002001, accessed 2026-08-18; SRC-002002, unpaginated
   paragraphs beginning “Ludlul divides generically…” and “All these factors…”).
6. Surviving medical and literary corpora are weighted toward learned male and often elite
   environments. Non-elite, household, women's, disabled people's, caregivers', and
   unsuccessful-treatment perspectives are mostly indirect or absent (SRC-002007, pp. 3–8,
   85–86; SRC-002004, pp. 261–267).
7. Three recurring “first” narratives—oldest epilepsy account, oldest psychosis-of-epilepsy
   account, and first clinical foundations of neurology/psychiatry—form one authorial and
   citation lineage, not three independent discoveries. Modern Assyriological work rejects
   their unqualified diagnostic equivalences (SRC-002005, pp. 185–198; SRC-002009 abstract;
   SRC-002010 abstract; SRC-002000, pp. 365–370; SRC-002004, pp. 261–263).

## Evidence anchors

### EVD-002000 — Corpus composition and survival

- **Text/evidence:** diagnostic-prognostic, therapeutic, incantation/ritual, drug, lexical,
  and commentary texts survive; most medical manuscripts available for reconstruction are
  first-millennium BCE, even when compositions or traditions may be older.
- **Locator:** SRC-002003, pp. 140–144; SRC-002007, pp. 3–8.
- **Inference limit:** a later copy does not by itself date every line to the second
  millennium BCE. “Babylonians in Hammurabi's time wrote X” requires a dated witness or an
  explicit textual-history argument.

### EVD-002001 — Diagnostic entry structure

- **Text/evidence:** learned entries typically sequence observed conditions or signs,
  attribution/diagnosis, and prognosis; therapeutic entries sequence a condition and
  remedy. These formats are textual classifications, not preserved transcripts of an
  encounter.
- **Locator:** SRC-002003, pp. 142–144; SRC-002004, pp. 261–263.
- **Claim fit:** A for form; B for what actual encounters may have looked like.

### EVD-002002 — Practitioners and false professional binaries

- **Text/evidence:** `asû` and `āšipu` are two healer roles; translations “physician” and
  “exorcist” are often misleading. Healing texts include technical preparation, materia
  medica, incantation, divination, and ritual without a stable modern natural/supernatural
  professional border.
- **Locator:** SRC-002003, pp. 140–142; SRC-002007, pp. 3–4.
- **Translation caution:** retain Akkadian role names at first use; explain functions for the
  specific time/place rather than assigning one “medical” and the other “religious.”

### EVD-002003 — `ašuštu`

- **Text/evidence:** Al-Rashid uses “Depression” as a convenience for a dejected/depressed
  state and explicitly excludes Major Depressive Disorder or any modern disorder. The term
  occurs with patterns that include fatigue/stupor, fear, altered activity, breathing, and
  bodily signs.
- **Locator:** SRC-002000, p. 122 n. 84; p. 136 n. 101; pp. 137–168, 365–370.
- **Public rule:** transliterate and gloss as provisional; never title a section “Babylonian
  depression” without an immediate translation warning.

### EVD-002004 — `ḫīp libbi`

- **Text/evidence:** the compound joins a disputed pain/crushing element with `libbu`, the
  bodily insides/heart/belly implicated in feeling and thought. Proposed translations such
  as “heartbreak,” “anxiety state,” stomach pain, or pain of the insides do not settle its
  meaning.
- **Locator:** SRC-002000, pp. 169–219, especially 171–175 and 199–204; SRC-002004,
  pp. 268–269.
- **Contradiction:** SRC-002006 treats BAM 234 as anxiety/agitated depression; SRC-002000
  and SRC-002004 preserve semantic and diagnostic uncertainty.

### EVD-002005 — `ṭēmu šanû/nakāru`

- **Text/evidence:** `ṭēmu` can concern reason, understanding, intention, planning, or
  disposition and is not clearly a bodily organ. “The mind alters/changes” is an explanatory
  translation. Medical entries connect alteration to changing or excessive speech,
  forgetfulness, movement, fear, and bodily conditions.
- **Locator:** SRC-002000, pp. 220–267, especially 221–225; SRC-002004, pp. 269–270,
  BAM 202:1–3 and rev. 5′–11′.
- **Lexical anchor:** SRC-002000, pp. 221–225, cites Igituḫ I 200–202 for Sumerian
  `dimma-kúr-ra` and Akkadian `šinīt ṭēmi`.

### EVD-002006 — Change and somatization as recurring scholarly patterns

- **Text/evidence:** mental/affective disturbances are commonly rendered through visible
  change—speech, wandering, responsiveness, fear, activity—and through bodily interiors,
  pain, breath, sleep, appetite, vertigo, hearing, and movement.
- **Locator:** SRC-002000, pp. 268–364 and 365–370; SRC-002004, pp. 263–270.
- **Inference limit:** “somatization” is Al-Rashid's analytic vocabulary, not an Akkadian
  diagnosis.

### EVD-002007 — BAM 234's sign cluster and ritual response

- **Text/evidence:** BAM 234:1–12 links losses and frustrated affairs with `ḫīp libbi`,
  unanswered speech, fear, disturbed sleep/dreams, weakness, reduced food/drink, and
  forgetting words, followed by a ritual response involving figurines and incantation.
- **Locator:** SRC-002004, pp. 263–265; upstream edition SRC-002008, pp. 23–30, not
  independently opened.
- **Object control:** SRC-002012 identifies BAM 234 as CDLI P285320, a clay tablet
  excavated at Assur (modern Qalat Sherqat), excavation no. `Ass 13955go`. Its museum
  number, collection, period/date, and catalogue language remain blank/unknown. CDLI's
  current JSON marks J. Cale Johnson's Akkadian transliteration 2323449 as latest; it
  supplies no translation or diagnosis.
- **Genre/voice:** a third-person learned case formulation, not a recorded patient interview.
- **Dating limit:** the extant tablet is a copy; SRC-002006 admits the original date is
  unknown. Do not assign the case specifically to Hammurabi's reign.

### EVD-002008 — First-person ritual scripts are not memoirs

- **Text/evidence:** anti-witchcraft materials (LKA 157; LKA 154 and duplicates, text
  8.2:52–78) put extensive bodily, fearful, perceptual, dream, speech, and agency-loss
  language in a first-person recitation.
- **Locator:** SRC-002004, pp. 265–267.
- **Genre limit:** the “I” is a ritual speaker-position available for performance. It may
  resonate with experience, but neither authorship nor an individual's biography follows.

### EVD-002009 — Ghost-related disturbance, kin obligations, and mixed remedies

- **Text/evidence:** BAM 323:65–68 describes heat/cold, repeated confusion, no rest, and a
  distinctive cry, followed by beerwort, fox grape, and oil application. KAR 32:40–44
  frames bodily impairment as pursuit by a family or strange ghost. Other prescriptions
  restore relations through offerings, burial, libation, or ghost marriage.
- **Locator:** SRC-002004, pp. 267–268.
- **Interpretive value:** body treatment and social/ancestral repair are simultaneous, not
  rival explanatory stages in the represented prescriptions. These texts do not establish
  that the actions were delivered, how often, with what acceptance, or with what result.

### EVD-002010 — Body-based fear and thought alteration

- **Text/evidence:** Suālu IV locates worry/dejection and their remedies among ailments of
  the insides; recipes use plant ingredients, beer, and food. Diagnostic Handbook 13:43
  locates `ḫīp libbi` among signs involving `libbu`. BAM 202 links altered reason/speech to
  remedies and another entry links fear/excessive speech to `bennu` and a demon.
- **Locator:** SRC-002004, pp. 268–270.
- **Interpretive value:** these passages directly undermine a claim that affective or
  cognitive disturbance was always represented as treated only by ritual. They remain
  textual prescriptions, not archaeological proof of use or efficacy.

### EVD-002011 — Spoken remedies act through images, narratives, and direct address

- **Text/evidence:** CT 4, 8a:1–21 uses a Sumerian/Akkadian Marduk–Ea dialogue and
  compares a sick belly with blocked containers and waterways; other spells directly
  address fever or use agricultural/canal metaphors.
- **Locator:** SRC-002003, pp. 151–154.
- **Interpretive limit:** “psychotherapy” would be anachronistic. The supported description
  is that recited words and material acts constituted treatment and rendered disorder
  actionable within the text's shared images. Frequency and performed practice require the
  separate material/documentary evidence identified in GAP-002012.

### EVD-002012 — *Ludlul*: social abandonment

- **Text/evidence:** the literary speaker says protective powers depart, he is forced from
  his house, public signs become hostile, family bonds fail, a brother becomes like a
  stranger, a friend becomes hostile, and street/city attention turns against him.
- **Locator:** SRC-002001, I 43–53 and I 79–95.
- **Genre:** Standard Babylonian literary composition; not a documentary deposition.

### EVD-002013 — *Ludlul*: dense embodied suffering and failed expertise

- **Text/evidence:** the poem accumulates darkness, tears, internal pain, convulsion,
  sensory impairment, numbness, inability to answer, loss of consciousness, trouble eating,
  wasting, confinement, disturbed nights/days, and impaired elimination. An exorcist and
  diviner do not resolve the crisis; death preparations begin.
- **Locator:** SRC-002001, II 49–120.
- **Inference limit:** the sequence shows what this composition made narratively meaningful,
  not prevalence or a single disease entity.

### EVD-002014 — *Ludlul*: altered experience, dreams, attendants, and recovery

- **Text/evidence:** the speaker's `ṭēmu` strays amid groaning and disrupted waking/sleep;
  three dream encounters precede healing actions involving water, incantation, and rubbing;
  III 46 says a swathe is entrusted to the people taking care of the speaker; III 49–106
  represents bodily recovery. III 107 fragmentarily begins to mention possessions and
  companions.
- **Locator:** SRC-002001, III 1–48, III 46, III 49–106, and III 107, current edition
  accessed 2026-08-18.
- **Translation caution:** cite the exact eBL line and access date; do not inflate attendants
  into an institution or hospital. III 108–117 and 119–120 are substantially or wholly
  lacunose, and III 118 concerns overgrown nails; they do not establish broad social
  restoration.

### EVD-002015 — *Ludlul* is literary, didactic, and hymnic

- **Text/evidence:** Hurowitz argues that the composition is best understood as a hymn of
  praise with autobiographical confession and a didactic tendency. A named protagonist may
  lend credibility, but does not prove autobiography. Comparison with Job can illuminate
  reception while also erasing major differences.
- **Locator:** SRC-002002, unpaginated XHTML paragraphs beginning “Ludlul divides
  generically into two sections…,” “The fact that the protagonist in Ludlul is named…,”
  and “All these factors support the classification…”; textual line work in SRC-002001.
- **Claim fit:** A for Hurowitz's stated interpretation; C for a present consensus claim.
  Hurowitz reviews the 2010 four-tablet reconstruction, not the current eBL five-chapter
  arrangement. Oshima 2014 or a later treatment remains required before public genre or
  biographical synthesis.

### EVD-002016 — Learned networks and archive position

- **Text/evidence:** first-millennium cuneiform scholarship was organized through specialist
  roles, apprenticeships, court/temple/household networks, mobile scholars, collections,
  and private consultation. Royal scholarship could be exclusive. Surviving evidence is
  profoundly uneven, especially rich for the early seventh-century BCE Assyrian court.
- **Locator:** SRC-002007, pp. 3–8, 85–86, and ch. 4, pp. 98–146.
- **Inference limit:** the archive cannot be treated as a population sample.

### EVD-002017 — Sakikkû tablet 25/26 and the “oldest epilepsy” claim

- **Text/evidence:** SRC-002005 works from SU 51/92+ (Sultantepe, Neo-Assyrian context) and
  BM 47753 (unprovenanced, assigned likely Babylonian origin), identifies the text as
  Sakikkû tablet 25/26, and calls it the oldest written account of epilepsy then known.
- **Locator:** SRC-002005, pp. 185–186; retrospective identifications pp. 187–198.
- **Evaluation:** text/witness description is usable with later-edition checking; “epilepsy”
  is a modern retrospective grouping, and “oldest” is contingent on survival, discovery,
  dating, translation, and definition.

### EVD-002018 — “Oldest psychosis of epilepsy” propagation

- **Text/evidence:** SRC-002009's title and abstract call another Babylonian passage the
  oldest account of psychosis of epilepsy and map its signs to interictal or
  schizophrenia-like psychosis.
- **Locator:** SRC-002009, PubMed abstract only.
- **Evaluation:** reject for public factual use until full text and underlying tablet edition
  are read. It belongs to IG-002000, the same author lineage as SRC-002005/006/010.

### EVD-002019 — “First clinical foundations” propagation

- **Text/evidence:** SRC-002010's abstract says Babylonians were the first to describe the
  clinical foundations of neurology and psychiatry. It lists epilepsy, stroke, psychoses,
  OCD, phobias, psychopathic behavior, depression, and anxiety using modern categories.
- **Locator:** SRC-002010, PubMed abstract only.
- **Evaluation:** reject as a public priority claim. It is a synthesis of the same authors'
  earlier retrospective articles, not a separate primary evidence base.

### EVD-002020 — Origin of the “no understanding of brain function” assertion

- **Source:** SRC-002006.
- **Text/evidence:** Reynolds and Kinnier Wilson state in the Introduction and repeat in the
  Discussion that Babylonians lacked understanding or knowledge of brain and psychological
  function. The article makes this categorical assertion while reading BAM 234 and related
  texts through modern neurological and psychiatric categories.
- **Locator:** SRC-002006, p. 478, Introduction, and pp. 480–481, final Discussion paragraph; direct PMC
  full text replayed through Jina Reader on 2026-08-18.
- **Claim fit:** A for documenting that the authors made the assertion; D for establishing
  the historical proposition. The article is not a dedicated review of Mesopotamian anatomy,
  head trauma, stroke/facial palsy, or neurological localization.
- **Evaluation:** this evidence can identify the origin and scope of the modern assertion. It
  cannot establish a universal absence of ancient brain knowledge.

### EVD-002021 — Same-lineage repetition of the brain-function assertion

- **Source:** SRC-002010.
- **Text/evidence:** the PubMed abstract repeats the proposition that Babylonians had no
  knowledge of brain or psychological function while also presenting them as first to
  describe the clinical foundations of neurology and psychiatry.
- **Locator:** SRC-002010, PubMed abstract, PMID 25037816; replayed through Jina Reader on
  2026-08-18.
- **Claim fit:** A for the wording and publication-lineage fact; D for the historical
  proposition. Only the abstract was opened, and SRC-002010 shares `IG-002000` with
  SRC-002006.
- **Evaluation:** this is repetition within one author/citation lineage, not independent
  corroboration. The historical proposition remains held for a dedicated brain/body packet.

## Citation-chain audit: three “first/diagnosis” narratives

| Narrative | Traceable chain | Shared dependency | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| “The oldest written account of epilepsy” | Sakikkû 25/26 witnesses → SRC-002005 transcription/translation → repeated in SRC-002006 and SRC-002010 | Same authors and same diagnostic method (IG-002000) | **Do not state as fact.** At most: a 1990 article argued that a particular learned tablet was the oldest written epilepsy account then known. |
| “The oldest account of psychosis of epilepsy” | Unopened underlying tablet/edition → SRC-002009 abstract → cited by SRC-002006 and listed alongside prior work in SRC-002010 | Same authors; full evidence inaccessible | **Reject pending original edition.** Modern labels are built into the claim. |
| “Babylonians first described depression/anxiety or founded psychiatry” | Köcher publication of BAM 234 → SRC-002008 edition titled “anxiety state” → SRC-002006 “agitated depression” → SRC-002010 “first clinical foundations” | Same BAM 234 chain plus same author lineage | **Reject.** SRC-002000 and SRC-002004 show translation ambiguity, mixed bodily/social/divine framing, and no equivalent psychiatric category. |

## Contradictions and disputes

### DIS-002000 — Retrospective diagnosis

- **Position 1:** SRC-002005/006/009/010 map sign descriptions to epilepsy subtypes,
  psychosis, depression, anxiety, OCD, phobias, or psychopathic behavior.
- **Position 2:** SRC-002000, pp. 365–370; SRC-002003, pp. 140–144, conclusion
  pp. 176–177 and p. 185 n. 96; and
  SRC-002004, pp. 261–263 argue that this method obscures native classifications and often
  imposes a modern mind/body framework.
- **Resolution for outline:** describe signs, terms, causal relations, text genre, and modern
  dispute. Do not adjudicate an ancient DSM/ICD diagnosis.

### DIS-002001 — Meaning of `ḫīp libbi`

- **Position 1:** SRC-002008 labels BAM 234 an “anxiety state”; SRC-002006 advances
  “agitated depression” and possible panic attacks.
- **Position 2:** SRC-002000, pp. 171–219, documents lexical uncertainty in both elements;
  SRC-002004, pp. 268–269, embeds the expression in `libbu`-based bodily classification.
- **Resolution for outline:** retain `ḫīp libbi`; offer several cautious glosses and explain
  why none is a diagnosis.

### DIS-002002 — Genre and biography in *Ludlul*

- **Position 1:** a named protagonist and first-person retrospective can be read as
  autobiographical or historically grounded.
- **Position 2:** SRC-002002 argues for a hymn of praise with autobiographical confession
  and didactic purpose; literary construction prevents treating its “I” as transparent
  patient testimony.
- **Resolution for outline:** call it a literary first-person account or literary speaker,
  never a patient diary. Attribute the genre position to Hurowitz and note that his review
  predates the current five-chapter reconstruction. Do not claim current consensus or infer
  autobiography from the protagonist's historicity pending Oshima 2014/later review.

### DIS-002003 — “Magic” and “medicine”

- **Position 1:** older and popular histories divide `asû` plant treatment from `āšipu`
  supernatural treatment.
- **Position 2:** SRC-002003 and SRC-002007 show overlapping practices, shared texts, and
  unstable professional/genre boundaries.
- **Resolution for outline:** organize by observed practice and relationship—preparation,
  recitation, divination, offering, bodily application, consultation—not by a progress ladder.

## Proposed outline transitions

1. **From sign lists to a world of relations:** introduce the documentary format, then show
   that bodily interiors, conduct, speech, gods, kin, and fortune can occupy one case.
2. **Three words that resist us:** a compact translation lab around `ašuštu`, `ḫīp libbi`,
   and `ṭēmu`, with side-by-side editions and a visible “not a diagnosis” flag.
3. **What treatment could include:** move across beer, plants, oil, food, rubbing,
   incantation, figures, offerings, and specialist interpretation without a magic/medicine
   ranking.
4. **A sufferer speaks—or does a poem speak?:** use *Ludlul* to stage social abandonment,
   bodily crisis, dreams, attendants, and recovery, then disclose genre and manuscript
   history before inviting empathy.
5. **Who is missing from the tablets?:** close with the archive's learned/elite position and
   state directly that patient satisfaction, prevalence, everyday family care, and failed
   treatment usually remain unknown.
6. **Myth-busting bridge:** use the citation-chain audit to transition from “first psychiatry”
   headlines to a larger question: what changes when ancient people are allowed to have
   categories unlike ours?

## Neighboring handoffs

- Comparisons with Egyptian, Levantine, Greek, Iranian, Jewish, or later Islamic traditions
  require their own packet and evidence chain; none is evidence of direct borrowing unless a
  transmission route is documented.
- Later Greek “Sacred Disease” comparisons in SRC-002009 are outside this packet and should
  be sent to the Greek/Roman packet as a disputed continuity claim.
- The late-first-millennium survival of cuneiform scholarly networks after 500 BCE belongs
  in a later exchange/transmission packet (SRC-002007, chs. 5–7).
