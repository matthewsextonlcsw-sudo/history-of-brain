# R02-MESOPOTAMIA — Signs, Suffering, Healing, and Voice

**State:** Authorized; research only

**Owner:** One research session; this folder only

**Identifier range:** `SRC/EVD/CLM-002000–002999`

## Research question

How did surviving Mesopotamian texts and objects describe troubling experiences, signs,
bodies, divine and social relations, prognoses, and responses—and what can literary first
persons such as *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* tell us without being misrepresented as patient memoirs?

## Boundary

Southern and northern Mesopotamian textual worlds, approximately 3200–500 BCE, with exact
date, language, city, archive, genre, and edition recorded for each item. Earlier or later
material enters only when needed to establish textual transmission.

## In scope

- diagnostic-prognostic, therapeutic, ritual, lexical, legal, and literary evidence;
- named practitioner roles and household/institutional contexts;
- material and spoken remedies without a magic-versus-medicine ladder;
- Akkadian and Sumerian terminology with edition/translator cautions;
- scribal genre, literary voice, and the archive's social position;
- what the texts record about distress, behavior, sleep, fear, grief, misfortune, or altered
  experience; and
- serious scholarly disputes about interpretation and continuity.

## Out of scope

- all of West Asian antiquity;
- Levantine, Egyptian, Greek, or Iranian traditions except documented handoffs;
- equating an ancient term with a DSM/ICD diagnosis;
- treating a literary speaker as a transparent historical patient; and
- public prose.

## Required evidence targets

- reliable editions/translations or tablet records for each pivotal textual claim;
- specialist Assyriological scholarship with exact pages;
- multiple translations when a claim turns on one word;
- scholarship on genre and scribal context;
- evidence about households or non-elite experience where it actually survives; and
- a citation-chain audit of at least three popular “first psychiatry/diagnosis” claims.
