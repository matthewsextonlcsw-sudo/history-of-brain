# R02 Mesopotamia — Lived Experience and the Archive

**Purpose:** prevent technical and literary sources from being mistaken for unmediated
patient experience while preserving room for humane, specific attention to suffering. This
is an archive audit, not public narrative.

## Voice classification

| Archive voice | Example | What it can support | What it cannot support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learned third-person case | BAM 234:1–12 (SRC-002004, pp. 263–265; P285320 provenance in SRC-002012) | Signs a scholarly text grouped together; a ritual response; textual classification; Assur/excavation provenance for the physical witness | A verbatim consultation, the patient's diagnosis, exact date of the underlying case, museum identity, outcome, or satisfaction |
| Diagnostic-prognostic entry | SA.GIG / Sakikkû examples (SRC-002000, pp. 268–364; SRC-002003, pp. 142–144) | Conditional sign sequences, attributions, prognosis, scribal ordering | Frequency in the population, a case history, or a modern disease entity |
| Therapeutic prescription | Suālu IV; BAM 323; BAM 202 (SRC-002004, pp. 267–270) | What a text instructs a practitioner to prepare/do; body concepts and causal relations | That the remedy was used exactly as written, by whom, how often, or with what result |
| Ritual first person | LKA 154 and duplicates, text 8.2:52–78 (SRC-002004, pp. 265–267) | A performable vocabulary of bodily pain, terror, dreams, voice, thought, and impaired agency | Authorship, a single speaker's biography, or a transcript of spontaneous self-report |
| Literary first person | *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* (SRC-002001; SRC-002002) | Cultural possibilities for narrating social abandonment, bodily crisis, failed help, dream intervention, care, and restoration | A transparent patient memoir, clinical record, or typical life-course |
| Scholar/court correspondence and network evidence | Assyrian and Babylonian scholarly networks (SRC-002007, pp. 98–146) | Mobility, patronage, professional relations, access, exclusivity, consultation contexts | The experience of the many people outside literate elite networks |
| Modern retrospective clinician | SRC-002005/006/009/010 | History of modern interpretation; how “first diagnosis” stories were constructed | Native diagnostic identity or independent corroboration of an ancient category |

## Experience-bearing passages

### LEA-002000 — Social unmaking in *Ludlul*

The poem does not isolate suffering inside an individual. In I 43–53, protective divine and
household relations withdraw and the speaker is driven from his house. In I 79–95, family,
brother, friend, street, city, and reputation all become sources of estrangement or threat
(SRC-002001). This can anchor an empathetic discussion of illness and social belonging if
the subject remains “the poem's speaker,” not “a Babylonian patient.”

**Do:** name the network of losses and cite exact lines.
**Do not:** write that ancient Mesopotamians generally expelled mentally ill people from
their homes; this poem cannot support that generalization.

### LEA-002001 — A body in crisis in *Ludlul*

II 49–107 accumulates darkness, tears, internal pain, convulsion, sensory impairment,
numbness, inability to answer, loss of consciousness, eating difficulty, wasting,
confinement, sleepless days/nights, and elimination problems. II 108–120 represents failed
specialist help, absence of the speaker's gods, death preparation, and the reactions of kin
and enemies (SRC-002001). The density of this description warrants empathy without a
retroactive diagnosis.

**Do:** describe the sequence as a crafted representation of embodied and social crisis.
**Do not:** choose one modern disorder to “explain” all signs or claim that the poem proves
how a real named person died/recovered.

### LEA-002002 — Dreams, hands-on acts, attendants, and return

In III 1–48 the speaker's reason/disposition strays amid groaning and troubled sleep/waking;
dream figures precede water, recitation, and body-rubbing; line III 46 mentions attendants
or, in Benjamin R. Foster's current eBL translation, “the people taking care of me”
(`muttabbilū`). III 49–106 narrates bodily restoration. III 107 fragmentarily begins to
mention possessions and companions; III 108–117 and 119–120 are substantially or wholly
lacunose, while III 118 concerns overgrown nails (SRC-002001, accessed 2026-08-18). The
intact passage shows care as narrative action, but one phrase for care people does not
establish a hospital, ward, or professional nursing role. The damaged ending cannot carry a
broad social/material-restoration claim.

**Do:** name actions and participants, with line numbers and translation access date.
**Do not:** turn the healing scene into an institutional-care claim or fill III's lacunae
from narrative expectation.

### LEA-002003 — Scripted loss of agency

The first-person anti-witchcraft recitation published as LKA 154 and duplicates, text
8.2:52–78, represents pervasive bodily pain, paralysis/weakness, fear, terrible dreams,
hearing disturbance, self-talk, inability to plan, inability to remember one's words, and a
sense that one's own inner faculties have become strange (SRC-002004, pp. 265–267). The
ritual text offers a language in which suffering can be voiced. It does not reveal whether
any one historical individual used every line.

**Empathy rule:** a scripted voice is not fake, but it is mediated. Treat the script as
evidence of culturally available expression and ritual performance, not as a recovered
interview.

### LEA-002004 — Misfortune, household position, and BAM 234

BAM 234 links losses in household/economic affairs, frustrated intentions, fear, altered
sleep/dreams, bodily weakness, reduced intake, and forgetfulness with `ḫīp libbi` and a
ritual remedy (SRC-002004, pp. 263–265). SRC-002006, p. 480, infers a wealthy male head of
household and compares him to a feudal “lord of the manor.” That comparison is modern,
culture-specific, and not needed. The safer inference is that the text's subject is positioned
through property, household personnel, work, and social obligations.

CDLI now identifies the physical witness as P285320, a clay tablet excavated at Assur
(modern Qalat Sherqat), excavation no. `Ass 13955go` (SRC-002012). Its museum number,
collection, period/date, and catalogue language fields remain unknown/blank. The catalogue
and transliteration improve object provenance; they do not convert the learned third-person
case into patient testimony or establish a diagnosis, outcome, or original date.

**Do:** state the represented losses and textual gender where the grammar supports it.
**Do not:** universalize elite household distress or reproduce the feudal analogy.

### LEA-002005 — Kinship with the dead

BAM 323:65–68 represents temperature changes, repeated confusion, lack of rest, and crying,
followed by plant/oil treatment. KAR 32:40–44 frames pain and impaired movement as pursuit
by a family or strange ghost. Related rites address failures of burial, offerings, libation,
or other duties to the dead (SRC-002004, pp. 267–268). The relevant lived world includes
obligation across generations; bodily intervention and repaired kin relations are not
separable “medical” and “superstitious” phases.

**Do:** explain the relational logic and concrete acts.
**Do not:** infer that every confused or sleepless person received this explanation.

## Archive position

### Who produced and preserved the core evidence?

- Cuneiform healing knowledge belonged to scribal learning and named professional fields;
  `asû`, `āšipu`, diviners, lamenters, apprentices, senior scholars, court and temple
  relationships, households, and private clients varied across time and city
  (SRC-002007, pp. 3–8).
- Royal scholarly networks could be deliberately exclusive. The unusually dense
  early-seventh-century BCE Assyrian record must not be projected backward or treated as
  representative of Mesopotamia (SRC-002007, pp. 6–8, 85–86).
- Technical compilations can preserve observation, imagination, school transmission, and
  reference practice at once. Al-Rashid's phrase that the diagnostic “madman” is an
  amalgam of scribal observation and imagination is a necessary warning
  (SRC-002000, p. 365).
- Copies came from many cities and contexts, and some objects lack archaeological
  provenance. “Babylonian” may name language/tradition rather than a verified Babylon
  findspot (SRC-002001 witness map; SRC-002005, pp. 185–186).

### Whose experience is easiest to see?

- male grammatical subjects in learned case formulations;
- a high-status male literary sufferer whose loss and restoration matter to household,
  office, gods, and city;
- rulers and court-connected scholars preserved in dense Neo-Assyrian correspondence;
- clients whose distress fit the entry or ritual templates that scribes copied; and
- successful restoration narratives more readily than quiet persistence, refusal, or
  failure.

### Whose experience is hard or impossible to recover here?

| Missing or weakly represented voice | Packet status | What would be needed |
|---|---|---|
| Non-elite clients and family caregivers | Mostly absent/indirect | Dated letters, household archives, legal/economic documents, or excavated domestic healing contexts tied to exact persons |
| Women's experiences | Some female-subject medical entries exist in the broader corpus, but not researched enough in this packet | Dedicated edition-based review of women's therapeutic/diagnostic texts, reproductive corpus boundaries, and female practitioners/caregivers |
| Children, older adults, disabled people | Not securely differentiated in the core sources used | Age-marked cases or letters and scholarship on ancient age/disability terminology |
| Enslaved/dependent people | Household personnel appear as assets/losses more readily than speakers | Administrative/legal records plus social-history analysis; no inference from silence |
| People who rejected or could not access specialists | Absent | Letters, complaints, legal disputes, or material evidence outside scholarly collections |
| Failed, harmful, coercive, or unwanted treatment | Outcome evidence absent | Case correspondence or outcome records; prescriptions alone cannot answer |
| Patient satisfaction and consent | Unknown | Direct contemporaneous testimony with secure genre/provenance; currently unavailable |
| Community prevalence | Unknown | A denominator and sampling frame that the cuneiform archive does not provide |

## Empathy protocol for later public drafting

1. **Locate the speaker before interpreting the feeling.** Say whether the voice is
   literary, ritual, diagnostic, prescriptive, or documentary.
2. **Keep the body in the story.** Do not extract fear, altered thought, or grief from pain,
   appetite, sleep, movement, breath, work, and kin relations when the source binds them.
3. **Use ancient terms without exoticizing them.** Transliteration plus a short semantic
   range is more honest than either untranslated spectacle or a false DSM label.
4. **Describe care concretely.** Name water, oil, beer, plant matter, rubbing, words,
   figurines, offerings, attendants, divination, or consultation where the source does.
   Say that a surviving text prescribes or represents an action unless separate material or
   documentary evidence establishes performance.
5. **Do not ridicule relational causation.** Gods, ghosts, kin duties, reputation, and
   household fortune were part of the represented world of health; “they blamed demons” is
   a flattening summary.
6. **Make uncertainty visible but readable.** One well-placed sentence about genre or
   translation protects the subject better than repeated hedging.
7. **Never manufacture a patient's reaction.** We do not know whether a person was
   comforted, terrified, coerced, relieved, angry, or “not thrilled” unless a source actually
   says so.
8. **Let absence have weight.** A vivid elite literary poem does not cancel the silence of
   non-elite people, women, caregivers, and unsuccessful clients.

## Safe and unsafe outline prompts

| Safe research prompt | Unsafe transformation |
|---|---|
| “How does *Ludlul* connect bodily crisis to the collapse and repair of social relations?” | “What disorder did Šubši-mešrê-Šakkan have?” |
| “What does BAM 234 group with `ḫīp libbi`, and how do translations differ?” | “How did Babylonians diagnose major depression?” |
| “Which material and spoken actions occur in this specific prescription?” | “Was Mesopotamian treatment medical or magical?” |
| “Who produced this tablet, where was its witness found, and for whom might it have been useful?” | “What did the average Babylonian patient experience?” |
| “What can a ritual first person make speakable?” | “Here is a patient's own account.” |

## Archive verdict

The packet can responsibly recover **representations of distress and care**, **learned
classification**, **literary and ritual speaker positions**, and **some social contexts of
expert practice**. It cannot yet recover a representative history of patients. Empathy must
therefore operate through specificity and disclosure, not invented interiority.
