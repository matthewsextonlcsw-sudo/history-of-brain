## Elisabeth: valuable voice without “patient” capture

The selected letters are the closest this packet comes to lived experience. They support four narrow propositions:

1. Elisabeth describes bodily illness and her own understanding of links among affliction, body, circumstance, activity, and advice (`EVD-028016`).
2. She calls the correspondence an antidote to melancholy but then states why responsibilities and events prevent easy diversion (`EVD-028017`).
3. She argues philosophically about mind–body interaction and passions (`EVD-028015`, `EVD-028018`).
4. Her words reach us through a copy/edition chain; Bos reports that she had refused publication and that the copy's provenance is unknown (`EVD-028019`).

Editorial rules:

- Use “Elisabeth wrote,” “described,” “asked,” or “argued.”
- If “lived experience” is used, immediately qualify it as copied, private, elite correspondence.
- Do not call her Descartes's patient, diagnose her, turn “antidote” into a cure outcome, or use her illness chiefly as a setup for Descartes.
- The language of “weaknesses of my sex” belongs to her period and rhetorical situation. It must not be flattened into either biological truth or a generic statement about early modern women.
- Political exile, dynastic responsibility, access to physicians, literacy, servants/messengers, and philosophical education mark status. Her archive cannot stand for poor, institutionalized, colonized, rural, or nonliterate people.

## Burton: self-writing is not patient testimony

The Democritus Junior preface is an authored performance. It is still evidence of how Burton chose to present writing, busyness, learned compilation, and his relation to melancholy. It is not transparent medical documentation.

- `EVD-028007` supports the printed self-presentation.
- `EVD-028008` is a dependent facsimile check, not corroboration.
- `EVD-028014` records the missing recipient/outcome fields in a defined mini-corpus.

Editorial rules:

- Keep persona, satire, compilation, and publication in the sentence.
- “Writing helped him” is too strong unless explicitly framed as what his persona claimed writing was for.
- Never state that he had depression, diagnosed himself, invented bibliotherapy, or cured himself.
- Remedy passages must be introduced as printed prescriptions. Where gentleness appears beside coercive permission, preserve both.

## Vesalius: the anatomical subject is not a recovered person

The selected pages make bodies visible as objects of learned observation while the people whose bodies were opened remain unidentified in the mini-corpus. `EVD-028006` is intentionally narrow.

Editorial rules:

- Do not invent fear, willingness, criminal status, poverty, execution status, or postmortem wishes.
- A visually striking dissection page is not automatically ethical to reuse. Before public use, identify the depicted procedure/body, establish image rights, write non-spectacular alt text, and review the dignity purpose.
- State “the opened pages do not tell us” instead of “cadavers had no voice” or “all bodies were stolen.”

## Gender, class, status, and archive-production audit

- **Gender:** One elite woman's letters survive because she corresponded with a famous philosopher and because a copy entered a castle library. Survival is not equal to representativeness.
- **Class/status:** Vesalius, Burton, Descartes, and Elisabeth all occupy exceptionally privileged literate networks. Printers, copyists, messengers, servants, patients, dissections' human subjects, and ordinary readers appear weakly or not at all.
- **Canon formation:** Descartes's letters were published and philosophically canonized earlier than Elisabeth's side; her copied letters were first published in 1879. A narrative centered only on Descartes would reproduce the archive's asymmetry.
- **Language:** Latin controlled anatomical expertise and reach; English expanded Burton's learned compilation to vernacular print readers; French shaped an elite philosophical correspondence and treatise. No translation layer is neutral.
- **Religion:** Burton's clerical, moral, medical, literary, and satirical registers overlap. Extracting only “medical” sentences falsifies the work.
- **Colonial/global scope:** This packet does not document colonial circulation or non-European reception. Its print networks must not be relabeled global.

## Trauma and safety controls

- No self-harm claim is retained. Burton death/suicide legends were not opened upstream and must not appear.
- Do not use coercive Burton language as entertainment or a “crazy treatments” hook.
- Do not use Elisabeth's illness language to expose private detail beyond what the historical argument requires.
- Do not present Descartes's strong/weak-soul language as a timeless moral measure of people with distress.
- No current-care takeaway is authorized from historical prescriptions. A later “what we know now” section requires separate current clinical evidence.

## Missing voices to carry into the outline

1. People whose bodies underwrote anatomy.
2. Named recipients of Burton-like advice and those who resisted it.
3. Poor, institutionalized, rural, colonized, non-Christian, and nonliterate people across the same centuries.
4. Printers, illustrators, copyists, household workers, and messengers who materially sustained the texts.
5. Family members and local healers outside elite correspondence.
6. Readers who found, rejected, misunderstood, or were harmed by prescriptions.

These are publication-visible gaps, not invitations to fill silence with representative anecdotes from another place or century.
