## What this packet can safely hand to the outline

1. **Print made revision visible.** The 1543/1555 Vesalius witnesses support a tightly bounded transition from received anatomy to page-specific criticism and continuing revision—not a “brain discovered” origin story.
2. **A category could be an argument, not a diagnosis.** Burton's definition pages make melancholy plural, compiled, and contested.
3. **Coping advice and control could coexist.** Burton places trusted conversation and gentle treatment beside surveillance, diversion, manipulation, and “fair or foul means.” Keep prescription separate from practice and outcome.
4. **The correspondent changes the theory.** Elisabeth's objections and requests can introduce mind–body mechanism and passions without reducing her to muse or patient.
5. **A famous slogan can be corrected by reading one article earlier.** *Passions* art.30 joins soul to the whole body before arts.31–34 assign the gland a principal interaction role.
6. **Edition layers matter.** A 1664 *L’Homme* sentence is a posthumous witness to a work that the BnF authority dates 1633, not proof of a finalized 1633 text, a lifetime publication, or the whole of Descartes's anthropology.

## Proposed outline transitions

- **From manuscript authority to reproducible disagreement:** Vesalius's page can be compared across editions, allowing readers to see correction rather than accept a hero myth.
- **From anatomy to the anatomy of a subject:** Burton borrows the cutting/opening metaphor but applies it to a sprawling world of medicine, theology, politics, satire, and self-writing.
- **From prescription to person:** after Burton's printed remedies, pivot to what his pages do not preserve—recipient voices—then to Elisabeth's rare but socially exceptional first-person correspondence.
- **From letter to treatise:** show Elisabeth asking the causal and definitional questions before opening Descartes's later articles; do not claim sole causation.
- **From mechanism to caution:** machine and gland models become historically interesting precisely when they are not converted into modern neuroscience, psychotherapy, or “humans are robots” slogans.
