# R16-PRINT-ANATOMY-MELANCHOLY-MECHANISM — Books, Bodies, Passions, and Self-Writing

**State:** Authorized; research only

**Identifier range:** `028000–028999`

## Research question

What can three named print, correspondence, and anatomical corpora from 1543–1649 establish
about changing representations of brain, body, soul, melancholy, passions, mechanism, and
self-writing without turning early-modern texts into modern neuroscience or psychiatry?

## Frozen settings

1. Padua–Basel anatomical print: Vesalius, *De humani corporis fabrica*, Book VII, 1543 and
   1555 editions, plus only necessary print/image front matter.
2. Oxford melancholy in print: Burton, *The Anatomy of Melancholy*, bounded passages across the
   1621–1651 edition chain.
3. Dutch Republic–Palatinate exchange: Descartes–Elisabeth letters, selected *Passions de
   l'âme* articles through 1649, and the pre-1637 *L'Homme* text only through its controlled
   posthumous 1664 witness.

## Required evidence and tests

- compare editions, languages, images, printer/editor/transmission layers, and exact passages;
- test Vesalius-discovered-the-brain, founded-neuroscience, simply-overthrew-Galen, and modern
  localization stories;
- separate Burton's pseudonym, satire, compilation, revision, authorial self-presentation,
  historical melancholy, selected remedies, delivery, effect, and modern depression;
- test first-psychiatry-textbook, diagnosed-patient, and writing-cured-him reception chains;
- treat Elisabeth as an elite correspondent and co-thinker, not a patient or muse; preserve
  her challenge to mind–body interaction and the later-copy/edition history of her letters;
- test Descartes-invented-dualism, soul-simply-lives-in-pineal-gland, and person-is-only-machine
  claims against exact texts and specialist disputes; and
- audit cadaver identities/consent, ordinary readers, remedy recipients, non-elite households,
  and absent direct patient experience.

## Exclusions

Exclude the medieval R15 corpora, witchcraft surveys, madhouses, poor law, asylum practice,
institutional patient histories, Atlantic/colonial contact, Willis, Locke, Cheyne, and
post-1650 reception except named handoffs. Forman–Napier consultations belong to the separate
casebook packet. No Europe-wide belief, prevalence, diagnosis, treatment-effect, or “first.”

## Stop

Stop at the three settings and the substantive 1543–1649 boundary. Use Latin, early modern
English, and French expertise; keep restricted modern translations link-only. Create five
standard outputs and rights-safe ignored cache; no public prose.
