# R21 Dullunda — Lived Experience and Archive Position

**Bottom line:** This packet can describe a colonial legal and administrative apparatus. It cannot recover the lived experience that apparatus largely excluded from the frozen record.

## Archive position

The frozen archive was produced to legislate, classify, inspect, count, manage, finance and review colonial institutions. Its tables and prose make administrative priorities highly visible: custody, certification, expense, mortality, labor, production, crowding, discharge categories and staff performance. That visibility is not the same as a balanced record of Dullunda.

The complete-item audit identified no direct first-person quotation by a confined person, family/friend or subordinate worker in the 1867, 1869 or 1872 parent items, and no affected-person, family/friend or subordinate-worker statement in the defined Dullunda sections. The complete 1867 parent item does contain two out-of-scope Cuttack passages in which administrators report confined people's speech. Those traces are not direct quotations, do not preserve original language or translation, and cannot be imported into a Dullunda lived-experience claim.

These are bounded archive findings, not claims that people did not speak, write, resist, negotiate, care or remember. English administrative survival may have erased, translated, summarized, selected, refused or never recorded vernacular speech.

## Law versus implementation

Act XXXVI of 1858 is evidence of legal design. The packet must preserve its distinct pathways:

- monthly visitor inspection and examination;
- police and magistrate custody routes;
- medical certificates and formal orders;
- Civil Court applications;
- guardians, friends and relatives as applicants or custodians;
- estates, government, friends, relatives and legally bound maintainers as expense actors; and
- schedule fields that classified the person, purported cause, danger and family history.

None proves that Dullunda followed the procedure, that a certificate was accurate, that an examination gave meaningful voice, or that a family undertaking produced safe care. Implementation requires Dullunda practice records tied to each provision and, where possible, evidence outside the reporting authority.

## Labor and institutional economy

The reports directly document gardens, workshops, weaving, industrial production, outside work, ratios counted as employed, sales, assigned value, raw-material costs, staff roles, maintenance receipts and official profit/gain language. The archive therefore permits a careful history of how administrators linked work and institutional finance.

It does not answer the questions most important to lived experience:

- Who chose or refused a task?
- What consequences followed refusal?
- Who controlled hours, pace and movement?
- Did confined workers receive money or other benefits?
- What injuries or dangers occurred?
- Did anyone understand work as relief, skill, obligation, punishment, survival, exploitation, therapy, or several of these at once?

The evidence permits neither a benevolent-therapy story nor an unqualified coercion story. Administrative silence is not consent, and institutional confinement alone does not let this packet specify every labor relation without further legal and affected-person evidence.

## Friends, relatives and family economies

The law and reports place friends and relatives inside custody and maintenance systems. In 1872, an administrator guessed that crowding or the prospect of maintenance recovery might explain some custody applications. Those are official hypotheses. The packet does not possess the families' reasons, resources, negotiations, relationships, or accounts of care after transfer.

Empathetic treatment means making the institutional pressure visible without converting relatives into villains or rescuers. It also means not using “transferred to friends” as a happy ending. Transfer is an administrative destination category; outcome and experience remain unknown.

## Gendered conditions

The 1872 Dullunda report describes women's accommodation and work space as smaller, hotter and poorly ventilated. That condition can be documented because the official source states it. The report does not preserve a woman's account of living or working there.

Officials also debated why women formed a smaller share of recorded admissions. One resolution floated relatives' reluctance or lower occurrence; a later report called the figures doubtful approximations. Neither explanation is established. Admission data cannot become population prevalence.

## Historical categories and language

The archive uses colonial medical, legal, racialized and disability language. In research records, exact historical labels may be needed to identify a field or decision. In future public work they require quotation marks, attribution, explanation and the minimum repetition necessary. They cannot be mapped silently onto present diagnoses or identities.

The consulted corpus is English. Bengali-language and other vernacular newspapers, petitions, letters, legal records and community histories were not verified here. That language gap blocks public voice and motive claims. Translation review is required even where the official English text implies that information passed through vernacular speakers.

## Privacy, dignity and harm

Public-domain status does not require publication of names or intimate details. The complete reports contain identifiable release/capacity judgments, diagnostic labels, legal classifications, mortality entries and harm traces. This packet deliberately omits names.

Default public rules remain:

- no searchable roster or person-level dataset;
- no name in a release, diagnosis, “criminal,” mortality or harm record without a documented public-interest and dignity review;
- no sensational description of death, restraint or bodily injury;
- no facsimile merely because the scan is legally reusable;
- no SEO/GEO hook built from demeaning source language; and
- no invented first-person scene, composite person, quotation or emotion.

## Empathy rule for future writing

Future prose should keep people grammatically present without pretending to speak for them. Prefer: “The table counted 211 people as employed, but it did not record whether they agreed to the work or received its proceeds.” Avoid: “Patients happily worked,” “inmates were forced into profitable therapy,” or a cinematic scene built from administrative silence.

Empathy here is precision about power, missing testimony and the cost of not knowing. It is not emotional decoration.

## Publication gate

This file authorizes no public prose. Publication remains blocked pending independent evidence closure plus legal-historical, Bengali/vernacular, disability/survivor, privacy/dignity, labor-history, harm, rights, and human editorial review.
