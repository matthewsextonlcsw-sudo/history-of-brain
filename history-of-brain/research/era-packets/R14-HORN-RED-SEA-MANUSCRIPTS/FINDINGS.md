# R14 Horn / Red Sea Manuscripts — Findings

**Purpose:** internal outline foundations, not public article prose
**Research boundary:** four named manuscript/corpus settings, approximately 1441–1796
**Evidence authority:** `SOURCE-LEDGER.md` and `CLAIM-MAP.md`

## Executive finding

This packet can support an outline transition about how body, soul, fear, wrongdoing,
confession, illness, protection, and communal memory were organized through manuscripts.
It cannot support a unified “Horn of Africa mental-health system.” The surviving records
are disproportionately royal, clerical, hagiographic, devotional, and catalog-based. They
mostly show what texts prescribed or represented, how they moved, and who controlled
their transmission—not what an ill, grieving, frightened, punished, or penitent person
experienced.

The most important correction is categorical: *Maṣḥafa faws manfasāwi* is a
penitential/confessional manual that uses medical metaphor. Its title does not make it a
clinical medical manual, psychotherapy text, or evidence of treatment outcomes.

## Setting 1 — Ethiopian Orthodox anointing texts

### Retained foundation

- `CLM-025201`: Anaïs Wion reports colophon variants locating *Maṣḥafa bāḥry* in
  Zarʾa Yāʿeqob's seventh/eighth regnal year. The safe range is 1441–1443, not a single
  1441 date, and the attribution is royal/colophonic rather than demonstrated autograph.
  This remains **Plausible**, not independently corroborated, until the primary colophons
  or critical edition are replayed; IES 00782 is later context only.
- `CLM-025202`: the text prescribes confession, prayer over oil, anointing, and
  absolution while linking body and soul. This is evidence of a ritual script, not proof of
  implementation, consent, frequency, or efficacy.
- `CLM-025203`: Wion reads this source complex as part of a royal-clerical campaign to
  displace practices labeled sorcery and discipline religious difference. Penalty language
  introduces power and coercion into the history, but no affected person's testimony or
  enforcement count was recovered.
- `CLM-025205`: Wion reports a February 1561 Arabic-to-Gəʿəz *Maṣḥafa qandil*
  colophon that also names Gälawdewos, dead since 1559. The contradiction is part of the
  evidence, not a nuisance to remove. `CLM-025219` separately records Wion's date-error
  and delayed-news explanations as unresolved alternatives within one source, not two
  independent scholarly camps.
- `CLM-025206`: the specialist CAe record distinguishes two Gəʿəz recensions translated
  from one Arabic Vorlage. Translation, recension, copying, and performance are separate
  events.
- `CLM-025220`: Wion's separate methodological finding is that manuscript-copying
  frequency does not establish ritual frequency or replacement. EMDA 00028's later
  co-transmission does not close that practice gap.

### Manuscript anchors

- IES 00782: uncertain eighteenth-century Gəʿəz composite, *Maṣḥafa bāḥry* at
  fols. 24v–28v, incomplete at the end; current IES custody. This is precise later
  transmission evidence (`CLM-025204`).
- EMDA 00028: 1750–1850 uncertain, not 1750–1800; lists both anointing books and
  protective texts but supplies no item folios. It establishes co-transmission in one later
  codex, not that rites were performed together or before 1800 (`CLM-025207`).

### Outline transition

> From a prescribed rite to an actual bedside is an evidentiary crossing, not a sentence.
> The manuscripts reveal clerical scripts and political authority; they usually do not reveal
> consent, comfort, fear, refusal, or outcome.

## Setting 2 — *Gädlä Wälättä P̣eṭros*

### Retained foundation

- The Gəʿəz *gädl* was composed in 1672–1673, roughly thirty years after Wälättä
  P̣eṭros's death, by Gälawdewos using community reports and literary composition
  (`CLM-025209`). It is not Wälättä P̣eṭros's autobiography.
- The 2015 edition separates introduction, manuscript history, translation method,
  Life-Struggles, miracles, additions, and later community history. Future writers must cite
  the exact layer and locator rather than treating the volume as one transparent account
  (`CLM-025210`).
- The source is unusually valuable for a woman-centered Ethiopian Orthodox community,
  but it remains a sacred hagiobiography: selected community memory, reported dialogue,
  literary shaping, miracle claims, and later transmission.

### Live dispute

- Yirga Gelaw Woldeyes argues that the 2015 translation and interpretations contain
  philological, cultural, sexual, medical, racial, and ethical distortions and marginalize
  affected-community authority.
- Michael Kleiner responds locus by locus to selected sexualizing-translation allegations,
  defends those choices, acknowledges two nonsexual criticisms, and explicitly does not
  answer every ethical/historical issue.
- `CLM-025211` and `DIS-025402` retain both positions without treating either as neutral
  settlement. All contested sexuality, disease, bodily, and medical passage claims remain
  out of public prose pending independent Gəʿəz and community review.

### Outline transition

> Community memory can move us closer to lived worlds than a ritual rubric can, but a
> sacred biography composed after a person's death still has narrators, genres, purposes,
> and disputes between the subject and the reader.

## Setting 3 — Arabic-to-Gəʿəz *Maṣḥafa faws manfasāwi*

### Retained foundation

- Kleiner distinguishes two works sharing this Gəʿəz title. The Michaelian work is a manual
  for clerical confessors: theological/pastoral instruction plus penitential canons
  (`CLM-025212`).
- Its medical vocabulary is a structured metaphor: sin/illness, confession/request for
  therapy and anamnesis, penance/medicine, forgiveness/healing, confessor/physician, and
  penitent/patient. The safe historical claim is about metaphor and pastoral ethics—not
  modern psychotherapy or clinical care.
- Kleiner reconstructs the Arabic work in Coptic Lower Egypt about 1250–1280. He infers
  a Gəʿəz translation by 1620 and considers a sixteenth-century date more likely
  (`CLM-025213`). Neither is a direct composition/translation colophon.
- EMML 2845 is a later 1750–1800 uncertain Gəʿəz witness, with the work at
  fols. 3r–82r (`CLM-025214`). Its catalog authority's “active 1243–1247” label dates
  other securely dated works/author activity, not this work's composition.

### Exchange note

This is a defensible Arabic–Gəʿəz textual exchange chain. It does not prove a smooth route,
named translator, continuous pastoral use, or equivalence between Coptic, Ethiopian, and
modern clinical categories. Upstream Coptic-Arabic doctrinal history belongs in the adjacent
packet; R14 owns only the reception/transmission boundary.

### Outline transition

> A title can travel farther than its genre. “Spiritual medicine” sounds clinical in modern
> English; the full textual study instead leads to confession, penance, clerical judgment,
> and a medical metaphor for moral-spiritual repair.

## Setting 4 — IES00272 conditional Harar lane

### Retained foundation

- Cataloger `MP` records three Arabic works. Part 779 reports *al-Nūr al-asnā* at
  fols. 13r–112r, an explicit/colophon at 111r, completion on Sunday, 15 Shawwāl
  1210 AH, and a normalized date of 23 April 1796 (`CLM-025215`). These are catalog
  reports under conflict: the root record marks 111r–113r blank, while 23 April 1796
  was Saturday rather than Sunday. The physical folio and exact civil-date resolution
  remain unknown pending image, cataloger, Arabic, and calendar review.
- The same part record transcribes Muḥammad b. Idrīs b. Yūsuf as scribe; a 13r ownership
  note names Muḥammad Yūnus. Neither statement establishes reading, use, or identity.
- The manuscript has former shelfmark Harar 36 and is now in the IES collection, but the
  opened record does not supply a copying place or acquisition history.

### Required negative finding

`CLM-025216` blocks four attractive but unsupported moves:

- former Harar shelfmark → copied in Harar;
- Arabic manuscript → Harari/Ajami manuscript;
- catalog “magic” or devotional category → healing/protective implementation;
- named owner/scribe → reader, practitioner, patient, or community representative.

The setting remains metadata-only until manuscript images, a relevant Arabic passage,
acquisition history, and Harari/Muslim community protocol are available.

## Cross-packet findings

### Archives and power

The records concentrate authority in rulers, clergy, saints' communities, scribes,
catalogers, translators, and modern scholars. A named owner appears once; direct care-
recipient testimony and outcomes do not (`CLM-025217`). This is a feature of the defined
archive, not evidence that ordinary people lacked beliefs, coping practices, agency, or
resistance.

### Translation controls

- Gəʿəz *bāḥry* is rendered by Wion as substance/nature; HMML's editorial uses pearl.
  Public naming needs specialist review rather than silently choosing the friendlier English.
- “Spiritual medicine” is a title/metaphor layer, not a clinical category.
- Gälawdewos-era translation, 1561 colophon, and the king's 1559 death must remain in the
  same chronology note.
- Arabic, Gəʿəz, English, French, and German interpretive layers must be named at every
  sensitive claim.

### Priority and continuity audit

Within the defined R14 search, no selected setting supplies criteria, comparable predecessor
research, clinical encounter, outcome, or continuous practice chain for “first psychologist,”
“first psychotherapist,” “psychiatric treatment,” “timeless holistic care,” “universal
tolerance,” or an “unbroken tradition.” `CLM-025218` records this bounded packet audit;
it is not a global proof of absence and quarantines all such formulations.

## Proposed master-outline placements

1. **Royal ritual, care, and control (1441–1468):** *Maṣḥafa bāḥry* as a body-soul
   anointing prescription embedded in ecclesiastical and political discipline.
2. **Alexandrian–Ethiopian translation and a contradictory colophon (sixteenth century):**
   *Maṣḥafa qandil* as a translation/recension case, not a diffusion-success story.
3. **A woman's community remembered through hagiobiography (1672–1673):** use genre
   and mediation before selecting any episode.
4. **When medicine is a metaphor (Arabic source about 1250–1280; Gəʿəz translation by
   1620 inferred):** penitential judgment and moral-spiritual repair.
5. **A catalog-dated Arabic manuscript with unresolved folio/date conflicts:** IES00272 as
   a custody and paratext case that demonstrates how much a shelfmark and catalog
   normalization cannot prove.
6. **Reader-facing archive lesson:** “what this manuscript records / what it cannot tell us”
   panels rather than seamless cultural summary.
