# R26 Grahamstown — Harry Wilbraham, 1908–1911 — Findings

**Research disposition:** A complete peer-reviewed source and its full published letter
transcription were opened. Original archival/private objects remain unopened. Findings below
describe the published source chain and its limits; they are not original-image verification.

## Bounded findings

1. Wilbraham's article deliberately constructs one case by crossing an affected person's
   retained letter, institutional case notes, civil-service and estate administration, private
   family traces, and retrospective descendant memory.
2. The article reports a sequence from disabling illness and work loss through medical
   boarding, committal on 24 March 1910, a retained letter dated 23 May 1910, and death in the
   institution in October 1911. This sequence is strong at the published-study level but has
   not been replayed against the original objects.
3. The article says it reproduces the letter in its entirety. The packet can therefore analyze
   the published transcription as mediated self-writing: it cannot verify the manuscript's
   handwriting, marginalia, physical order, annotations, or transcription accuracy.
4. The letter transcription represents its writer connecting illness, bodily change, work
   loss, travel, treatment, separation, and attachment to family. It establishes what the
   published text reports he wrote, not diagnosis, event truth, medical causation, capacity,
   or representativeness.
5. The article's placement of the letter beside a casebook note and its preservation in a
   patient-letter file supports a careful inference that institutional staff read and retained
   it. The source does not establish a universal correspondence policy or why it was not sent.
6. Family presence survives through an institutionally recorded committal statement, private
   financial records, family photographs, and later interviews. These layers are not
   interchangeable, and none supports an abandonment claim.
7. The opened study explicitly records uncertainty about visits and correspondence. The
   author's stated suspicion is preserved as interpretation, not promoted into fact.
8. The opened article argues that the case's unusually dense English-language administrative
   and private trail is shaped by the subject's position as a white settler civil servant and
   by the author's descendant access. This is one attributed case interpretation without a
   comparative denominator, not a quantified archive-survival claim.
9. No affected-person original, worker-authored record, nurse/attendant account, Black patient
   first-person source, family notebook, or interview transcript was independently opened.
10. Public quotation, images, clinical details, family finances, intimate allegations, and
    descendant material remain held regardless of technical web access.
