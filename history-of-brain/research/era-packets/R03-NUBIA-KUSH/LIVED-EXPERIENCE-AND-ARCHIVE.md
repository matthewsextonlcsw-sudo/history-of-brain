# R03 Nubia and Kush — Lived Experience and Archive

Status: internal research note; not public prose; **regional review pending**.
Date: 2026-08-18.

## The central archive problem

The people most relevant to a history of illness, disability, care, distress, and emotion are usually the least directly audible in the surviving ancient record. This packet contains bodies, tomb assemblages, architecture, graffiti, inscriptions, museum catalogues, and archaeologists' reconstructions. It contains no verified ancient first-person statement by an injured person, a care recipient, a person in distress, or a healer.

That absence is not neutral. Preservation favours bone and stone. Excavation and publication favour monumental sites and diagnostic cases. Meroitic language remains only partly understood. Colonial surveys removed human remains and objects while sorting people through racial categories. Dam-building and salvage work displaced modern communities. Current war threatens museums, records, and professional continuity. The archive is therefore a product of bodies and practices that survived **and** of political choices about excavation, translation, removal, custody, and publication.

## Ancient individuals: what can be said without stealing a voice

### Tombos Unit 27 Burial 1

**Directly preserved:** an adult woman's healed femur, humerus, and rib fractures; major femoral deformity; funerary bed/coffin traces, amulets, figurines, and beads; late New Kingdom/early Napatan dating. The article's case description, Figures 1–5, Discussion, and Conclusion describe organized healed callus, varus malunion, and complete union, while its abstract anomalously says “healed ... nonunion”; that source inconsistency remains explicit (SRC-004000, pp. 2–4, 9–10).

**Reasoned possibility:** impaired mobility and need for help with daily activities during some part of recovery. The likelihood rises if injuries were simultaneous, which is not known. A modern care framework can make the steps explicit but cannot prove delivered care or motive (SRC-004019).

**Not preserved:** her name, words, pain, fear, hope, preferences, relationships, whether assistance was welcome or coercive, who helped, the duration of impairment, and whether she considered herself disabled.

**Dignity rule:** refer to her as a person first, not “the fracture case.” Avoid scripting gratitude, bravery, helplessness, or community compassion for her.

### Amara West individual 244-8

**Directly preserved:** a 25–35-year-old man's skeleton and multiple destructive lesions; scientific imaging and microscopic observations; burial in a Nubian-region colonial settlement around 1200 BCE (SRC-004001).

**Best differential diagnosis:** metastatic carcinoma from an unidentified primary soft-tissue cancer.

**Not preserved:** symptoms, pain, awareness of illness, treatment, work or family response, stigma, spiritual interpretation, cause of death, or his own political/cultural identity.

**Dignity rule:** do not turn him into a “first cancer patient” spectacle. He is evidence that a disease process occurred, not a complete patient chart. The authors' possible schistosomiasis pathway is not his known cause.

### Sedeinga mortuary participants

**Directly preserved:** statues, dress attributes, stelae, graves, damage, and graphemes (SRC-004003).

**Scholarly reconstruction:** an officiant comparable to an Egyptian *sem*-priest; a name element related to life or vital force; mortuary ritual; possible deliberate damage by intruders.

**Not preserved:** a deceased person's understanding of self, mourners' grief, ritual efficacy, participants' words, or a perpetrator's fear. The article's “fear of the deceased's spirit” is an interpretation of damage, not a witnessed emotion.

**Dignity rule:** avoid narrating a cinematic grave robbery or using “vital force” as an exotic substitute for mental health.

## The translation silence

Meroitic inscriptions are not mute, but this packet's securely opened state-of-decipherment account is dated 2016. Rilly reported that letter values could be read and formulae sometimes parsed, while much vocabulary, verbal morphology, and narrative meaning remained uncertain (SRC-004002, printed pp. 2–3 and 7–9). The circa-100-word and corpus counts are 2016 reports, not current figures. This creates three distinct silences:

1. **Lexical silence:** no securely verified packet evidence for indigenous equivalents of “mind,” “mental illness,” “emotion,” “patient,” or “therapist.”
2. **Genre silence:** the corpus is dominated by funerary formulae; everyday letters, case histories, and therapeutic instructions are rare or inaccessible.
3. **Translation mediation:** specialists use Egyptian parallels, comparative linguistics, and archaeological context. These can be rigorous without becoming ancient self-explanation.

The archive must never use a modern English psychological term merely because an object or practice feels analogous. Nor should Egyptian terms be projected southward as though political and cultural interaction erased local difference.

## Sacred, restricted, and culturally controlled knowledge

No consulted page grants permission to reproduce sacred or restricted Nubian knowledge. Public museum images and public project media were deliberately not cached. The packet caches only four openly licensed scholarly PDFs.

For future work:

- Treat public availability as different from community permission.
- Ask source communities or governing projects before reproducing ritual media, mortuary images, human-remains images, or oral-history recordings beyond normal citation.
- Do not reproduce named collaborator testimony outside its editorial frame without checking the project's reuse and consent terms.
- Avoid displaying human remains simply to dramatize suffering.
- Attribute Meroitic translations to the translator and preserve uncertainty markers.
- If a locally governed archive identifies material as closed, gendered, ceremonial, or context-limited, exclude it even when a copy is technically reachable.

No culturally restricted full text or media was downloaded in this pass.

**Use:** as her situated experience and political understanding of language learning.
**Do not use:** as evidence that modern and ancient linguistic identities are identical, or as a substitute for ancient Meroitic translation.

### Shadia Abdrabo — museum work amid war

Sudanese archaeologist and curator Shadia Abdrabo describes forced displacement after the 2023 war, emotionally demanding heritage work, damage and loss, a database project for Sudanese collections, the importance of local communities, and disrupted professional continuity (SRC-004014).

**Use:** as named first-person professional testimony and as an explanation of current archive conditions.
**Do not use:** as an audited national loss inventory or as the sole Sudanese view.

## Colonial mediation and collection custody

### The First Archaeological Survey

The 1910 human-remains volume is both evidence and an artifact of colonial knowledge production (SRC-004018). It records fractures and splints and sometimes admits uncertainty. It also organizes evidence through early racial science, explicitly including “The Racial Problem.”

The usable unit is therefore: “the investigators reported X at page Y, using the methods and categories of 1910.” Their report cannot silently become a current diagnosis. The excavated people did not consent through a documented modern framework, and their descendants' preferences are absent from the report.

### UNESCO salvage and displacement

UNESCO's institutional history foregrounds international preservation, object recovery, and temple relocation (SRC-004010). Lemos argues that monument-centred salvage histories can underweight displacement and local questions of benefit (SRC-004009, especially pp. 29–33). Yasmin separately reports displacement-related loss in her own modern experience (SRC-004012).

These accounts should not be collapsed into false balance. They answer different questions:

- UNESCO documents what the institution organized and preserved.
- Critical scholarship analyzes whose interests and stories structured that work.
- Named collaborators report lived consequences and heritage meaning.

### Division, export, and long-term custody

- The MFA says division of finds from Reisner's 1913–1932 excavations produced major holdings outside Sudan (SRC-004016).
- The British Museum says some Sudanese human remains were transferred by NCAM in 2007 and describes their care through its own ethical framework (SRC-004017).
- The Tombos team records NCAM permission to export remains for study in 2011 and identifies Purdue curation (SRC-004000, p. 2 and Acknowledgments).

These statements establish institutional chains. They do **not** establish:

- descendant or local-community consent;
- whether consent could be withdrawn;
- community access to remains, data, or results;
- preferred place of curation;
- repatriation claims or the absence of such claims;
- whether legal transfer and ethical legitimacy coincide.

Future public content should link to the holding institution but also state what its page does not document.

## Translation and terminology cautions

- **“Ba statue”:** retain as a catalogue/scholarly label; do not define it as the Egyptian soul without a locally grounded argument.
- **“Magic”:** historical scholarly category that may flatten religion, protection, medicine, and ritual. Attribute its use.
- **“Vital force”:** attributed linguistic gloss, not a biomedical substance or mental-energy concept.
- **“Patient”:** useful only when discussing a modern care analogy; no ancient clinical relationship is established.
- **“Hospital”:** rejected as a finding for Musawwarat; at most a former hypothesis.
- **“Compassion”:** a possible motive, not an osteological observation.
- **“Disability”:** modern analytic category that can illuminate barriers and care but should not be imposed as an ancient self-identity.
- **“Hydrocephalus case”:** do not repeat the conference abstract's stigmatizing terminology; no public use until reassessed.
- **“Kushite”:** do not use as a blanket ethnic label for every individual or site in geographical Nubia across all periods.
- **“Nubia”:** a region and historically shifting scholarly/political construct, not a timeless single culture.

## Empathetic narrative guardrails

1. Begin with the person, not the diagnosis or specimen number.
2. State the observation before the interpretation.
3. Make uncertainty visible without making the person disappear.
4. Never invent thoughts, dialogue, suffering, resilience, gratitude, or caregiver motive.
5. Do not use modern community testimony as atmosphere for ancient scenes.
6. Avoid “primitive/superstitious,” “despite disability,” “sufferer,” “defect,” and sensational “first ever” labels.
7. Name colonial collection and current custody when a museum object or human-remains study carries the claim.
8. Give locally grounded voices authority over their own reported experience, with exact attribution and editorial context.
9. Include institutional successes and harms in the same causal frame when both shaped the archive.
10. Let “we do not know” be a substantive historical result.
