# R11 Bīmāristāns and Institutions — Lived Experience and Archive Audit

**Principle:** institutional provision is not recipient experience. “Patient voice” is not claimed where a physician, traveler, jurist, endowment scribe, chronicler, editor, or modern historian controls the surviving wording.

## Recipient evidence that survives

### 1. Distrust, work, family negotiation, and death

EVD-016024 is the strongest recipient-adjacent passage in the packet. It is valuable because it does not fit a triumphalist recovery arc: a patient is represented weighing medical instruction against returning to work, disagreeing with physicians, negotiating with his wife, and dying. It is also ethically hazardous. The story is written by a physician within a biographical tradition and uses the outcome to vindicate practitioners. Public use would need to keep the attribution visible—“the physician-author says”—and avoid converting blame into fact.

### 2. A person turned into a case label

EVD-016023 preserves no name or speech for the person labeled `māniyā`. The institution and celebrated practitioner are more legible than the recipient. The case can illustrate historical categorization and a narrated intervention, but not the person's diagnosis in modern terms or their experience of opium, ward life, improvement, or discharge.

### 3. Restraint alongside treatment

EVD-016044 is important precisely because it refuses a simple humane-versus-cruel binary: the traveler reports both treatment and chains in the **old** Damascus hospital. EVD-016027 separately describes the new hospital's registers, physician visits, medicines, and foods. The historical term, criteria for confinement, type/duration of restraint, and recipient interpretations are unrecoverable. Neither institution is identified as al-Nūrī by name in this page. A source-specific account should preserve the attribution seam and leave the moral category open.

## Workers and unpaid care

Formal physicians, supervisors, teachers, and later paid male/female staff appear in institution-centered sources. Missing or weakly visible groups include:

- nurses, attendants, cleaners, cooks, water carriers, pharmacists' assistants, guards, porters, builders, repair workers, scribes, and burial workers as individual people;
- wives and other kin who interpreted advice, supplied food, managed dressings, accompanied people, or absorbed household labor;
- enslaved or unfree workers and recipients;
- women as healers, practitioners, donors, recipients, or household decision-makers beyond brief gendered categories;
- children, older people, rural travelers, migrants, and people unable to work;
- Jewish and Christian medical practitioners or recipients affected by the Manṣūrī exclusion rule;
- people refused admission, discharged early, restrained, or treated at home.

SRC-016001 explicitly warns that the biographical corpus privileges elite urban male physicians. EVD-016033's later reference to male and female paid personnel is not a substitute for worker testimony.

## Archive-production audit

### What each surviving form was made to do

| Archive form | Likely purpose | Structural bias |
|---|---|---|
| Building/foundation inscription | memorialize patronage, piety, repair, and legal legitimacy | makes patron, supervisor, and ideal beneficiary visible; suppresses contested access and failure |
| Waqf deed | govern property, eligibility, offices, and distributions | records norm and asset structure, not implementation or feelings |
| Appointment decree | confer office and authority | foregrounds elite officeholders; says little about actual attendance or subordinate labor |
| Physician biography/case anecdote | establish lineage, learning, professional reputation, memorable skill | selects exemplary cases and centers practitioners; recipients are narrated objects or foils |
| Traveler account | make places notable to readers | episodic observation; may compress institutions and categories |
| Later chronicle/topography | preserve urban memory and institutional description | can mix documents, retrospective report, and contemporary conditions across dates |
| Surviving architecture | material trace of planned/built space | use, staffing, sensory experience, and later alteration require separate evidence |

### Colonial, editorial, and translation mediation

- Modern access depends on European and contemporary critical editions, public-domain scans, OCR, repository systems, and English/French/Turkish translations. The chain must name these layers.
- The 1907 Ibn Jubayr OCR is navigation-only. Printed p. 283 was visually collated from the public-domain scan, separating lines 8–14 (new hospital) from lines 14–16 (old hospital). Public quotation still requires a named rights-cleared critical translation or reviewed project translation.
- Terms such as `bīmāristān`, `māniyā`, `al-junūn al-sabuʿī`, and translated “mad” do not map cleanly onto modern hospital, mania, psychosis, or psychiatric-patient categories.
- Modern architectural reconstruction is vulnerable to converting plausible room functions into observed facts; EVD-016034 documents this exact historiographical dispute.
- The packet did not reproduce copyrighted monument photographs or culturally restricted material. No sacred/restricted-knowledge issue was identified for the opened sources, but absence of a flag in catalogue metadata is not affirmative permission to reuse images.

## Archive silences by claim type

- **Admission:** modern conduits report Qalāwūn norms, but the deed was not opened; al-Nūrī admission procedure is not comparably documented. Actual refusals, queues, capacity, and enforcement remain unknown.
- **Ward use:** architectural rooms and later departmental descriptions survive, but foundation-year use is not directly observed.
- **Mental distress:** one al-Nūrī case and a traveler report specifically about the old Damascus hospital survive; no psychiatric census, dedicated-ward foundation record, recipient account, or diagnostic series was found.
- **Treatment:** substances, foods, surgery, rounds, and prescriptions appear; consent, dose safety, side effects, and durability are largely absent.
- **Outcome:** a few exemplary recoveries and a death appear; counts, denominators, follow-up, and recipient-reported outcomes do not.
- **Community care:** a Qalāwūn home-support norm and one wife-mediated case show that care exceeded institution walls; household experience remains almost entirely silent.
- **Religion/status/access:** Qalāwūn legal inclusion/exclusion is visible; lived enforcement and alternative networks are not.

## Empathy rules for later writing

1. Name the mediation: “the physician-author represents,” “the deed directs,” “the traveler reports.”
2. Preserve historical terms before offering a cautious gloss.
3. Do not turn a person into proof that a treatment worked or failed.
4. Put exclusion, restraint, and adverse outcomes beside charitable ideals without sensationalizing either.
5. Avoid “patients were treated humanely” and “the mentally ill” as totalizing categories.
6. Describe what the record cannot tell us—consent, fear, pain, family burden, return to work, duration, and outcome—when those absences change interpretation.
