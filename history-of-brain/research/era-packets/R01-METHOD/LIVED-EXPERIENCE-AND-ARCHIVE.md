# R01-METHOD — Lived Experience, Missing Voices, and Archive Limits

## The first finding is absence

No direct account from M487, M9, Shanidar 1, a caregiver, a family member, or another member of their communities survives in the evidence consulted for this packet. There is no recorded name, self-description, diagnosis, memory, feeling, complaint, request, refusal, gratitude, fear, pain statement, identity label, or account of a caring relationship.

That means:

- material survival is not patient testimony;
- an excavator's empathy is not the individual's experience;
- a modeled care regimen is not a remembered daily routine;
- modern disability expertise can expose our assumptions but cannot supply a prehistoric person's missing words; and
- “they probably felt…” is not an acceptable bridge across the gap.

This packet can discuss **the archive of a life**, not recover **the life as experienced**.

## What the archive selects

### Bone-visible survival bias

The three cases entered scholarship because they are unusually visible:

- a deliberate-looking hole in a relatively intact skull;
- extreme vertebral fusion and limb disuse changes;
- multiple healed lesions and narrowed auditory canals in a comparatively complete fossil.

This selection excludes or undercounts:

- mental distress and most cognitive/psychiatric differences;
- pain without skeletal remodeling;
- deafness, blindness, paralysis, illness, or trauma without diagnostic bony change;
- fluctuating or episodic conditions;
- acute conditions that killed before bone could respond;
- soft-tissue disability;
- people whose remains decayed, were cremated, disturbed, repatriated, not excavated, not retained, or never studied;
- people cared for so successfully that no lesion developed;
- people neglected, excluded, or killed in ways that left no distinctive trace; and
- routine dependence and mutual aid that did not involve pathology.

Dettwyler explicitly notes that deafness, blindness, intellectual/mental differences, and many illnesses may have no skeletal manifestation. The archive therefore cannot establish how rare disability or caregiving was. [`SRC-001004`, pp. 380–381](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajpa.1330840402)

### Survival paradox

A visible chronic lesion can require survival long enough for bone to change. The record can therefore overrepresent survivors relative to people with the same condition who died earlier, while simultaneously underrepresenting conditions that never affect bone. This is not a prevalence sample and cannot support “people in this culture usually…”

### Excavation and preservation

- M487's body is poorly preserved apart from the skull. The authors themselves suggest other openings may have been lost with poorly preserved remains, but this is not countable evidence. [`SRC-001000`, PDF pp. 2–7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12520-020-01151-w)
- M9 lacks the lower thoracic region, most ribs/lumbar vertebrae, and lower appendicular joints, limiting diagnosis and function. [`SRC-001002`, pp. 107–109](https://doi.org/10.1537/ase.081114)
- Shanidar 1 observations were made in 1976–78 without cranial radiography, and later reanalysis was not feasible. [`SRC-001005`, Materials and methods](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0186684)

Negative evidence must always name this preservation boundary.

## Five voices that must not be collapsed

| voice/position | what it can tell us | what it cannot stand in for |
|---|---|---|
| The person's skeleton/remains | physical alteration, biological response, some duration and function constraints | the person's words, feelings, identity, wishes, or interpretation |
| Excavator/analyst | recovery context, measurements, differential diagnosis, scholarly inference | neutral access to the individual's experience |
| Institution/museum | curation, object/cast provenance, public framing, rights statements | original community authority or proof of a historical motive |
| Modern clinical literature | physiological possibilities and functional ranges | exact prehistoric symptoms, environment, treatment, or quality of life |
| Disabled scholars/communities today | expertise about ableist assumptions, adaptation, access, identity, language, and interpretive harms | a license to assign a specific prehistoric identity or experience |

The Disabled Archaeologists Network authors argue for broad, identity-aware definitions and for disability expertise to shape archaeological practice. That influence is methodological: it widens hypotheses and catches ableist assumptions. It must not become invented testimony. [`SRC-001008`](https://onlinedigeditions.com/article/Confronting+Ableism+in+Archaeology+with+Disability+Expertise/4346876/761718/article.html)

## Impairment, disability, dependence, and care

Use these as separate research terms:

- **Observed alteration:** a measured feature of bone or context.
- **Impairment:** a bounded functional difference inferred from anatomy/clinical comparison.
- **Disability:** interaction among body, activity, environment, social structures, identity, and participation. It is not read directly from bone.
- **Dependence/interdependence:** need for assistance in particular tasks; all human communities contain it. It is not a moral ranking.
- **Care/support:** an action, arrangement, accommodation, or resource that helps meet a need.
- **Compassion:** an emotional state or motive; not an archaeological synonym for care.

Avoid the heroic binary in which a disabled person is either a helpless burden saved by kindness or an inspirational figure who overcomes disability. Dettwyler's critique and contemporary disability expertise both show how that binary erases adaptation, contribution, ordinary interdependence, and social barriers. [`SRC-001004`, pp. 379–384](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajpa.1330840402); [`SRC-001008`](https://onlinedigeditions.com/article/Confronting+Ableism+in+Archaeology+with+Disability+Expertise/4346876/761718/article.html)

## Case-specific lived-experience ceilings

### M487

**Can say:** the case authors favor deliberate alteration, and no apparent healing is compatible with perimortem or postmortem alteration; the evidence does not resolve timing or motive.
**Cannot say:** the person sought treatment; was conscious; experienced pain; consented; believed in spirits; was a “patient”; died during surgery; or was subject to a ritual.

The distinction between perimortem and immediate postmortem action is decisive. With no healing and no written/relational evidence, patient experience is unavailable.

### M9

**Can say:** severe, long-standing physical changes and survival make recurring assistance plausible and strongly favored by the case authors, pending independent functional review.
**Cannot say:** who helped; whether help was affectionate, obligatory, reciprocal, coercive, or resented; whether M9 accepted or refused it; whether care was continuous; what role M9 held; what he contributed; or how he evaluated his life.

The 2011 paper's inferences about will, self-esteem, psychological adaptation, and an inspiring personality are rejected for project use. Those are not modest analogies; they are claims about a person with no surviving voice. [`SRC-001003`, p. 40](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpp.2011.02.003)

### Shanidar 1

**Can say:** this individual lived for years with multiple long-standing physical and probable sensory differences; social support is a credible interpretation.
**Cannot say:** he was nonproductive, completely dependent, loved, revered, a shaman, pitied, abused, or proof of Neanderthal moral humanity.

The public record often uses his body to decide whether Neanderthals were “human.” That framing makes a disabled person's survival a moral test for an entire species and then treats compassion toward disability as the marker of worth. Penn Museum's page states this explicitly. The packet should document that history of interpretation, not reproduce it. [`SRC-001009`, archived institutional page](https://web.archive.org/web/20250817042710/https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/revealing-ancient-faces/)

## Missing caregivers

Caregiver labor is inferred but almost completely anonymous. The archive does not identify:

- number, age, gender, kinship, status, or health of helpers;
- whether tasks were shared or specialized;
- whether support was occasional, intensive, or technologically mediated;
- costs, burdens, benefits, conflict, reciprocity, fatigue, or refusal;
- the knowledge used; or
- the difference between direct help and an accommodating routine/environment.

Do not turn caregiver absence into the generic figure of a devoted mother, family, healer, nurse, or compassionate band. If later evidence supplies gendered or relational information, it needs its own record.

## Mortuary treatment is one event

M9's broadly comparable mortuary treatment and Shanidar 1's deposition context may show how bodies were treated after death. Neither supplies a continuous report of life. Mortuary inclusion can coexist with affection, duty, stigma, conflict, hierarchy, or exploitation. It should be linked as contextual evidence, not promoted to direct evidence of lifetime social acceptance.

## Remains, images, casts, and public display

### Current provenance facts

- `SRC-001005` reports that Shanidar 1 was analyzed in the Iraq Museum; Penn Museum says the original skeleton remains in Iraq and describes a 1970s mold transferred to Penn in 1980, from which a cast was made. [`SRC-001009`, archived institutional page](https://web.archive.org/web/20250817042710/https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/revealing-ancient-faces/)
- `SRC-001002` and `SRC-001003` acknowledge cooperation with the Institute of Archaeology, Hanoi, but do not provide a complete current custody/protocol statement for M9.
- `SRC-001000` does not identify M487's present repository or a community/cultural protocol.

### Ethical decision for this packet

No photograph, CT image, reconstruction, cast image, or in-situ human-remains image is approved for public use.

Reasons:

1. CC BY status for an article image is only copyright permission; it does not answer cultural authority, dignity, descendant/community consultation, or necessity.
2. M487 and M9 repository/protocol details are incomplete.
3. Facial reconstruction can turn uncertain tissue, complexion, hair, scarring, and age into apparent fact. Penn's reconstruction page includes several such choices.
4. The evidence can be taught through diagrams of the inference ladder and non-identifying anatomical schematics without displaying remains.

Any later asset proposal needs repository confirmation, exact image/object identifier, rights holder, credit line, original caption, alteration history, cultural/ethical review, and a reason the learning goal cannot be met less intrusively.

## Archive language rules for later writing

- Say “the remains show…” for direct observation.
- Say “the authors infer…” for diagnosis, function, care, or motive.
- Say “survival is consistent with / strongly suggests recurring assistance…” only when alternatives were tested.
- Say “we cannot recover…” for motive, emotion, identity, or experience.
- Avoid “suffered from” unless direct testimony or a carefully bounded clinical effect supports suffering.
- Avoid “confined,” “crippled,” “burden,” “nonproductive,” “helpless,” and “normal” as unmarked descriptions.
- Use identity-first or person-first language only when appropriate to a living person's stated preference; for unnamed ancient individuals, describe the evidence and avoid assigning identity.
- Never use a remains image or impairment as spectacle, proof of evolutionary worth, or a hook for “the first compassion.”
