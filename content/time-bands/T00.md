## Draft

This is the world before writing. Bodies, care, survival, and the limit of inference. How can we study minds before words survive? The answer is: we can't, directly. We can only infer — and the inference is where the violence lives.

The [backbone's B0 lived-experience rule](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: no direct voice survives. Say so prominently. The [evidence model's voice rule](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) is blunt: protector, elder, clinician, archive, or institutional speech is not family or patient consent. Do not infer burden, support, abandonment, motive, or consensus.

Trepanation — a hole in a skull — is the most common piece of evidence we have for prehistoric mental health. What it can tell us: someone cut into a skull, and the person survived. What it cannot tell us: why they did it, what they thought they were doing, whether the person consented, whether the community cared, whether the person was "sick" in any sense we can recognize. The hole is a fact. The story is an inference.

Did prehistoric communities care for disabled people? The evidence is mixed. Some skeletal remains show healed fractures, healed infections, and healed trepanations — signs of survival past the point where the injury or illness would have been fatal without care. Other remains show no such care. The answer depends on the site, the period, and the specific community. It is not one thing. It is a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

The [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) records diagnostic tablets that locate thought, emotion, and decision-making in the heart. The [UCL Letters to the Dead](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/lettersdead.html) show family petitions and heart-centered vocabularies. The [National Library of Medicine](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html) shows the Edwin Smith Papyrus: a surgical text that describes the brain as soft and vulnerable.

The [Lloyd & Bowen account](https://doi.org/10.1525/9780520964800) shows the heart–brain debate in Greek medicine. The [UK National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/mental-health/) warns that many mental-health patient files were destroyed. The silence is not absence of resistance or consent. It is the result of a specific, traceable act of destruction.

The [backbone's B4.9 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) asks who gets believed in the Ann Pratt official dossier. The archive structurally underrepresents people outside the property-and-court jurisdiction. The [backbone's B2.7 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) asks who had a choice of healer. The answer depends on status, gender, enslavement, household authority, cost, and pilgrimage.

The [backbone's B10.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows neurodiversity, mad pride, recovery, and disability justice: separate and overlapping movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects. The evidence lab is not a hierarchy. It is a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

The answer depends on what you think a person is. If a person is a machine, the bone is a part. If a person is a relation, the bone is a network. If a person is a whole that cannot be taken apart, the question itself is the mistake.
