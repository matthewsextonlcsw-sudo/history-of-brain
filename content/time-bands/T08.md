## Draft

This is not one policy. It is a set of national and colonial variations, each with its own deinstitutionalization, its own promise, its own violence. The dates overlap because the variations overlap. There is no single "deinstitutionalization." There are many, in many places, in many jurisdictions.

The [backbone's B8.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: deinstitutionalization was not one policy or one outcome. Family burden, abandonment, and housing are part of the uneven process. The [backbone's B8.2 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) names the movements: patients, survivors, disabled people, and families organize. Rights, refusal, peer support, independent living, recovery, mad liberation, family advocacy, and internal disagreements. The movements are not a single story. They are a set of overlapping, sometimes conflicting, sometimes separate movements.

The [backbone's B8.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) traces decolonization and institutional inheritance: Fann in Senegal, Fanon in Algeria, African national systems, South Asian institutions, Southeast Asian states, the Caribbean, and Pacific policy changes. The [backbone's B8.4 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows when reform fails catastrophically: use inquiries and testimony—such as Life Esidimeni—to examine outsourcing, neglect, accountability, and the meaning of community care. The [backbone's B8.5 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) names the right to leave the hospital—and the right to real support: Brazil's anti-asylum movement, the [1990 Caracas Declaration](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241580520), Italy's influence, Ghanaian user organizing, and the gap between closure and care.

The [backbone's B8.6 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) traces neurodiversity, mad pride, recovery, and disability justice: separate and overlapping movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects. The [backbone's B8.7 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: the archive is not a neutral record. It is a site of power.

The [R31 Japan packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) shows the family's role in legal form: the 1950 law assigned duties to a legally designated protector, including financial and discharge-retrieval obligations. The [R33 Aro packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) shows the worker's role in care: organizer/admin descriptions include "described nursing and medical roles" and "treatment and occupational activity."

The transition is not a clean break. Deinstitutionalization does not end. It changes. The next band picks up where these variations leave off — not where they end, because they do not end.
