## Draft

The institution runs on their labor, and the history runs on their silence. Nurses, attendants, social workers, peer workers, community workers, interpreters, domestic workers, and ritual specialists — they are the machinery that holds the person, and they are the first voices the archive forgets.

The [backbone's B5.2 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) names the asylum as a workplace. The architecture, the promise, the instrument of rule — all of it runs on the labor of the people inside it. The [backbone's B6.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) asks what happened to the people inside expanding asylums: admissions, chronicity, work, restraint, medication, abuse, community ties, death, discharge, and patient writing. The workers are in that list, but they are not the subject. They are the infrastructure.

The [backbone's B6.6 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) traces the shift from charity to social work: settlement work, casework, mutual aid, poor law, child welfare, public health, Black reform networks, labor, and the contested professional boundary. The workers were the ones doing the work, and the professionals were the ones writing the history. The [backbone's B8.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the workers' role in organizing: peer support, independent living, recovery, mad liberation, family advocacy, and internal disagreements. The workers were not passive. They organized, they resisted, they refused.

The [R31 Japan packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) shows the worker's role in legal form: the 1950 law assigned duties to a legally designated protector, including financial and discharge-retrieval obligations. The worker — or their proxy — was the enforcement mechanism. The [R33 Aro packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) shows the worker's role in care: organizer/admin descriptions include "described nursing and medical roles" and "treatment and occupational activity." The workers were the care infrastructure.

The [evidence model's voice rule](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) is blunt: no workload, pay, discretion, safety, dissent, labor condition, or professional experience without direct, role-specific sources. The archive does not give us the worker's voice. It gives us the institution's record of the worker's role. The [whose-voice-survives page](H04.md) is the method for tracing that chain. And the [people-speaking-for-themselves page](V01.md) is the complement: when the worker speaks for the person, the chain is longer, and the voice is more mediated.

The [backbone's B0 lived-experience rule](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) applies to workers the same way it applies to patients: no direct voice survives. Say so prominently. The workers are the machinery, and the machinery is silent.
