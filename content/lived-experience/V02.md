## Draft

The voice is real. The frame is not. When a person's words appear inside someone else's record, the question is not "did they say it?" but "who held the pen, who selected the words, who framed the context, and who decided what survives?"

The [backbone's B5.7 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) asks the right question: who gets believed in the Ann Pratt official dossier? The answer is a constructed evidence chain — elicited examinations, reported experiences, worker evidence, administrative defense, hostile credibility framing, and bounded procedural agency. The chain is not impartial. It is a machinery of belief, and the person inside it is both the subject and the object.

**Pratt, Jamaica, 1860.** Trench took down her statement, read it back to her, she corrected it, she signed it, and she refused to alter its substance. The clerk controlled the transcription. The subject controlled the correction. But Trench also called her an "ignorant woman," selected the supporting witnesses, and conceded servants "sometimes used more roughness and severity than desirable" — hostile framing and partial concession inside the same file. The [R22 packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/R22-JAMAICA-OFFICIAL-DOCUMENTS-1860/FINDINGS.md) preserves both the voice and the frame. Fryar (2013) is blunt: Black testimony did not enter the imperial archive unfiltered.

**St. Thomas, 1738–1753.** The six letters that survive are a fraction of a corpus of ~150. The 1996 Bøgh edition silently narrowed the sample — the editor selected which voices survive. Van Rossem reports that most 1741 correspondents dictated, which means the dictator shaped the words. Damma's 1739 letter carries three language layers — an African-language original, a Creole rendering, an English translation — and each layer is a different hand. The [R19 packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/R19-ST-THOMAS-ENSLAVED-LETTERS/FINDINGS.md) preserves the transmission matrix: who dictated, who translated, who edited, who printed, who filed.

**Grahamstown, 1908–1911.** A 2014 descendant-scholar article publishes a complete letter transcription. The scholar selected the letter, transcribed it, and framed it within clinical, administrative, employment, pension, family, and retrospective-memory layers. The original manuscript remains unopened. The [R26 packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/R26-GRAHAMSTOWN-WILBRAHAM-1908-1911/FINDINGS.md) preserves the boundary: the transcription is direct only for what the publication reports, not for the manuscript's handwriting, marginalia, or physical order.

The pattern is consistent: the voice is real, but the frame is controlled by someone else. The healer, the clerk, the judge, the relative, the translator, the editor, the scholar — each one held the pen at some point, and each one shaped what survives. The [whose-voice-survives page](H04.md) is the method for tracing that chain. The [evidence ladder](H03.md) applies to reported speech the same way it applies to bone: the record supports a claim about what was reported, not about what a person felt. And the [people-speaking-for-themselves page](V01.md) is the complement: when the voice is direct, the chain is shorter, but it is never zero.
