## Draft

The voice in the record is the doctor's voice. The doctor wrote down what he saw. The doctor wrote down what he did. The person did not write down what they felt.

The National Archives guide says the records "relate mainly to the administration of the institutions." The voice is the administrator's voice. The voice is the doctor's voice. The voice is not the patient's voice.

Here is how to tell the voices apart. The admission register is the clerk's voice. It gives you the name, the age, the date. The quarterly return is the superintendent's voice. It gives you the number of patients. The correspondence with the poor law union is the union's voice. It gives you the name, the age, the type of disability, and whether the person was considered dangerous. None of these voices is the patient's voice.

The patient's voice is in the gap. Most patient files have been destroyed. The direct voice is often gone. What survives is the institutional voice. A good page tells you which voice you are reading. It does not let you mistake the doctor's words for the person's words.

The Wellcome Collection has documents. The documents are the doctor's words. The documents are not the patient's words. The patient's voice is not in the record. The patient's voice is in the gap.

One more thing: the voice label. A good page labels the voice. It says: this is the clerk's voice, this is the superintendent's voice, this is the union's voice. It does not let you mistake one voice for another. The label is the point. It tells you whose words you are reading.

That is the point of this page: the voice in the record is the doctor's voice. The person's voice is not in the record. You can check the record. You can see whose voice it is. You can see whose voice is missing.
