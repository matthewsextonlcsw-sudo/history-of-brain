## Draft

The archive is not only a record of what was done to people. It is also a record of what they did back. Patient, survivor, disability, Mad, neurodivergent, Indigenous, queer, feminist, antiracist, family, and labor movements — they are not a footnote to the history of mental health. They are the history.

The [backbone's B8.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) names the movements: rights, refusal, peer support, independent living, recovery, mad liberation, family advocacy, and internal disagreements. The movements are not a single story. They are a set of overlapping, sometimes conflicting, sometimes separate movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects.

The [backbone's B9.5 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the movements in action: Brazil's anti-asylum movement, the 1990 Caracas Declaration, Italy's influence, Ghanaian user organizing, and the gap between closure and care. The [1990 Caracas Declaration](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241580520) names the rights to mental health care and to protection from abusive treatment as central — the moment "care" and "control" are joined in the same sentence. The movement was not a single event. It was a set of movements, in different countries, with different arguments, different strategies, and different outcomes.

The [backbone's B10.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) traces the contemporary movements: neurodiversity, mad pride, recovery, and disability justice. These are separate and overlapping movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects. The [backbone's B8.4 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the decolonizing movements: Fann in Senegal, Fanon in Algeria, African national systems, South Asian institutions, Southeast Asian states, the Caribbean, and Pacific policy changes. The [backbone's B6.8 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) centers Indigenous testimony, sovereignty, language, land, and community-led healing — and refuses to reduce the story to a clinical trauma label.

The [backbone's B9.8 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the movements' limits: when reform fails catastrophically, the inquiry and the testimony are the record. Life Esidimeni is one example: outsourcing, neglect, accountability, and the meaning of community care. The movement did not save everyone. The record shows what it did, and what it did not.

The [evidence model's voice rule](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) is blunt: never create interior experience, composite testimony, consent, preference, label, recovery, or representative response. The movements' voices are real, but they are mediated — through the organization, the publication, the testimony, the inquiry. The [whose-voice-survives page](H04.md) is the method for tracing that chain. And the [people-speaking-for-themselves page](V01.md) is the complement: when the movement speaks for the person, the chain is longer, and the voice is more mediated.
