## Draft

The family is not a backdrop. It is the machinery that holds the person — and the person holds the family. Care, conflict, cost, commitment, discharge, advocacy, burden, love, and refusal are not private matters. They are the infrastructure of mental health, and they show up in the record in specific, traceable ways.

The [backbone's B1.2 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows this in the earliest written worlds: family petitions alongside heart-centered vocabularies. The family was a site of care and appeal, not just a social unit. In Egypt, the family petitioned the dead, the gods, and the living on behalf of the distressed. The family was the first institution of mental health, and it was also the first institution of control.

The [backbone's B5.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) asks the question the popular story skips: did moral treatment feel moral to patients? The records show the design and the rules — the regimen, the environment, the labor, the restraint. But family burden, commitment, and discharge are part of the institutional machinery. The family was the commitment mechanism: who decided the person was "mad," who paid for their care, who retrieved them at discharge. The [R31 Japan packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) shows this in legal form: the 1950 law assigned duties to a legally designated protector, including financial and discharge-retrieval obligations. The family — or its legal proxy — was the enforcement mechanism.

The [R33 Aro packet](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) shows the same pattern in a different setting: organizer/admin descriptions include "accompanying relatives" and "selected boarding in neighboring villages." The family was the care infrastructure. The relatives accompanied the patient, boarded them in neighboring villages, and maintained the connection between the hospital and the community. The family was not a bystander. It was the system.

The [backbone's B8.2 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the family's role in deinstitutionalization: family burden, abandonment, and housing are part of the uneven process. When the hospital closed, the family absorbed the care — or the person was abandoned. The [backbone's B8.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the family's role in organizing: rights, refusal, peer support, independent living, recovery, mad liberation, family advocacy, and internal disagreements. The family is not a monolith. It is a site of conflict, love, burden, and refusal.

The [evidence model's voice rule](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/BATCH-08-SEAM-SYNTHESIS.md) is blunt: protector, elder, clinician, archive, or institutional speech is not family or patient consent. Do not infer burden, support, abandonment, motive, or consensus. The family's voice is mediated — through the protector's legal duty, the elder's authority, the clinician's record, the archive's selection. The [whose-voice-survives page](H04.md) is the method for tracing that chain. And the [people-speaking-for-themselves page](V01.md) is the complement: when the family speaks for the person, the chain is longer, and the voice is more mediated.
