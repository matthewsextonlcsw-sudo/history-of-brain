## Draft

You are reading about a treatment. The treatment was used two thousand years ago. The treatment is still used today. But the treatment is not one thing. It is five things.

The treatment evidence lab separates the five things.

**Historical use.** What the treatment was. What it looked like. Who used it. Where. When. Example: "This herb was used in Mesopotamia around 1000 BCE to treat a specific condition." That is the historical use. It is a fact. The herb was used. The date is established. The place is established.

**Patient experience.** What the patient felt. What the patient said. What the patient did. Example: "The patient described relief after taking the herb." That is the patient experience. It is a testimony. It is not a fact in the same way the historical use is a fact. It is a report. It may be accurate. It may not. The lab records the testimony and the limitations.

**Mechanism claim.** How the treatment works. What it does to the body. What it does to the mind. Example: "The herb is believed to affect the brain in a specific way." That is the mechanism claim. It is an interpretation. It is not a fact in the same way the historical use is a fact. It is a hypothesis. It may be correct. It may not. The lab records the hypothesis and the evidence behind it.

**Present efficacy.** Does the treatment work now? What does current evidence say? Example: "Current research shows that the herb has a specific effect on the brain." That is the present efficacy. It is a current claim. It is not a historical claim. It is a claim about now. The lab records the current evidence and the limitations.

**Current safety.** Is the treatment safe now? What are the risks? What are the side effects? Example: "Current research shows that the herb has specific risks when taken in high doses." That is the current safety. It is a current claim. It is not a historical claim. It is a claim about now. The lab records the current safety information and the limitations.

The lab separates the five things. It does not collapse them into one. It does not say "the treatment works" and mean all five at once. It says: here is the historical use. Here is the patient experience. Here is the mechanism claim. Here is the present efficacy. Here is the current safety. Each one is a different claim. Each one has a different burden of proof.

The burden of proof for a causal claim is high. The evidence model says: "Evidence designed or argued for causality plus credible limitations and alternatives." That means: you need evidence that was designed to test the causal claim. You need to argue for the causality. You need to name the limitations. You need to name the alternatives. You cannot say "the treatment works" unless you have done the work.

The sources in this collection come from trusted places. The [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/) holds medical history materials, including treatment descriptions and patient records. The [UK National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/mental-health/) holds mental health patient files.

These are not blogs. These are institutions that have been checking and preserving these sources for centuries. The treatment evidence lab tells you which institution holds which source, and what the source actually says.

You do not have to trust the treatment. You have to trust the lab. The lab shows you the five things. You can disagree with the claim. You can look at the source yourself. That is the point.

The treatment evidence lab is not a decoration. It is the foundation. Without it, the claim is a guess. With it, the claim is a claim with a trail. You can follow the trail. You can check the work. You can disagree with the claim and still trust the source.

That is the treatment evidence lab. Not a list. A lab. You can run it on any treatment.
