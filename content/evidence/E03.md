# E03 — Translation Desk

> *Every word you read on this page was translated. Not just from one language to another. From one world to yours. The translation desk shows you who did the translating, what they chose, and where the choices get contested.*

## Draft

You are reading a word. The word is "mind." But the word was not always "mind." Two thousand years ago, in Mesopotamia, the word was something else. In Egypt, something else. In Greece, something else. In China, something else.

The translation desk is where the words get translated. Not just from one language to another. From one world to yours.

Here is the problem. When a translator takes a word from Akkadian and turns it into English, they make a choice. They choose the English word that feels closest. But "closest" is not the same as "the same." The Akkadian word might mean "the thing that thinks." The English word "mind" means "the thing that thinks." But the Akkadian word also means "the thing that feels" and "the thing that decides." The English word "mind" does not carry all three meanings at once.

The translation desk shows you the choice. It tells you: the original word is X. The translator chose Y. Here is why. Here is what they lost. Here is what they gained.

The desk also shows you the contested terms. Some words are not settled. Scholars disagree about what the original word means. The translation desk names the disagreement. It does not pick a side. It shows you both positions and the evidence behind each.

The desk also shows you semantic change. Words change meaning over time. The word "soul" in 1600 means something different from the word "soul" in 2025. The translation desk tracks the change. It tells you: in this period, this word meant this. In this later period, it meant that. The change is not a mistake. It is a fact.

The desk also shows you the translator. Not just the name. The translator's position. Were they a specialist in the language? Were they working from the original or from a previous translation? Did they have access to the full text or only fragments? The translator's position changes the translation. A specialist working from the original is not the same as a generalist working from a previous translation.

The desk also shows you the cultural protocol. Some words are not just words. They carry cultural weight. They are sacred. They are sensitive. They belong to a community. The translation desk records the protocol. It tells you: this word is sacred to this community. This word is sensitive. This word requires attribution. The protocol is not a decoration. It is a rule.

The sources in this collection come from trusted places. The [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) provides access to Mesopotamian diagnostic tablets in Akkadian. The [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection) holds cuneiform tablets from Mesopotamia. The [National Library of Medicine](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html) holds the Edwin Smith Papyrus in hieratic script. The [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/) holds translated editions of historical medical texts.

These are not blogs. These are institutions that have been checking and preserving these sources for centuries. The translation desk tells you which institution holds which source, who translated it, and what the translator chose.

You do not have to trust the translation. You have to trust the translation desk. The desk shows you the choice. You can disagree with the choice. You can look at the original yourself. That is the point.

The translation desk is not a decoration. It is the foundation. Without it, the word is a guess. With it, the word is a claim with a trail. You can follow the trail. You can check the work. You can disagree with the translation and still trust the source.

That is the translation desk. Not a dictionary. A desk. You can sit down and look at the choices.
