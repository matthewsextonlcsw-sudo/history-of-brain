# E05 — Retrospective Label Lab

## Draft

You are reading about a person from two thousand years ago. The person had a label. The label was not "schizophrenia." The label was something else. A word in a language you do not speak. A category that did not exist in your world.

The retrospective label lab asks: can you compare that person to a modern label? When does the comparison help? When does it rewrite them?

Here is the problem. Modern labels are not the same as historical labels. "Schizophrenia" is a modern label. It was created in 1911 by a Swiss psychiatrist named Eugen Bleuler. Before 1911, the label did not exist. The person from two thousand years ago did not have schizophrenia. They had a label that meant something different in their world.

The comparison helps when you are careful. It helps when you say: "This person's experience is similar to what we now call X." Not "This person had X." The similarity is real. The identity is not.

The comparison rewrites when you are not careful. It rewrites when you say: "This person had schizophrenia." That is not a comparison. That is a rewrite. You are taking a modern label and putting it on a historical person. You are changing the person. You are making them into someone they were not.

The lab shows you the difference. It shows you: here is the historical label. Here is the modern label. Here is the similarity. Here is the difference. Here is what the comparison can tell you. Here is what it cannot.

The lab also shows you the anachronism risk. Anachronism is when you put a modern idea in a historical context. The risk is high when you use a modern label for a historical person. The risk is low when you use the historical label and explain the similarity. The lab records the risk. It tells you: this claim has a high anachronism risk. This claim has a low anachronism risk.

The lab also shows you the current evidence note. When you compare a historical belief with current knowledge, you need a note. The note says: here is what we know now. Here is what they believed then. Here is the difference. The note is not a correction. It is a comparison.

The sources in this collection come from trusted places. The [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/) holds medical history materials, including patient records and treatment descriptions. The [UK National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/mental-health/) holds mental health patient files.

These are not blogs. These are institutions that have been checking and preserving these sources for centuries. The retrospective label lab tells you which institution holds which source, and what the source actually says.

You do not have to trust the comparison. You have to trust the lab. The lab shows you the difference between a comparison and a rewrite. You can disagree with the comparison. You can look at the source yourself. That is the point.

The retrospective label lab is not a decoration. It is the foundation. Without it, the comparison is a guess. With it, the comparison is a claim with a trail. You can follow the trail. You can check the work. You can disagree with the comparison and still trust the source.

That is the retrospective label lab. Not a label. A lab. You can run it on any comparison.
