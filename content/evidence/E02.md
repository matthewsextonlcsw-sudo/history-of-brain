## Draft

You are reading a page about the mind across four thousand years. Every claim on that page carries a label. Not a grade. Not a ranking. A label — a way to tell you what the evidence actually does, and what it cannot do.

The label is not about the source. It is about the claim. A museum catalog entry can be L1 (direct, authoritative evidence) for the claim "this tablet exists and says these words," and L5 (discovery lead only) for the claim "this tablet proves that Mesopotamians believed in a specific mental illness." The source is the same. The claim is different. The label is different.

**Documented.** Appropriate direct evidence establishes the bounded proposition. You state it directly while preserving scope. Example: "The Edwin Smith Papyrus, dated to approximately 1200 BCE, describes the brain as soft and vulnerable." That is documented. The papyrus exists. The words are there. The date is established by Egyptologists. You do not need to say "may" or "could." You state it.

**Strongly supported.** Multiple strong, sufficiently independent sources support it, or a strong scholarly consensus does. You say "evidence indicates" or "historians broadly agree." Example: "Historians broadly agree that trepanation was practiced across multiple prehistoric cultures." That is strongly supported. Multiple independent excavation reports from different sites, different periods, different regions all show the same thing. But it is not documented in the way a specific tablet is documented — it is a pattern across many sources.

**Plausible.** Evidence permits the interpretation but does not establish it. You say "may," "could," "one interpretation is," and you name the missing proof. Example: "One interpretation is that the healed trepanation at this site suggests the community cared for the patient. The missing proof is direct evidence of that care — no record of who performed the procedure, why, or what happened afterward." That is plausible. The interpretation is reasonable. The evidence permits it. But it does not establish it.

**Disputed.** Credible experts materially disagree. You name the question, the serious positions, the evidence behind each, and the uncertainty. Example: "Historians disagree about whether the heart or the brain was considered the seat of the mind in ancient Egypt. One position, based on the Book of the Dead and the weighing-of-the-heart ceremony, holds that the heart was the seat of consciousness. Another position, based on surgical texts that describe the brain, holds that the brain was recognized as a physical organ with specific properties. The evidence does not resolve the question." That is disputed. Both positions are serious. Both have evidence. The question is open.

**Unknown.** Available evidence cannot responsibly answer. You say what is unknown and why. You do not fill the gap narratively. Example: "We do not know what the person who underwent trepanation at this site experienced during or after the procedure. No record of their subjective experience survives. The silence is not absence of experience — it is the result of a specific, traceable gap in the record." That is unknown. You do not invent an answer. You name the gap.

The labels are not a ladder. Documented is not "better" than Unknown. Unknown is not a failure. It is the honest answer when the evidence cannot responsibly answer the question. The label tells you what the evidence does — and what it cannot do.

The labels can move. A claim that is Plausible today can become Strongly supported tomorrow when a new source is opened. A claim that is Documented can become Disputed when a new interpretation challenges the reading. Every change requires a dated note. The label is not a verdict. It is a state — and states change.

The source rating (L1–L5) and the claim-fit grade (A–D) are separate from the evidence label. The source rating evaluates the input — what kind of accountability and access to evidence the source has. The claim-fit grade evaluates how directly that source supports that specific claim. The evidence label evaluates the claim itself — what the evidence actually establishes, supports, permits, disputes, or cannot answer.

Three webpages repeating one unsourced statement are one weak chain, not three sources. A claim cannot become "strongly supported" by counting multiple members of one independence group. The independence rule is the brake on the label. It prevents the label from becoming a popularity contest.

You are not reading a bibliography. You are reading a claim with a label. The label tells you what the evidence does. The source tells you where the evidence comes from. The claim tells you what the project is asserting. All three are separate. All three are inspectable.
