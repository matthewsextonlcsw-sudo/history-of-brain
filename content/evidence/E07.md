## Draft

You are about to read a page about a person. The person is in an archive. The archive has rules. The rules are not just legal. They are ethical.

The archive and ethics room shows you where the line is.

**Privacy.** The person in the archive is a real person. They have a name. They have a story. They have a right to privacy. The archive has rules about who can see the person's story. The rules are legal. But they are also ethical. The legal rule says: you can see the file after 50 years. The ethical rule says: you should think about whether seeing the file is right.

**Human remains.** The person in the archive may be dead. Their remains may be in a museum. Their remains may be in a collection. The museum has rules about who can see the remains. The rules are legal. But they are also ethical. The legal rule says: you can see the remains. The ethical rule says: you should think about whether seeing the remains is right.

**Patient images.** The person in the archive may have an image. The image may be a photograph. The image may be a drawing. The image may be a scan. The archive has rules about who can see the image. The rules are legal. But they are also ethical. The legal rule says: you can see the image. The ethical rule says: you should think about whether seeing the image is right.

**Colonial records.** The person in the archive may be from a colony. The records may have been made by the colonizer. The records may not tell the person's story. The records may tell the colonizer's story. The archive has rules about who can see the records. The rules are legal. But they are also ethical. The legal rule says: you can see the records. The ethical rule says: you should think about whose story the records tell.

**Indigenous cultural authority.** The person in the archive may be from an Indigenous community. The community may have authority over the person's story. The community may have rules about who can see the story. The rules are not just legal. They are cultural. The legal rule says: you can see the story. The cultural rule says: you should ask the community first.

**Legal access versus ethical access.** The legal rule says: you can see the file. The ethical rule says: you should think about whether seeing the file is right. The two rules are not the same. The legal rule is a floor. The ethical rule is a ceiling. You can see the file because the law allows it. But you should think about whether seeing the file is right.

The sources in this collection come from trusted places. The [UK National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/mental-health/) holds mental health patient files. The [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/) holds medical history materials, including patient records and treatment descriptions.

These are not blogs. These are institutions that have been checking and preserving these sources for centuries. The archive and ethics room tells you which institution holds which source, and what the rules are.

You do not have to trust the archive. You have to trust the room. The room shows you the line. You can disagree with the line. You can look at the source yourself. That is the point.

The archive and ethics room is not a decoration. It is the foundation. Without it, the access is a guess. With it, the access is a claim with a trail. You can follow the trail. You can check the work. You can disagree with the access and still trust the source.

That is the archive and ethics room. Not a list. A room. You can walk in and see the line.
