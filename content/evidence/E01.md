## Draft

You are about to read a page about what people thought about the mind two thousand years ago. Before you read it, you should know where the facts came from. Not "a study says." Not "experts believe." A specific place. A specific document. A specific date.

That is the source room.

The source room is not a bibliography. A bibliography is a list of books. The source room is a map. It shows you exactly where each fact came from, who opened the source, when they opened it, and what they found.

Here is how it works. Every page in this collection has a source room. The source room has shelves. Each shelf holds a source. Each source has a label. The label tells you:

- **What it is.** A tablet. A papyrus. A patient file. A law. A diary. A dataset.
- **Who made it.** A scribe. A doctor. A family. A government. A researcher.
- **When it was made.** 1200 BCE. 1850. 2020.
- **Where it is.** The British Museum. The National Library of Medicine. The UK National Archives. The Wellcome Collection.
- **Who opened it.** The researcher who actually looked at it. Not a search result. Not a snippet. A person who opened the source and checked it.
- **When they opened it.** The date. Not "recently." A specific date.
- **What they found.** The exact page, folio, object number, or passage. Not "the source says." The specific words.

The source room has rules. The first rule: never cite a source that has not been opened and checked. A search result is not a source. A snippet is not a source. A citation list is not a source. A person has to open the source and check it.

The second rule: a link is access, not proof. A link tells you where to go. It does not tell you what you will find. The source room records what was checked and when.

The third rule: the source room is organized by page. Not by author. Not by date. By page. Each page has its own source room. The source room for this page is here. The source room for the next page is there. You can walk from one to the other.

The sources in this collection come from trusted places. The [National Library of Medicine](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html) holds the Edwin Smith Papyrus, one of the oldest surgical texts in the world. The [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection) holds cuneiform tablets from Mesopotamia. The [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/) holds medical history materials. The [UK National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/mental-health/) holds mental health patient files. The [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) provides access to Mesopotamian diagnostic tablets.

These are not blogs. These are not SEO pages. These are institutions that have been checking and preserving these sources for centuries. The source room tells you which institution holds which source, and what the researcher found when they opened it.

You do not have to trust the page. You have to trust the source room. The source room shows you where each fact came from. You can go there yourself. You can open the source and check it. That is the point.

The source room is not a decoration. It is the foundation. Without it, the page is a guess. With it, the page is a claim with a trail. You can follow the trail. You can check the work. You can disagree with the interpretation and still trust the source.

That is the source room. Not a bibliography. A room. You can walk in.
