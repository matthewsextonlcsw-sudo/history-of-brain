## Draft

A word is not a label. It is a place, a date, a community, and a set of assumptions about what counts as a problem. When you read *melancholia* in a 2nd-century-BCE Greek text, you are not reading "depression." You are reading a specific theory of black bile, cold-dry temperament, and social withdrawal that had its own medical logic, its own treatments, and its own boundaries. The word traveled. It changed. It means different things in different hands.

This is the method of the [terminology bridge](H02.md), and it runs in one direction only: from the term to its setting, never from the term to a modern category. The [evidence model's historical-label protocol](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/sources/EVIDENCE-MODEL.md) is blunt about this: describe the person's documented words, behavior, context, and period categories, and do not assign a modern label as a settled fact. If scholars debate a retrospective label, the debate — not the label — is the claim.

Here is a selection of terms that the collection tracks, each bounded by place and date:

**Greek *mania* (μανία), c. 5th century BCE onward.** Frenzy, divine possession, social disruption. Not a single clinical entity. In the Hippocratic corpus it sits beside naturalizing arguments about the brain; in Asclepian healing it sits beside ritual care. The [backbone's B2.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) pairs it with competing philosophies rather than collapsing it into one story.

**Greek *melancholia* (μελαγχολία), c. 5th century BCE onward.** Black bile, cold-dry temperament, sadness, withdrawal. In Hippocratic and later Galenic medicine it is a humoral condition with specific treatments. In later periods it shifts toward a moral and aesthetic category — the "melancholic" as a type, a temperament, a creative disposition. The [backbone's B4.5 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) tracks this semantic change without turning it into a timeless depression label.

**South Asian *unmada* (उन्मत्त), Vedic through Buddhist contexts.** Derangement, social disruption, loss of self-control. The term appears across Vedic, Ayurvedic, and Buddhist traditions, and its meaning shifts with the tradition. The [backbone's B2.3 ruling](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is explicit: never equate *unmada* with a current label. It is a category of social and spiritual disruption, not a clinical entity.

**South Asian *manas* (मनस्), Vedic through Buddhist contexts.** Mind, consciousness, inner faculty. A layered term that covers cognition, emotion, and spiritual faculty across different traditions. It is not a single "mind" that maps onto a modern organ. The [backbone's B2.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) distinguishes Vedic and Upaniṣadic arguments, changing Ayurvedic corpora, and Buddhist schools rather than inventing a single "Indian psychology."

**Chinese *xin* (心), premodern through early modern.** Heart-mind. A term that covers emotion, conduct, and order across named schools. The [backbone's B2.4 ruling](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md): compare named schools and changing uses of *qing* rather than inventing a single Chinese psychology. *Xin* is not "the mind" in the modern sense. It is a heart-mind that covers feeling, intention, and social order.

**Chinese *dian* (癲) and *kuang* (狂), premodern.** Madness terms that trace wind, phlegm-fire, emotional disturbance, drugs, needling, talking, and ritual. The [backbone's B2.5 ruling](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md): do not call the whole past "TCM." These are specific terms with specific treatments and specific contexts, not a monolithic "traditional Chinese medicine" category.

**Late-antique *acedia*, c. 3rd–8th centuries CE.** Boredom, listlessness, spiritual laziness. In ascetic and monastic contexts it is a spiritual danger — a failure of attention, a turning away from God. In later periods it shifts toward a psychological and moral category. The [backbone's B3.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) pairs it with Augustine, ascetic disciplines, and natural and demonic explanations. The [B4.5 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) tracks its semantic change without turning it into a timeless depression label.

The pattern is consistent: a term is a place, a date, and a set of assumptions. It is not a label. It is not a category that maps cleanly onto a modern term. It is a word that traveled, changed, and meant different things in different hands. The collection's job is to hold each term in its setting — to show you what it meant then, in that place, in that community — and to keep the modern category separate. That is the [evidence ladder](H03.md) applied to language: the word supports a claim about what was said, not about what a person felt. And that is the [then-and-now distinction](H05.md) applied to vocabulary: the term's meaning then is a different question from its efficacy or safety now.
