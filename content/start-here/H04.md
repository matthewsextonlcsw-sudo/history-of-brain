## Draft

Ask first: are these really patient stories? Sometimes they are — but almost never in the raw form we imagine. What survives is rarely a voice; it is a chain of hands, and the honest work is to trace who shaped it, who selected it, and who was never asked.

The backbone's rule is blunt and we keep it prominent: **no direct voice survives. Say so.** The missing are predictable — non-literate people, children, enslaved people, rural households, and most women reach us through elite or institutional records, if at all. When the archive is silent, name the silence. Do not fill it with our categories.

The Pratt dossier is the cleanest modern example of the chain. Ann Pratt's Jamaica narrative of 1859 exists in the record as a printed summary ordered at the governor's direction and a five-page first-person report — but nearly everything else passed through the solicitor and supporters who gathered it. Her elicited statement of July 1860 was taken down by Trench, read back to her, corrected, signed, and closed with a refusal to alter its substance: dictation, verification, and constraint in a single documented moment. Trench called her an "ignorant woman," selected the supporting witnesses, and still conceded servants "sometimes used more roughness and severity than desirable" — hostile framing and partial concession inside the same file. And no family-authored text survives: every family trace is mediated. The family's silence is itself evidence. Read as a chain, not as testimony, the dossier answers a different question — who gets believed, and on what machinery — than the one its title implies.

St. Thomas shows the same shape across two centuries earlier. Six selected letters from 1738–1753 preserve layered voice: Peter's 1738 letter is self-written as Martin's German note attests; the 1739 king petition was composed by Domingo Gesoe writing for a constituency of 650-plus; Damma's letter survives in three language layers — an African-language original, a Creole rendering, an English translation — which a 2025 collaboration has identified as Gbe, overturning the 1996 edition's "unidentified" label; and Cornelius's 1753 letter, with its devotional blood-and-wound language, reaches us through the Herrnhut/Danish transcription chain. Even the best letter preserves a fraction of a speech community: Van Rossem reports that most 1741 correspondents dictated, and Bøgh cautions that the 1753 Seidel sample cannot represent the whole enslaved population.

Notice what this does to the reading. The words "very sad" and "ill from sadness" in a 1753 letter are devotional, translated, relational, and archival objects — they carry that setting, and converting them into any modern category erases exactly what they were. The correction runs both ways: the archive's silence about most people is as real as the voices it keeps, and the mediation of the voices it keeps is as much a part of the evidence as the words themselves. Who speaks, who translates, who selects, who edits, who prints, who files — the archive is all of that, and the patient story is the last link, not the first.
