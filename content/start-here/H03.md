# H03 — How Do We Know What People Felt in the Past?

> *A healed skull can document an intervention and perhaps survival, but it cannot by itself
> document a psychiatric purpose, fear, belief, or gratitude. This page is the method of the
> whole collection in miniature: the evidence ladder from bone to claim.*

## Draft

You cannot walk into a museum and read a mind. You can, however, learn to read the
ladder by which evidence climbs from bone to claim — and to notice where the rungs stop.

Start with the object. A skull with a hole in it and bone grown back over the edge is
direct evidence of an intervention and, if the healing is complete, of survival. That is the
strongest rung: it is observed on the object itself. But the next rung — *why* the hole was
made — is not on the bone. Trepanation holes have been read as psychiatric, religious,
ritual, and practical acts, and the bone does not decide among them. The inference from bone
to motive is a separate, weaker step, and it has to be argued from context: the other bones
in the grave, the tools nearby, the written records, the cultural setting. This is the whole
method of the collection in miniature.

The same discipline applies as the ladder rises. A burial is evidence of how a community
treated the dead, but it cannot document what the dead person felt. A written text is
evidence of what was written, not of the full inner life of the writer. An ancient
first-person poem is a literary voice with a genre, a scribal setting, and a long history of
reconstruction — reading the narrator as a modern "patient" would collapse those layers.
A retained letter is mediated self-writing: what survives is the published transcription,
not the manuscript's handwriting, marginalia, or physical order. A published study can
preserve an affected person's authorship while leaving the original objects unopened, and a
notebook is not the same evidence as an unfinished novel — authored self-report, revision,
posthumous editing, family-to-archive provenance, and later scholarship are separate layers.

The highest rungs are testimony and institutional records, and they come with their own
limits. Institutional records are strong for what they report and weak for what they omit.
Medieval royal competency categories are normative property-and-custody text: they show how
the law was worded, not that it was implemented, or universally, or as described. The
archive structurally underrepresents people outside the property-and-court jurisdiction.
Testimony, too, arrives mediated — through dictation, translation, editing, and selection.

The point of the ladder is not to make you hopeless about the past. It is to make your
claims match the strength of your evidence. Bone supports a claim about intervention and
survival. Burial supports a claim about treatment of the dead. A text supports a claim about
what was written. A record supports a claim about what was reported. And inference — the
step from any of these to a claim about what a person felt, feared, believed, or meant —
is always a step you take yourself, in the open, with the rungs visible.
