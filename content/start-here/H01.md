# H01 — The Two-Minute Global History of Mental Health

> *The big story, in one page. Read this first; everything else is a door off it.*

## Research

- The **Edwin Smith Papyrus** (~17th century BCE, based on earlier material) is a treatise on trauma surgery of 48 cases, each laid out as injury → diagnosis → prognosis → treatment; it rarely invokes the supernatural — an early, rational clinical register rather than "Egyptian neuroscience." [Source: National Library of Medicine](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html)
- The **Ludlul bēl nēmeqi** ("The Poem of the Righteous Sufferer"), a Babylonian literary lament, contains one of the oldest first-person voices of mental anguish, giving suffering a narrator rather than only a diagnosis. [Source: Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/2)
- Ancient Mesopotamian diagnostic-prognostic tablets already paired divine and social relations with material and ritual remedies — care was never only "magic" or only "medicine." [Source: Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/2)
- Hippocrates' **On the Sacred Disease** reframes epilepsy as a natural brain disorder, yet the tradition never escapes the entanglement of mind, spirit, and community — a pattern that recurs across every later band. [Source: classics.mit.edu](https://classics.mit.edu/Hippocrates/sacred.html)
- The **Friendship Bench** project (Zimbabwe, begun 2006) delivers structured collaborative care for depression by trained lay health workers in primary care; a 2010–2014 case study reports ~505 patients seen per year and high acceptability — community-embedded care, not a technology story. [Source: Abas et al., Int J Ment Health Syst 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4865009/)
- The **1990 Caracas Declaration** (WHO) names the rights to mental health care and to protection from abusive treatment as central — the moment "care" and "control" are joined in the same sentence. [Source: WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241580520)
- The **Te Whare Tapa Whā** Māori model places tūpua (spiritual) and whānau (family) alongside taha tinana (physical) and taha hinengaro (mental) health — a whole-person model, not a brain in a box. [Source: NZ Health](https://www.health.govt.nz/your-health/services-and-support/mental-health-and-addictions/mental-health/mental-health-services/mental-health-services-overview/mental-health-models/)

## Draft

Mental health is older than the word "mind." The oldest documents in this history are not about brains at all: a 17th-century-BCE Egyptian scroll, the Edwin Smith Papyrus, walks through forty-eight cases of wounds and trauma with a calm, clinical formula — injury, diagnosis, prognosis, treatment — and reaches almost never for magic. A Babylonian lament, the Ludlul bēl nēmeqi, gives suffering a first-person voice, a sufferer who speaks, long before psychology existed to name his distress. Across these early bands the same pattern repeats: care is never only "magic" or only "medicine," never only the individual or only the community. It is woven through family petitions, divine relations, and social standing.

Greek and Roman thought reframes illness as natural — epilepsy becomes a sacred disease of the brain rather than a curse — but the whole person stays entangled with spirit and society. Medieval Islamic physicians build hospitals, the bimaristans, that treat the sick in the open, and the great translation networks of Baghdad and Toledo move ideas between Arabic, Latin, and Greek. In Africa, the Americas, and Asia, care continues through elders, lay workers, and communal practice. When colonial medicine arrives it brings control as much as care — asylums and asylums' successors impose order on bodies and souls, and the twentieth century's grand experiments swing between the two poles.

The modern turn is not a clean break. The 1990 Caracas Declaration joins the right to mental health care with the right to protection from abuse. The Friendship Bench in Zimbabwe shows community-embedded care working at scale — trained lay workers, a wooden bench, problem-solving therapy delivered in primary care. Te Whare Tapa Whā treats the whole person: body, mind, family, and spirit. The big story, then, is not progress from ignorance to science. It is a long, uneven negotiation between care and control, between the individual and the community, between the brain and the whole person — and it is still being written. Every band on the [chronological backbone](#chronological-backbone) holds its own chapter of that story, from the trauma cases of the Edwin Smith band down to today's community and whole-person models. Read it first, then walk any single band — the [ancient bands](#T00), the [medieval translation networks](#T01), the [colonial and asylum bands](#T02), the [twentieth-century control band](#T03), or the [contemporary care band](#T04) — and you will find this same story, told in a different voice.
