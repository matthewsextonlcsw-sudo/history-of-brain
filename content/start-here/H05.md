# H05 — Then and Now Are Different Questions

> *Does old mean effective—or wrong? A healed skull documents an intervention, not an outcome. A historical treatment's meaning is not its current efficacy. This page separates the two questions the collection keeps open.*

## Draft

The first question a reader asks about an old treatment is usually the wrong one. "Did it work?" is a question about now, not then. A healed skull can tell you that someone opened a skull and survived. It cannot tell you whether the opening helped, whether the person felt better, whether the community believed it would, or whether the bone's owner would have recognized the procedure as medicine at all. The intervention is documented. The outcome is not — and that gap is the whole method of this collection in miniature.

The hinge runs in two directions, and the second one is easier to miss. The familiar direction is projecting a current label onto a historical person. A 1753 letter that says "ill from sadness" is a devotional, translated, relational, and archival object. Converting it into "depression" erases exactly what it was — the language, the setting, the chain of hands that carried it. The [historical-label protocol](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/sources/EVIDENCE-MODEL.md) is blunt about this: describe the person's documented words, behavior, context, and period categories, and do not assign a modern label as a settled fact. If scholars debate a retrospective label, the debate — not the label — is the claim.

The less familiar direction is judging a historical treatment by current efficacy standards and calling the judgment a historical finding. Bloodletting did not work for most of the conditions it was applied to — that is a current-evidence judgment, and it is a true one. But it is not a historical explanation. The historical question is different: what did the practitioner believe, what did the patient experience, what did the community reward, and what evidence was available at the time? Those are questions about then, and they have answers that current efficacy does not settle. A treatment can be wrong by today's evidence and still be intelligible — even reasonable — in its own context. The [backbone's trepanation node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) makes the same point from the other end: an opening in a skull is not a psychiatric label, and the bone does not decide among the psychiatric, religious, ritual, and practical readings that have been pressed onto it.

The collection keeps the two questions separate by design. The [evidence model](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/sources/EVIDENCE-MODEL.md) requires a `current_evidence_note` whenever a historical belief is compared with current knowledge, and the current-science protocol insists on recent synthesis appropriate to the question — distinguishing mechanistic evidence, association, clinical outcome, expert consensus, and policy rather than collapsing them. The [drift alarms](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/PROJECT-BRAIN.md) name the failure modes: using present-day diagnoses as labels for historical people without strong justification, and confusing an explanatory analogy with evidence.

So when you read a page in this collection, notice which question it is answering. "What did this mean, and what did people do, and why?" is the historical question. "Does this work now, and is it safe now?" is the current question. The collection holds both open, and it will tell you when the evidence for one runs out. A healed skull documents an intervention. It does not document an outcome. Moral treatment's records show the design and the rules. They do not show the patient's experience or the result. The bone, the letter, the register — each answers a bounded question, and the collection's job is to keep the questions from collapsing into each other. That is also the job of the [terminology bridge](H02.md) and the [evidence ladder](H03.md): to hold the past in its own terms long enough to see what it actually says.
