## Draft

A lived-experience page is a window. The window shows what the record says about a specific experience. The window does not show what the record does not say.

The lived-experience zone is where you go when you want to know whose voice survives. The zone has six streams. Each stream is a window. It shows what the record says about a specific kind of voice, and it names what the record does not show.

Here is how to enter the lived-experience zone. Pick a stream. Open it. Read the Research section. Check each source. Then read the Draft. Check the voice label. Notice where the person's own words are missing.

The hardest rule in this zone is the voice rule. The page must label whose voice it is. There are four labels: direct, near-direct, reported, and institutional. A direct voice is the person's own words. An institutional voice is a doctor's, a clerk's, or a judge's words. The National Archives guide says most patient files have been destroyed. That means the direct voice is often gone. What survives is the institutional voice: the admission register, the quarterly return, the correspondence with the poor law union.

A lived-experience page tells you which voice you are reading. It does not let you mistake the doctor's words for the person's words. It names the gap.

One more thing: the streams are not the same as the time-bands. A stream is about a kind of voice. A time-band is about a period. The "People speaking for themselves" stream is about direct voice. The 19th-century time-band is about a period. They are different tools for different jobs. You pick the tool that matches your question.

One more thing: the streams connect. A lived-experience page links to a time-band page. A time-band page links to a place page. A place page links to a route page. That is the collection in motion. You can follow the links and walk through the whole collection.

That is how you enter the lived-experience zone: pick a stream, check the voice label, and notice where the person's own words are missing.
