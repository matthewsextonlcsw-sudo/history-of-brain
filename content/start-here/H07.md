## Draft

The lived-experience rule is the rule that says the record is limited. The record does not show what the person felt. The record does not show what the person thought. The record does not show what the person wanted.

The rule has three parts. First, the record is the doctor's words, not the person's words. The doctor wrote down what he saw. He wrote down what he did. He did not write down what the person felt. Second, the record is administrative, not personal. The National Archives guide says the records "relate mainly to the administration of the institutions." The admission register gives you the name, the age, the date. It does not give you the feeling. Third, the record is incomplete. Most patient files have been destroyed. The direct voice is often gone.

Here is what the rule does not say. It does not say the person did not feel anything. It does not say the person's experience was unimportant. It says the record does not show it. That is a different claim. The record is silent. The silence is a fact. The silence is not the same as absence.

The Wellcome Collection has photographs. The photographs show what the room looked like. The photographs do not show what the person felt. The photographs are a fact. The fact is limited. The rule names the limit.

The rule is not a limitation. It is a standard. It tells you what the record can and cannot do. It tells you where the evidence stops. It tells you where the guess begins. A good page follows the rule. It names the gap. It does not fill it with invention.

That is the lived-experience rule: the record is what it is, and the page names what it does not show.
