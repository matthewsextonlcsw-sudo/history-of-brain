## Draft

The popular story is almost always too neat. It has a hero, a discovery, a before and an after, and a moral. The evidence is messier. It has networks, conflicts, silences, and outcomes that no one intended. This page checks the recurring myths against the record and shows you what the evidence actually says.

**Myth: "Trepanation was a psychiatric treatment."** The bone does not decide. A healed skull documents an intervention and survival. It does not document a psychiatric purpose, a fear, a belief, or a gratitude. The [backbone's B0.2 ruling](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: an opening in a skull is not a psychiatric label. The inference from bone to motive is a separate, weaker step, and it has to be argued from context — the other bones in the grave, the tools nearby, the written records, the cultural setting. The myth collapses that step. The evidence keeps it open.

**Myth: "Ancient people were all superstitious."** The medicine-versus-magic ladder is a false binary. The [backbone's B1 ruling](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is explicit: care was never only "magic" or only "medicine." The Edwin Smith Papyrus walks through forty-eight trauma cases with a calm, clinical formula and reaches almost never for magic. The Mesopotamian diagnostic-prognostic tablets pair divine and social relations with material and ritual remedies. The myth flattens a plural, entangled record into a single story of ignorance. The evidence shows a world where the natural and the sacred were woven together, not opposed.

**Myth: "The bimaristan was the first psychiatric hospital."** The [backbone's B3.4 ruling](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) rejects unsupported "first psychiatric hospital" claims. The bimaristan was a general hospital — it treated the sick in the open, with endowments, staffing, and household care. It was not a psychiatric institution. The myth confuses a general hospital with a specialized one. The evidence keeps the distinction.

**Myth: "Moral treatment cured madness."** The [backbone's B5.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) asks the question the myth skips: did moral treatment feel moral to patients? The records show the design and the rules — the regimen, the environment, the labor, the restraint. They do not show the patient's experience or the outcome. Institutional records are strong for what they report and weak for what they omit. The myth reads the design as the outcome. The evidence keeps the two separate.

**Myth: "Deinstitutionalization was a success."** The [backbone's B8.2 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: deinstitutionalization was not one policy or one outcome. It was a set of policies, in different countries, with different community services, different housing, different incarceration rates, different family burdens, and different abandonment. The myth collapses a complex, uneven process into a single story of success or failure. The evidence keeps the variation visible.

**Myth: "Freud invented psychotherapy."** The [backbone's B6.7 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is explicit: psychoanalysis is made by a network. Freud belongs in a story of patients, correspondents, critics, women analysts, colonial adaptations, and rival theories. The myth elevates one person into a sole inventor. The evidence shows a network of people, ideas, and institutions.

**Myth: "Psychiatry discovered the brain."** The [backbone's B4.6 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: Vesalius, Burton, and Descartes are changes within European debates, not the moment humanity discovered the brain. The brain was known long before psychiatry. The myth confuses a change in emphasis with a discovery. The evidence keeps the timeline honest.

The pattern is consistent: the popular story is too neat. It has a hero, a discovery, a before and an after. The evidence is messier. It has networks, conflicts, silences, and outcomes that no one intended. The [backbone's acceptance check](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt about this: "First," "discovery," and retrospective label require explicit review. The collection's job is to keep the myths visible — to show you what the popular story says, and then to show you what the evidence actually says. That is the [evidence ladder](H03.md) applied to popular narratives: the story supports a claim about what was said, not about what happened. And that is the [then-and-now distinction](H05.md) applied to history: the popular story's meaning then is a different question from its accuracy now.
