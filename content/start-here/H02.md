# H02 — Before "Mental Health": How to Read Old Words Fairly

> *Did people have mental illness before the term existed? Short answer: yes — but not in our terms. Read the old words in their own grammar, or you will misread the people.*

## Research

- Medieval English miracle collections preserve a **family of non-interchangeable terms** — `mente captus`, `amentia`, `insania`, `furor`, `plena daemonio`, `non compos mentis`, `phrenesis`, `melancholia`, `timor`, `tristitia` — each carrying its own bodily, legal, sacred, and moral weight. The English word "madness" collapses them all. [Source: R15 Medieval England corpus analysis](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/R15-EUROPE-MEDIEVAL/LIVED-EXPERIENCE-AND-ARCHIVE.md)
- A 1285 royal administrative order for Andrew le Merk distinguishes **being "an idiot from birth"** from other legal categories of incapacity — a legal, property-jurisdiction task, not a modern intellectual-disability category. [Source: Close Roll calendar entry, printed Statutes of the Realm](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/R15-EUROPE-MEDIEVAL/FINDINGS.md)
- Constantine the African's **De melancholia** (late eleventh century, in a 1536 Latin witness) opens by distinguishing hypochondriac/stomach and brain-centered species, and defines sadness as loss of a loved thing and fear as expectation of harm — an early, bounded emotional vocabulary with its own theory of causes. [Source: UB Tübingen digital edition](https://opendigi.ub.uni-tuebingen.de/opendigi/Ja1_fol_1#p=301)
- **Acedia** (the "noonday demon") was a medieval monastic sin of spiritual sloth, not a depression label; over centuries it feeds a semantic chain that ends in **melancholy** and eventually **depression** — track the words, do not turn the chain into one timeless disease. [Source: CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md B4.5](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md)
- **Premodern Chinese madness was medically plural**: `dian`, `kuang`, wind, phlegm-fire, emotional disturbance, drugs, needling, talking, and ritual each named different routes into and out of distress — do not flatten the whole past into one "TCM" category. [Source: CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md B2.5](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md)
- **Hildegard of Bingen's** twelfth-century Scivias describes her own suffering in theological-literary categories — phrenesis, vigilans, "pure mind," interior eyes and ears. Modern migraine-aura readings of her are a twentieth-century retrospective label, not her own grammar. [Source: R15 claim map, SRC-024005–006](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/research/era-packets/R15-EUROPE-MEDIEVAL/CLAIM-MAP.md)
- **Shell shock was not simply PTSD by another name.** It carried bodily, psychological, moral, disciplinary, pension, gendered, and national interpretations at once — reading it as a timeless anxiety disorder erases most of what it meant to the people who lived it. [Source: CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md B7.1](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md)
- A Mexican self-publication from c.1928, **El sueño de un loco realizado** by Alberto Nicolat, keeps its historical labels (`loco`, `anómalo`, `paranoia`, `delirio`) in period context — the author's own chosen words are the archive-silence-preserving voice, not a modern clinical transcription. [Source: UNAM Instituto de Investigaciones Históricas](https://saludmental.historicas.unam.mx/sites/default/files/2023-03/LIBRO-El-sueno-de-un-loco-realizado.pdf)

## Draft

Yes, people had mental suffering long before anyone said "mental illness." But the old words do not mean what we want them to mean, and reading them as if they did is one of the easiest ways to misread history.

Start with the smallness of the old vocabularies. In a twelfth-century English miracle collection, a person might be `menti captus`, `amentia`, `furor`, or `non compos mentis` — and each of those terms pointed somewhere different: a bodily condition, a legal incapacity, a sacred affliction, a punishment. A 1285 royal order treats someone as "an idiot from birth" inside a property-and-custody framework that has nothing to do with our modern categories of intellectual disability. The same English word, "madness," hides all of this. If you read "madness" as one thing, you will invent a uniformity that was never there.

The same discipline applies to big concepts. "Melancholy" was not depression's ancestor in any simple sense. It was a humoral idea, a monastic sin (acedia), a literary figure, and a legal label at different moments — track the word, watch how it changes, and do not freeze it into a single disease. The same holds for "nostalgia" as a once-fatal homesickness, or "hysteria" as a century-long container for women's bodies and nerves. Words are not windows onto timeless illnesses; they are the handles people of their time used to grip suffering.

And watch the modern temptation to retro-diagnose. Reading Hildegard of Bingen's Scivias as "migraine" imports a twentieth-century neurology into a twelfth-century theological-literary grammar. Reading shell shock as "PTSD by another name" erases the bodily, disciplinary, pension, and national meanings it carried. The correct move is not to refuse the past but to translate: ask what the old word did in its own grammar, and what it could not say.

A useful rule of thumb: **when an archive is silent, say so.** When the sources preserve the suffering person's own voice — as Alberto Nicolat's 1928 self-published book preserves his — let that voice stay in its period words. When the sources are silent, name the silence rather than fill it with our categories. Read old words fairly, and you will stop finding timeless mental illnesses in the past — and start finding people who had their own, different, words for what they lived.
