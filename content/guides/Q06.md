## Draft

This is not one thing. Not one person. Not one time. Not one place. Not one record. It is a set of variations, each with its own body, its own promise, its own violence. The question "What was the body for?" looks simple. It is not.

In Mesopotamia, the [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) records diagnostic tablets that locate thought, emotion, and decision-making in the heart. The body was the site of the disturbance. The promise was restoration. The violence was the ritual itself — the binding, the confinement, the public performance.

In the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, [Hippocrates' On the Sacred Disease](https://classics.mit.edu/Hippocrates/sacred.html) reframes epilepsy as a natural brain disorder. The body was the site of the disease. The promise was cure. The violence was the regimen — the diet, the exercise, the confinement.

In China, the [backbone's B2.4 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows *xin* as heart-mind, emotion, conduct, and order. The body was the site of the disorder. The promise was order. The violence was the order itself — the confinement, the public performance, the family's authority.

In the early modern period, the [backbone's B4.6 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: Vesalius, Burton, and Descartes are changes within European debates, not the moment humanity discovered the brain. The body was the site of the machine. The promise was control. The violence was the machine itself — the anatomy, the regimen, the confinement.

In the asylum century, the [backbone's B5.4 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: the body is the site of control. Restraint, seclusion, and the patient's body are not footnotes. They are the mechanism. The promise was care. The violence was the asylum itself — the architecture, the regimen, the confinement.

In the pharmacological turn, the [backbone's B6.2 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: the body is the site of control. The drug is not neutral. It is a mechanism. The promise was control. The violence was the drug itself — the sedation, the confinement, the public performance.

In the present, the [backbone's B10.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows neurodiversity, mad pride, recovery, and disability justice: separate and overlapping movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects. The body was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

The answer depends on what you think a person is. If a person is a machine, the body is a part. If a person is a relation, the body is a network. If a person is a whole that cannot be taken apart, the question itself is the mistake.
