## Draft

A time-band page is a window. The window shows what the record says about a specific time. The window does not show what the record does not say.

The dates on a time-band page overlap. That is not a mistake. Historical change overlaps. The 19th-century band and the early-20th-century band share years. The Enlightenment band and the 19th-century band share years. A good time-band page names the overlap and tells you which facts belong to which band.

Here is how you check the dates. Take a fact from the Draft. Find the date. Check the source. Does the source give the same date? If yes, the date holds. If the source gives a different date, the page should say so.

The National Archives guide says the County Asylums Act of 1808 encouraged justices of the peace to build county lunatic asylums. The date is 1808. You can open the guide and check it. The date holds.

But the guide also says the response was slow. That means the 1808 Act did not change everything in 1808. The change took years. A good time-band page tells you that. It does not pretend the law worked the day it passed.

Here is how to handle the overlap. When two bands share years, the page should say which facts belong to which band. The 1845 Acts belong to the 19th-century band. The early-20th-century band picks up where the 19th-century band leaves off. The overlap is not a conflict. It is a handoff. A good time-band page names the handoff and tells you where one band ends and the next one begins. The overlap is a feature, not a bug.

One more check: the handoff. When two bands share years, the page should say which facts belong to which band. The 1845 Acts belong to the 19th-century band. The early-20th-century band picks up where the 19th-century band leaves off. The overlap is a handoff, not a conflict.

That is how you check the dates on a time-band page: find the date, check the source, and notice where the change took longer than the law said.
