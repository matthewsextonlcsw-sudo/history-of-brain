## Draft

This is not one thing. Not one person. Not one time. Not one place. Not one record. It is a set of variations, each with its own spirit, its own promise, its own violence. The question "What was the spirit for?" looks simple. It is not.

In Mesopotamia, the [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) records diagnostic tablets that locate thought, emotion, and decision-making in the heart. The spirit was the heart. The promise was restoration. The violence was the ritual itself — the binding, the confinement, the public performance.

In Egypt, the [UCL Letters to the Dead](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/lettersdead.html) show family petitions and heart-centered vocabularies. The spirit was the person. The promise was the person's standing in the community. The violence was the family's authority — the commitment, the discharge, the retrieval.

In the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, the [backbone's B2.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the brain, soul, regimen, and sanctuary. The spirit was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

In Mesoamerica and the Andes, the [backbone's B2.6 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) reserves a first-class lane for locally grounded concepts, material evidence, and later contact-zone sources. The warning is explicit: do not reconstruct preconquest experience from colonial accounts alone. The spirit was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

In the Buddhist world, the [backbone's B3.7 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) tracks Buddhist mind and medicine on land and sea routes: translators, monasteries, courts, ports, and changing terminology. The spirit was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

In the colonial archive, the [backbone's B4.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) reads the babaylan/katalonan through a colonial archive — and asks what the archive reveals, distorts, translates, and persecutes. The [Tremml-Werner account](https://doi.org/10.1093/pastj/gtz031) shows the archive's hand. The spirit was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

In the present, the [backbone's B10.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows neurodiversity, mad pride, recovery, and disability justice: separate and overlapping movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects. The spirit was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

The answer depends on what you think a person is. If a person is a machine, the spirit is a part. If a person is a relation, the spirit is a network. If a person is a whole that cannot be taken apart, the question itself is the mistake.
