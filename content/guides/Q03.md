## Draft

This is not one answer. It is a set of variations, each with its own practice, its own promise, its own violence. The question "What did people do when they were distressed?" looks simple. It is not.

In Mesopotamia, the [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) records diagnostic tablets that locate thought, emotion, and decision-making in the heart. The practice was ritual and exorcism. The promise was restoration. The violence was the ritual itself — the binding, the confinement, the public performance. The [backbone's B1.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows this in the earliest written records.

In Egypt, the [backbone's B1.2 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows family petitions and heart-centered vocabularies. The practice was family care and ritual. The promise was restoration of the person's standing in the community. The violence was the family's authority — the commitment, the discharge, the retrieval.

In the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, the [backbone's B2.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the brain, soul, regimen, and sanctuary. The practice was medical and ritual. The promise was cure. The violence was the regimen — the diet, the exercise, the confinement.

In South Asia, the [backbone's B2.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows layered selves, *manas*, suffering, and transformation. The practice was medical and spiritual. The promise was transformation. The violence was the transformation itself — the ritual, the confinement, the public performance.

In China, the [backbone's B2.4 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows *xin* as heart-mind, emotion, conduct, and order. The practice was medical and moral. The promise was order. The violence was the order itself — the confinement, the public performance, the family's authority.

In the Islamic world, the [backbone's B3.4 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the bimaristan: a general hospital, not a psychiatric one. The practice was medical and charitable. The promise was care. The violence was the confinement — the ward, the regimen, the public performance.

In the asylum century, the [backbone's B5.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows moral treatment: Pinel, Tuke, and their successors. The practice was medical and moral. The promise was cure. The violence was the asylum itself — the architecture, the regimen, the confinement.

In the pharmacological turn, the [backbone's B6.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows chloral hydrate to chlorpromazine. The practice was medical and chemical. The promise was control. The violence was the drug itself — the sedation, the confinement, the public performance.

In the present, the [backbone's B8.5 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the right to leave the hospital—and the right to real support. The practice is medical and social. The promise is support. The violence is the gap between closure and care.

The answer depends on what you think a person is. If a person is a machine, the practice is repair. If a person is a relation, the practice is restoration. If a person is a whole that cannot be taken apart, the question itself is the mistake.
