## Draft

An evidence page is a tool. The tool shows how to read the evidence. It does not show what the record does not say.

An evidence page uses five labels to tell you how strong a claim is. The labels are: documented, strongly supported, plausible, disputed, and unknown. Documented means a primary source directly supports the claim. Strongly supported means multiple strong sources agree. Plausible means the evidence allows the interpretation but does not prove it. Disputed means credible experts disagree. Unknown means the evidence cannot answer the question.

Start with the Research section. Each fact carries a source and a label. The label tells you how much weight the fact carries. You can open the source and check it.

Here is a concrete example. Take a claim about a prehistoric skull with a healed hole. The claim "this skull has a healed trepanation" is documented: the object is in a museum and you can see it. The claim "the community cared for this person" is not documented. It is plausible at best. A good evidence page keeps those two claims separate. It does not let the second claim ride on the first.

Here is how the labels work in practice. If a page says "the skull has a healed hole," that is documented. If it says "the community cared for the person," that is plausible. If it says "the person consented to the procedure," that is unknown. The page keeps those three claims separate. It does not let the plausible claim ride on the documented one. That is the whole job of the evidence page: to keep the claims at the level the evidence supports. It does not round up. It does not smooth over the gap between what is documented and what is only plausible.

That is how you read an evidence page: check the label on each claim, and notice where the evidence stops.
