## Draft

A time-band page is a window. The window shows what the record says about a specific time. The window does not show what the record does not say.

Start with the Research section. Each fact there carries a source. You can open the source and check it. If the source says "lunatic asylums were first established in Britain in the mid-19th century," then that is what the record says. If the source does not say something, the page should not say it either.

Then read the Draft. The Draft is the window. It shows what the record says about the time, and it names what the record does not say. A good time-band page tells you both. It does not pretend the record is complete.

Here is a concrete example. Take the 19th-century band. The National Archives guide says the County Asylums Act of 1808 encouraged justices of the peace to build county lunatic asylums, but the response was slow. The 1845 Acts made them compulsory and set up the Lunacy Commission. The 1890 Act widened the role so wealthier patients could be admitted. Those are three dated, named facts. A time-band page strings them in order and tells you what each one changed.

Now check the limits. The guide also says most patient files have been destroyed. So the 19th-century band can tell you about the system, the laws, and the institutions. It cannot tell you what a specific person felt in a specific room. That gap is part of the page, not a flaw in it.

The References section at the bottom lists every source the page used. If a fact in the Draft feels shaky, go to the References and find the matching entry. The entry tells you whether the source is a primary record, a scholarly book, or an institutional guide. That is how you judge how much weight the fact carries.

That is how you read a time-band page: follow the dated facts, check each source, and notice where the record goes quiet.
