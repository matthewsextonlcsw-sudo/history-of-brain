> *A place page is a map. The map shows what the record says about a specific place. The map does not show what the record does not say. That is the point of this page: the place page is a map, and the map is limited.*

## Draft

A place page is a map. The map shows what the record says about a specific place. The map does not show what the record does not say.

A place page is bounded by geography. It tells you what people in one region thought, did, and left behind. It does not tell you what people in another region thought. That boundary is the point. A good place page names the region and stays inside it.

Start with the Research section. Each fact carries a source. The source tells you where the fact comes from. If the source is a museum collection, the fact is about an object. If the source is a translated text, the fact is about what a writer said. You can open the source and check it.

Here is a concrete example. Take the Mesopotamia page. It points to a diagnostic tablet. A doctor held the tablet, looked at a patient, and wrote down what he saw. The tablet says "if a man's head is heavy, and he sees dark, and his ears are blocked, then..." That is a medical observation. The page names the object, the place, and what the object says. It does not claim the tablet explains the whole mind of ancient Mesopotamia.

Now check the limits. The tablet is one object. It does not cover every region, every period, or every kind of care. A place page tells you what the record says about that place, and it names what the record does not cover.

One more check: the gaps. A place page should tell you what the record does not cover for that region. If the page is about Mesopotamia, it should say what the Mesopotamian record does not show. It should not pretend the tablet covers every kind of care, every period, or every social class. The gap is part of the map.

That is how you read a place page: stay inside the region, check each source, and notice where the map runs out.
