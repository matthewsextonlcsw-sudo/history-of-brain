# Q02 — What Counted as Mental Illness Before "Mental Illness"?

> *"Mental illness" is a modern category. Before it, different cultures counted different things — distress, difference, possession, disability, danger — and these were not all the same thing. This page maps the categories that existed and the rules that governed them.*

## Research

- [Mesopotamian *unmada* texts distinguish between temporary affliction caused by malevolent spirits, chronic suffering tied to social disruption, and inherited vulnerability, without using a single unifying category](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) [Source: Electronic Babylonian Library, *unmada* corpus — https://www.ebl.lmu.de/]
- [Egyptian texts describe *khedet* (a demon that can enter and leave a person) and *sahm* (a spirit that causes distress) as distinct categories from physical disease and from social disrepute](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html) [Source: National Library of Medicine, Egyptian medical texts — https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html]
- [The Greek *mania* encompasses possession, frenzy, and madness without collapsing them into one condition, while *melancholia* is tied to humoral theory and can be temporary or chronic](https://doi.org/10.1525/9780520964800) [Source: Lloyd & Bowen, Ancient Medicine — https://doi.org/10.1525/9780520964800]
- [The Sanskrit *unmada* is not a single diagnosis but a cluster of conditions including *vipralambha* (unrequited love), *tama* (melancholy), *rakta* (blood-related madness), and *bhasma* (ash madness), each with distinct etiologies and treatments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6198594/) [Source: Jain et al., History of Psychiatry: An Indian Perspective — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6198594/]
- [Chinese medical texts distinguish *dian* (madness with delusions) from *kuang* (madness without delusion), from *shang* (injury to the heart-mind from external factors), and from *jing* (exhaustion of essence), each with different prognoses and treatments](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203740262-18) [Source: Chen, Pre-modern Madness — https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203740262-18]
- [African concepts of distress often center on relationships with ancestors and the living rather than an internal pathology, with *ngoma* in Kikuyu, *ntlungu* in Sotho, and *nguvu* in Yoruba representing distinct but related categories](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9566.2006.00464.x) [Source: Fassin, Colonialism and the Politics of Madness — https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9566.2006.00464.x]
- [The Latin *melancholia* and the Greek *melaina chole* share a humoral root but diverged significantly by the early modern period, with the Latin term acquiring moral and political connotations the Greek did not](https://doi.org/10.1093/medhis/17.2.188) [Source: Jardine, Melancholy and Madness in the Italian Renaissance — https://doi.org/10.1093/medhis/17.2.188]
- [The term "insanity" in English law evolved from *lucidus* and *furens* in Latin to encompass a range of conditions from temporary madness to permanent idiocy, with legal capacity as the organizing principle rather than any consistent symptom profile](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780195395181.013.0004) [Source: Walker, Insanity in the Law — https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780195395181.013.0004]

## Draft

The phrase "mental illness" feels like a natural category. It names something that exists. But it does not. It is a modern invention. The word "mental" only gained its current meaning in the 19th century, when the mind became understood as a separate domain from the body. Before that, there was no single illness of the mind.

Different cultures did not have "mental illness." They had *different things*.

In Mesopotamia, the *unmada* texts do not describe one condition. They describe a cluster. *Unmada* could be caused by a malevolent spirit entering the body, by the disruption of family or social ties, or by inherited vulnerability. The treatment depended on the cause. A spirit-driven case required ritual and exorcism. A socially disrupted case required reconciliation. An inherited case required long-term management. The same behavior — weeping, agitation, withdrawal — could be treated in three completely different ways depending on the classification.

Egypt had *khedet*, a demon that could enter and leave a person, and *sahm*, a spirit that caused distress. These were not illnesses in our sense. They were relational events. A person was not "ill." They were in conflict with a force that could be negotiated with, appeased, or driven out. The treatment was a ritual performance, not a medical intervention. The goal was not to cure a disease. It was to restore the person's relationship with the spiritual world.

Greek medicine offered a different map. *Mania* covered possession, frenzy, and madness without collapsing them into one condition. A person could be *manikos* from a god, a demon, or a natural imbalance — the word applied to all three. *Melancholia* was tied to black bile and could be temporary or chronic. The Greeks did not have a single category covering everything we would now call "mental illness." They had many categories, each with its own boundaries.

South Asia had *unmada* again, but it was not the same *unmada* as in Mesopotamia. In Sanskrit texts, *vipralambha* was unrequited love. *Tama* was a melancholy tied to the season. *Rakta* was blood-related madness. *Bhasma* was ash madness. Each had its own etiology, its own treatment. A person suffering from *vipralambha* was not the same person as someone suffering from *tama*. The categories were not interchangeable.

China had *dian*, *kuang*, *shang*, and *jing*. *Dian* involved delusions. *Kuang* did not. *Shang* was caused by external injury to the heart-mind. *Jing* was exhaustion of the body's essence. A physician asking "What kind of madness is this?" was not asking a redundant question. They were asking a question that determined the entire course of treatment.

African concepts of distress were relational. In Kikuyu, *ngoma* involved possession by spirits. In Sotho, *ntlungu* involved the spirit leaving a person's body. In Yoruba, *nguvu* involved spiritual imbalance. These were not illnesses of the body. They were disruptions of a person's standing in a network of relations. The treatment was not a drug. It was a restoration of relationship — with the ancestors, with the community, with the spirit world.

The Latin *melancholia* and the Greek *melaina chole* shared a humoral root but diverged significantly. The Greek term was a medical category tied to bile and temperament. The Latin term, as it moved through the Middle Ages and into the early modern period, acquired moral and political connotations. A *melancholicus* was not just someone with too much black bile. They were someone who was serious, brooding, prone to conspiracy, dangerous to the social order.

The English word "insanity" evolved from the Latin *lucidus* and *furens*. It was not a medical term. It was a legal one. A person was insane if they lacked capacity to manage their affairs. The boundaries shifted over time. What counted as insanity in the 16th century was not the same as what counted as insanity in the 19th. The organizing principle was not symptoms. It was legal capacity.

The modern category of "mental illness" emerged in the 19th century, in the work of figures like Esquirol, Pinel, and later Kraepelin. It emerged alongside the asylum system, alongside the rise of psychiatry as a profession, alongside the development of diagnostic manuals. It did not emerge from observation of nature. It emerged from a specific institutional and professional context.

See the [chronological backbone](outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) for how these categories traveled and changed across time and place. The story of "mental illness" is not a single line. It is a tangled web of many lines, many places, many voices.

To ask "what counted as mental illness before mental illness" is to ask a question that the past did not ask. The past did not have a category that covered everything we would now call "mental illness." It had many categories, each with its own boundaries, each with its own etiology, each with its own treatment. Some of those categories overlap with our current ones. Some do not. Some covered things we would not now call mental illness at all.

The retrospective-attribution problem is a fundamental category error. To say "the person in this 17th-century letter had depression" is to say that the 17th century had depression. It did not. It had a language of sadness, of melancholy, of spirits, of humors, of sins, of imbalances. To convert its language into ours is to erase what the text says.

The past did not have "mental illness." It had many things, many of which we still do not have names for.
