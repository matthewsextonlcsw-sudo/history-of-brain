## Draft

This is not one thing. Not one person. Not one time. Not one place. Not one record. It is a set of variations, each with its own soul, its own promise, its own violence. The question "What was the soul for?" looks simple. It is not.

In Mesopotamia, the [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) records diagnostic tablets that locate thought, emotion, and decision-making in the heart. The soul was the heart. The promise was restoration. The violence was the ritual itself — the binding, the confinement, the public performance.

In Egypt, the [UCL Letters to the Dead](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/lettersdead.html) show family petitions and heart-centered vocabularies. The soul was the person. The promise was the person's standing in the community. The violence was the family's authority — the commitment, the discharge, the retrieval.

In the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, the [backbone's B2.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the brain, soul, regimen, and sanctuary. The [Gill account](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007123400001702) shows *psyche* as breath, life, soul, and mind. The soul was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

In the Hebrew Bible, the [Olyan account](https://doi.org/10.1177/000712340106400302) shows *nephesh* as a living being rather than a separate mental substance. The soul was the person. The promise was the person's standing in the community. The violence was the community's authority — the commitment, the discharge, the retrieval.

In the Islamic world, the [backbone's B3.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) maps the sciences of the soul in Arabic and Persian: perception, imagination, memory, sleep, emotion, body, ethics, and revelation. The soul was not one thing. It was a set of faculties, each with its own function, its own promise, its own violence.

In the early modern period, the [backbone's B4.5 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) tracks *acedia* and *melancholy* through semantic change without turning either into a timeless depression label. The soul was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

In the present, the [backbone's B10.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows neurodiversity, mad pride, recovery, and disability justice: separate and overlapping movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects. The soul was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

The answer depends on what you think a person is. If a person is a machine, the soul is a part. If a person is a relation, the soul is a network. If a person is a whole that cannot be taken apart, the question itself is the mistake.
