## Draft

This is not one thing. Not one person. Not one time. Not one place. Not one record. It is a set of variations, each with its own mind, its own promise, its own violence. The question "What was the mind for?" looks simple. It is not.

In Mesopotamia, the [Electronic Babylonian Library](https://www.ebl.lmu.de/) records diagnostic tablets that locate thought, emotion, and decision-making in the heart. The mind was the heart. The promise was restoration. The violence was the ritual itself — the binding, the confinement, the public performance.

In the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, the [backbone's B2.1 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows the brain, soul, regimen, and sanctuary. The mind was the brain. The promise was cure. The violence was the regimen — the diet, the exercise, the confinement.

In South Asia, the [backbone's B2.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows layered selves, *manas*, suffering, and transformation. The [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/) shows *manas* as a mediating organ between the self and the senses. The mind was not a location. It was a faculty. The promise was transformation. The violence was the transformation itself — the ritual, the confinement, the public performance.

In China, the [backbone's B2.4 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows *xin* as heart-mind, emotion, conduct, and order. The [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-mind/) shows *xin* as a single embodied locus where thought, emotion, and moral character intertwine. The mind was not a location. It was a whole. The promise was order. The violence was the order itself — the confinement, the public performance, the family's authority.

In the Islamic world, the [backbone's B3.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) maps the sciences of the soul in Arabic and Persian: perception, imagination, memory, sleep, emotion, body, ethics, and revelation. The mind was not one thing. It was a set of faculties, each with its own function, its own promise, its own violence.

In the early modern period, the [backbone's B4.6 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) is blunt: Vesalius, Burton, and Descartes are changes within European debates, not the moment humanity discovered the brain. The mind was the machine. The promise was control. The violence was the machine itself — the anatomy, the regimen, the confinement.

In the present, the [backbone's B10.3 node](https://github.com/matthewsexton/history-of-brain/blob/main/history-of-brain/outline/CHRONOLOGICAL-BACKBONE.md) shows neurodiversity, mad pride, recovery, and disability justice: separate and overlapping movements, each with its own arguments, its own internal disagreements, and its own institutional effects. The mind was not one thing. It was a set of variations — the movement's promise, the movement's violence.

The answer depends on what you think a person is. If a person is a machine, the mind is a part. If a person is a relation, the mind is a network. If a person is a whole that cannot be taken apart, the question itself is the mistake.
