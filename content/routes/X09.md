# X09 — The Naval Route

> *The naval route is the story of how the mind became a naval problem. The Royal Navy had its own asylums for sailors with mental illness. The records are in the National Archives.*

## Draft

The Royal Navy had its own problem with mental illness. Sailors broke. They broke from the sea. They broke from the discipline. They broke from the isolation. The Navy had to deal with them.

The National Archives holds the records. ADM 102/415-420 covers Hoxton House, 1755 to 1818. ADM 102/356-373 covers Royal Hospital Haslar, 1818 to 1854. The Royal Naval Hospital in Yarmouth was also a major hospital for naval lunatics. ADM 105/28 holds reports on the treatment of naval lunatics from 1812 to 1832.

The naval route is a small part of the bigger story. But it is a real part. The Navy was a separate system. It had its own hospitals. It had its own records. It had its own rules. The sailor was not a civilian patient. The sailor was a naval patient.

The records survive. The musters survive. The treatment reports survive. The individual sailor's story mostly does not. But the system is documented. You can search the catalogue. You can find the records. You can see what the Navy did.

Here is one more detail. The Royal Naval Hospital in Yarmouth was a major hospital for naval lunatics. It was not an asylum. It was a hospital. The Navy used the hospital to treat its own people. The hospital records are in the National Archives. They are the mechanism of the naval route.

The musters of sea-going hospital ships can be found with the other musters in ADM 36 and ADM 37. The musters are the Navy's version of the admission register. They give you the name, the rank, the ship, and the date. They do not give you what the sailor felt. The sailor's story is in the gap.

The naval route is not a story of progress. It is a story of a separate system doing its own thing. The Navy dealt with its own people. The records show it. The gaps show what the Navy did not want to record.
