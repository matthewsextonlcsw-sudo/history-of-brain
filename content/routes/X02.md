# X02 — The Medical Route

> *The medical route is the story of how the mind became a medical problem. Before the 19th century, mental illness was a legal problem, a religious problem, or a family problem. The 19th century made it a medical one. The records are in the National Archives and the Wellcome Collection.*

## Draft

Before the 19th century, mental illness was not a medical problem. It was a legal problem. The Crown took custody of "idiots" and "lunatics" who owned property. The Chancery court determined whether a person was of sound mind. If they were not, their property was handed to "committees" — usually family members. The person was not a patient. They were a legal case.

The 1845 Acts changed this. The Lunacy Commission was established to regulate asylums. For the first time, the state required that people with mental illness be cared for in medical institutions, not prisons or workhouses. The asylum became a hospital. The patient became a case file.

The Wellcome Collection holds medical texts from this period. They show the shift from legal language to medical language. The word "lunatic" gave way to "patient." The word "custody" gave way to "treatment." The shift was not instant. It was not complete. But it was real.

The 1890 Lunacy Act widened the role again. Wealthier patients began to be admitted. The asylum was no longer just for the poor. It became a place for anyone whose mind broke, regardless of class.

The records that survive are administrative. Admission registers. Correspondence with poor law unions. Quarterly returns. The individual patient's story is mostly gone. Most patient files have been destroyed. What survives is the system, not the person.

The medical route is the story of how the mind became a body problem. The body could be examined. The body could be treated. The mind could not. That gap — between what the doctor could see and what the patient felt — is the central tension of the entire 19th century.
