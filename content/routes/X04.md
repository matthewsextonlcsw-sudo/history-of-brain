# X04 — The Legal Route

> *The legal route is the story of how the mind became a legal problem. Before the 19th century, the Crown took custody of people deemed "of unsound mind." The Chancery court determined whether a person was a lunatic. The records are in the National Archives.*

## Draft

Before the 19th century, the mind was a legal problem, not a medical one. If you owned property and your mind broke, the Crown took custody of you and your estate. The Chancery court determined whether you were of sound mind. If you were not, your property was handed to "committees" — usually family members. You were not a patient. You were a legal case.

The National Archives guide is clear on this: "Until the 19th century the custody of 'idiots' and 'lunatics', as well as the ownership of their land and property, fell to the Crown." The king was entitled to administer the lands of an idiot until they died. For a lunatic, the Crown only took control during periods of insanity.

The process was formal. Petitioners had to provide at least two sworn affidavits supporting their claim that the person was of unsound mind. The Lord Chancellor ordered commissioners to hold an inquisition. If the person was deemed not of sound mind, the Chancellor committed their custody and estate to "committees."

Here is the key distinction: the Chancery process had nothing to do with committal to an asylum. The asylum was a medical procedure. The Chancery was a legal one. You could be a Chancery lunatic without being in an asylum. You could be in an asylum without being a Chancery lunatic. The two systems ran in parallel.

The records survive in the National Archives. Series C 211 holds the abstracts of petitions from 1627 to 1932. They are name-searchable from 1853 onwards. The affidavits themselves mostly do not survive. What survives is the legal process, not the person.
