# X07 — The Family Route

> *The family route is the story of how the mind became a family problem. Before the 19th century, wealthy families made private arrangements for their members with mental illness. The records are in local archives and in the National Archives.*

## Draft

Before the 19th century, if you were wealthy and your mind broke, your family made private arrangements. They sent you to a "madhouse" — a private house licensed by the justices of the peace. The family paid. The family visited. The family decided when you came home.

The National Archives guide is clear: "Wealthier people had to make private arrangements for the care of family members with a mental illness." The madhouses were examined by government commissioners. They were not hospitals. They were private businesses. The keeper made money. The family paid. The patient was a customer.

The records survive in local archives and in the National Archives. MH 51/735 is a register of admissions to private asylums outside London, from 1798 to 1812. It includes the names of 1,788 patients. It is indexed by both the names of inmates and the keepers of licensed houses. You can search it. You can find your ancestor.

The family route is not a story of neglect. It is a story of responsibility. The family took responsibility. The family paid. The family decided. The state did not get involved until the 1845 Acts. Before that, the family was the system.

The Wellcome Collection holds letters and documents from this period. They show the family's perspective. They show the patient's perspective, when the patient could write. The gaps in the record show what the family did not want to say.

Here is one more detail. MH 51/745 is a list of patients' names, ages, illnesses, and causes of death from Haydock Lodge, a private asylum. It is a rare survival: a private asylum's patient list, with the cause of death recorded. Most private asylum records did not survive. The ones that did give you the name, the age, the illness, and the outcome. They do not give you the family's motive or the patient's feeling.
