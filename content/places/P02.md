# P02 — Egypt

> *Egypt gave the world two kinds of medical writing. One kind was surgical. The other was spiritual. The surgical kind is the Edwin Smith Papyrus. The spiritual kind is the Letters to the Dead. Both are in museums today. Both can be read. Both are different from each other, and that difference is the point.*

## Research

- [The Edwin Smith Papyrus is a surgical treatise from around 1300 BCE, copied from an older text. It describes 48 cases of head, neck, and spine injuries with observations and treatments. The National Library of Medicine holds a digital copy.](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html) [Source: NLM Technical Bulletin — https://www.nlm.nih.gov/pubs/techbull/ma10/ma10_hmd_reprint_papyrus.html]
- [The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) is a pharmacological text containing over 700 remedies. It covers treatments for eye, ear, skin, and digestive conditions. The Wellcome Collection holds a copy.](https://wellcomecollection.org/) [Source: Wellcome Collection — https://wellcomecollection.org/]
- [The Letters to the Dead are a collection of petitions written to deceased family members, asking for protection or help. They are not medical texts, but they show how Egyptians understood the relationship between the living and the dead, and how grief was processed.](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/lettersdead.html) [Source: UCL Egypt Exploration Society — https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/lettersdead.html]
- [The British Museum holds Egyptian medical papyri, including fragments of the Kahun Papyrus (c. 1800 BCE), which contains gynecological and obstetric treatments.](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection) [Source: British Museum — https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection]

## Draft

The Edwin Smith Papyrus is a surgical manual. A doctor wrote down 48 cases of head and spine injuries. For each case, he wrote what he saw, what he thought it meant, and what he did. Some cases he treated. Some he did not. He wrote "an unsavable ailment" for the ones he could not fix. That is honest medicine. It is not a guess. It is a record.

The Ebers Papyrus is a pharmacy. Over 700 remedies, written down. Eye drops. Skin salves. Digestive tonics. The doctor did not explain why the remedy worked. He just said what to use and when. That is a different kind of knowledge. It is practical, not theoretical.

The Letters to the Dead are something else entirely. A person wrote a letter to a dead family member. "You are my mother. You are in the afterlife. Please protect me from my enemies." That is not medicine. That is grief. But it tells you how Egyptians understood the mind: the dead were still present, and the living could talk to them.

Three documents. Three different ways of thinking about the body and the mind. All in museums. All readable. You can check them.
