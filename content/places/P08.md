## Draft

Medieval Europe is where the mind became a religious and legal problem. The Church dealt with mental illness as a spiritual problem. The law dealt with it as a property problem. The two systems ran in parallel. They did not always agree.

The Church described mental illness in terms of the soul. The soul was in distress. The treatment was prayer. The treatment was ritual. The treatment was exorcism. The mind was a spiritual entity.

The law described mental illness in terms of property. If you were "insane," your property was managed by a guardian. The legal system did not care about your mind. It cared about your estate.

The Wellcome Collection holds materials from both traditions. The NLM holds digitized editions with English translations. You can read the texts. You can see what the Church thought. You can see what the law thought. You can see where they disagreed.

The British Museum holds manuscripts and documents from this period. They show the religious practice. They show the legal practice. They show the gap between the two.

The Church is the religious system. The law is the legal system. The two systems ran in parallel. They did not always agree. The Church treated the soul. The law managed the estate. The patient received both. The record shows both. The gap shows what each system did not want to say.

The Church described mental illness in terms of the soul. The soul was in distress. The treatment was prayer, ritual, and exorcism. The law described mental illness in terms of property. If you were "insane," your property was managed by a guardian. The two systems ran in parallel. The patient received both.

The medieval contribution is not a story of progress. It is a story of two systems running in parallel. The religious system. The legal system. The records show both. The gaps show what each system did not want to say.
